Paying your way on a rental scooter in Hanoi is simpler than it first looks, but it works differently from home. The short answer: carry Vietnamese dong in small notes for petrol stations and parking attendants, treat QR payment as a useful second option rather than a guarantee, and do not budget for road tolls at all, because motorbikes are exempt at toll stations on national road projects. Cards work at many large petrol stations but not at most street-side parking spots. This guide focuses on the mechanics of paying: what to carry, which apps foreign visitors can actually use as of October 2026, what the pump attendant will hand you, how parking claim tickets connect to payment, and the few places where a rider might still meet a fee on the road.

## The short version for your wallet

Before you ride away from the rental shop, set up three layers of payment and you will rarely be stuck.

- **Cash in small denominations.** A small bundle of low-value notes covers almost every fuel stop and parking spot a scooter rider meets in a day. Keep it in a front pocket or a zipped pouch, separate from your main wallet.
- **A QR option.** If your home bank or e-wallet is linked to Vietnam's cross-border QR network, you can scan many merchant codes directly. If not, a Vietnamese bank account is the alternative, which most short-stay visitors do not have.
- **A bank card.** Useful at chain petrol stations, supermarkets and larger car parks in malls or hotels, much less so on the street.

The reason for this layering is that payment acceptance in Hanoi is uneven. A brand-name fuel station on a main road may take everything; a family-run parking spot in an Old Quarter alley may take only notes. Planning for the weakest link is what keeps a ride smooth.

## Why small notes still matter in 2026

Vietnam has moved quickly towards cashless payment, and Hanoi residents scan QR codes for coffee, street food and parking every day. For a visitor, though, cash remains the one method that works everywhere. Many QR codes on the street are domestic bank-transfer codes that need a Vietnamese banking app, and an attendant at a busy parking spot may not wait while you troubleshoot a foreign app.

The practical problem is change. Cash machines usually dispense large notes, and a parking attendant or small fuel kiosk may not be able to break them, especially early in the morning. Break large notes when you buy something bigger, such as a meal, a supermarket shop or a full tank at a large station, and keep a stock of small ones topped up. Polymer notes in similar colours are easy to confuse in poor light, so glance at the number before handing one over, and keep notes flat so you are not unfolding a wad at the pump while traffic waits behind you.

Do not offer foreign currency. Prices are in dong, attendants are not set up to convert, and it slows everyone down.

## QR payments a foreign visitor can actually use

The biggest change for visitors in 2026 is the spread of cross-border QR payment. Vietnam's national payment switch, NAPAS, and the payment company VNPAY have linked their merchant codes with apps from several countries, so a tourist can scan a Vietnamese QR code with the payment app they already use at home and pay in their own currency.

According to reporting in August 2026, visitors from China can pay through Alipay, WeChat Pay (Weixin Pay) or UnionPay International; from Thailand through apps on the ITMX network; from Singapore through apps such as LiquidPay; and visitors from South Korea, Laos and Cambodia through linked networks of their own. Vietnam's central bank announced the Vietnam–Singapore cross-border QR service on its own website. Acceptance has spread beyond hotels and shopping centres into markets, restaurants and small stalls.

What this means on a scooter trip:

- If your country is on the list, open your home app before you travel, confirm cross-border QR is enabled and check any foreign-exchange fee your provider charges.
- At a petrol station or parking spot, look for a printed QR code with the VietQR or VNPAY-QR mark. Scan it, enter the amount the attendant shows you if the code is static, and show the confirmation screen.
- If your country is not on the list, a European, American or Australian banking app generally cannot pay a Vietnamese QR code directly. Rely on cash and cards instead.

Mobile data matters too. A payment that hangs on a weak signal in a basement car park is frustrating for everyone, so have cash ready as a fallback.

## Paying at the petrol pump

Our separate guide to [fuel in Hanoi](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/fuel-in-hanoi-petrol-stations-rental-scooter/) explains which petrol to buy, how pump prices are set and how far a tank goes. Here is the payment side of a fuel stop.

In Hanoi you almost never pump your own fuel. Ride up to the pump on the lane for motorbikes, switch off the engine, step off and open the fuel cap, which on most automatic scooters sits under the seat or behind a flap at the front. Tell the attendant either an amount of money or "full". Saying an amount is the easiest way to control spending: the attendant sets the pump, and the display shows both the litres and the total.

Then pay:

1. **Cash** goes straight into the attendant's hand. Wait for your change before you ride off, and check it against the pump display.
2. **QR** is usually a printed code at the island or a code on a small screen. Scan, pay the displayed total and show the success screen.
3. **Cards** work at many larger stations operated by the big chains. Hand the card over; the attendant brings a terminal to you. Smaller independent stations may not have one.

Keep the payment brisk. Riders queue behind you at busy times, so have your money ready before the nozzle goes in.

## The electronic invoice you are offered

Since 1 June 2025, under Decree 70/2025/ND-CP, petrol retailers in Vietnam must issue an electronic invoice for every individual sale, including sales to private customers. Petrolimex said its network of around 2,800 stations moved to this system on that date. A later decree on invoices, Decree 254/2026/ND-CP, reportedly in force from 1 July 2026, keeps the rule that a retail fuel invoice is created at the end of each sale.

For a visitor this has two practical effects. First, some stations print a slip or show a code you can scan to look up the invoice online; you can usually ignore it if you do not need a receipt. Second, it is a useful check on honesty: the amount on the pump display and the invoice should match what you paid. If you are travelling for work and need a company invoice, ask before the sale so the attendant can enter your company details. You do not need to give personal information for an ordinary cash purchase.

## Paying for parking: tickets, codes and attendants

Our guide to [parking a motorbike in Hanoi](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/parking-a-motorbike-in-hanoi/) covers where you may and may not leave a bike, towing and theft. The payment routine is a separate skill.

At a staffed parking spot, the attendant usually gives you a claim ticket or chalks a number on the seat, and you pay either when you arrive or when you collect the bike. Ask which, with a simple gesture at the ticket and your money. Keep the ticket somewhere safe: it is your proof that the bike is yours, and losing it can mean an awkward wait while the attendant checks your documents.

Hanoi has been running a cashless parking programme for several years. In the pilot schemes, staffed motorbike points issue a dynamic QR code when you park, which you can pay through various apps, and an attendant scans it with a handheld device when you leave. In one reported phase, more than nine in ten motorbike payments at the pilot points were made by QR. Some car parks in malls and office buildings use cards or automatic barriers with license-plate cameras. For a visitor, however, the dynamic QR at a street point is usually a domestic code, so cash is still the reliable option.

Parking charges are not uniform across the city and can differ by location, time of day, overnight stays and events, so we do not quote a figure here. Look for a posted price board, ask before you leave the bike if none is visible, and be wary of anyone who asks for money but cannot show a ticket or a uniform. Our page on [where to park near Hoan Kiem Lake](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/where-to-park-a-scooter-near-hoan-kiem-lake/) shows how this works in the busiest area of the city.

## Do motorbikes pay road tolls in Vietnam?

At ordinary toll stations, no. Toll stations on national road projects, often called BOT stations after the build-operate-transfer model that funds them, charge cars, buses and trucks. Circular 32/2024/TT-BGTVT, effective from 1 January 2025, lists motorcycles, motor scooters and non-motorised vehicles among the groups exempt from the road-service charge at these stations. In practice, motorbike riders use a separate lane or a gap beside the barriers and pass without stopping.

Most toll lanes in Vietnam are now electronic for cars, using a sticker tag linked to an account. None of that applies to your rental scooter, so you do not need a tag, a wallet top-up or a toll app.

The more important rule is where you cannot ride at all. The Law on Road Traffic Order and Safety, in force since 1 January 2025, keeps motorbikes off expressways. Expressways are where most of the newest automated toll plazas sit, so if you ever find yourself queuing at a car toll gate with a ticket machine, you are probably on the wrong road. Our guide on [checking whether a navigation app suggests an expressway](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/how-to-check-if-a-navigation-app-suggests-an-expressway/) explains how to avoid this.

## Ferries, pontoon crossings and other roadside fees

The toll exemption covers road charges, not every fee you might meet on a longer trip. Outside the city, a few river crossings are still made by small vehicle ferries or pontoon bridges, typically on minor routes, and these may charge each motorbike a small crossing fee, usually collected in cash on the spot. Car ferries to islands, such as those in Ha Long Bay or the Gulf of Tonkin, have their own ticket offices and fares that change with the season, so check them on the day.

Other fees you may meet include parking at tourist sites, temples, waterfalls and national parks, often collected by a staffed point at the entrance, and occasionally an entrance ticket that includes a parking element. Treat all of these the same way: small notes ready, a ticket or receipt in exchange, and a question if the amount seems unclear. ## Cards and ATMs: what works and where

International debit and credit cards are accepted at many chain petrol stations, supermarkets, hotels and larger restaurants. They are rarely useful for street parking. If you plan to rely on a card, tell your bank you are travelling and keep a second card in a different place in case one is declined.

ATMs are common in central Hanoi, and many banks charge a withdrawal fee for foreign cards on top of whatever your home bank charges. Withdrawing a larger amount less often usually costs less in fees, but that brings you back to the problem of large notes. Plan to break them at a supermarket or restaurant soon after withdrawing. Use machines inside bank branches or busy buildings rather than isolated kiosks, and do not leave your scooter unattended at the kerb while you use one.

Contactless phone wallets work wherever a card terminal accepts contactless, which in practice means the same larger businesses that take cards.

## Avoiding payment problems and disputes

Most payment problems on a scooter trip come from rushing. A few habits help:

- **Watch the pump reset to zero** before fuelling starts, and read the total yourself before paying.
- **Count your change** at the pump or the parking point, not after you have ridden away.
- **Photograph posted price boards** at parking points if you plan to stay long or overnight.
- **Keep QR confirmation screens** until the attendant has accepted the payment; a screenshot settles most disagreements.
- **Never hand over your phone or wallet** to someone to "help" you pay.

If you think you have been overcharged at a parking point, stay calm, show the posted price or your screenshot, and ask for a ticket. If the person cannot show they operate the location, it is often wiser to move the bike to a clearly organised lot than to argue.

## FAQ: payment questions from visiting riders

### Can I pay for petrol in Hanoi with a foreign card?

Often, yes, at larger stations run by the main fuel chains, where an attendant brings a card terminal to the pump. Smaller independent stations may take only cash or domestic QR, so carry small notes as a backup.

### Can tourists use QR codes to pay in Vietnam?

Visitors from countries linked to Vietnam's cross-border QR networks, including China, Thailand, Singapore, South Korea, Laos and Cambodia, can scan many Vietnamese merchant codes with their home apps. Visitors from most other countries cannot pay these codes directly and should rely on cash and cards.

### Do I need to pay tolls on a motorbike in Vietnam?

Not at toll stations on national road projects. Motorcycles and motor scooters are listed among the exempt groups under Circular 32/2024/TT-BGTVT, effective from 1 January 2025. Motorbikes are also barred from expressways, where many toll plazas sit.

### Should I keep the parking ticket?

Yes. It is your claim to the bike, and losing it can mean showing documents and waiting while the attendant checks. Keep it with your phone, not loose in the scooter's seat compartment.

### Do I need an e-invoice from the petrol station?

Not for an ordinary purchase. Stations must issue an electronic invoice for each sale, but a tourist can ignore it unless a company receipt is needed, in which case ask before the attendant starts fuelling.

### What if I run out of small notes?

Break a large note at a supermarket, a restaurant or a big petrol station before you need to pay for parking. Many attendants cannot change large notes, especially early in the day.
