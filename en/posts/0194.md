Small wheels drop into potholes and catch on edges more readily than large ones, so a 50cc scooter on 10-inch wheels needs earlier scanning, lower speed over rough patches and correct tyre pressure. Honda's specification pages show the difference: its small-wheel Dio and Tact scooters use 10-inch tyres, while the Super Cub 50 runs 17-inch tyres. Scooter-focused writers explain the consequence plainly, saying that smaller wheels do not roll over obstructions as well and may fall into a large hole, and that small wheels transmit bumps more sharply to the rider. The good news is that the technique to manage this is simple, and it costs nothing: look further ahead, slow before the hazard rather than in it, hold the bike straight, relax your arms and keep the tyres inflated as the manufacturer specifies.

## Why wheel size matters

A wheel meets a pothole edge at an angle that depends on the wheel's radius. A large wheel strikes the far edge at a shallow angle and tends to roll up and out. A small wheel strikes it at a steeper angle and is more likely to drop in and stop, throwing the impact into the front forks and the rider's hands. That is a general description of the geometry, and it matches the explanation on scooter-focused sites that small wheels do not roll over obstructions as well and can fall into large holes.

The same sources point out the trade-off. Small wheels give a lighter feel at low speed, which is why they suit crowded city streets, and larger wheels give more stability at speed. In slow Hanoi traffic that trade is usually in the small wheel's favour, as long as you ride with the weakness in mind.

## Reading the road early

On small wheels, time is your friend. The earlier you see a hazard, the more gently you can deal with it. Build these habits:

- **Look 3 to 4 seconds ahead,** not at the wheel in front. Scan the surface for dark patches, broken edges and sunken covers.
- **Read the traffic as a clue.** If bikes ahead swerve or slow at the same spot, there is something there.
- **Treat puddles as unknown depth.** After rain, a puddle can hide a pothole, so slow before you cross it.
- **Be wary at night.** Shadows hide holes, and your headlight shows less than you think. Slow down and ride where the surface looks smoother.
- **Watch edges and joins.** Road patches, utility covers and drain edges can catch a small wheel.

## How to ride over a bad patch

This is general riding guidance, not a Honda procedure, and every situation differs.

1. **Slow down before the patch.** Use both brakes smoothly while the bike is upright and straight.
2. **Release the brakes just before you reach the hazard.** Hard braking into a hole loads the front wheel and the forks, and the impact is worse.
3. **Keep the bike straight.** Avoid swerving at the last moment, since a sudden sideways move on a small wheel can cause a slide or put you in the path of other traffic.
4. **Keep your arms soft.** Stiff arms pass the shock to the handlebars. Bent elbows and a loose grip let the bike move beneath you.
5. **Stay seated and centred,** with your weight balanced, and keep your feet on the floorboard or pegs.
6. **Keep a steady throttle** and do not snap it open or shut.
7. **Check your line afterwards** before you get back to normal speed.

If you cannot avoid a hole and you have not slowed, accept the hit in a straight line. A straight, slow impact is usually easier to control than a late swerve. If you are in doubt about a stretch, the safest choice is often to stop and walk the bike.

## Tyre pressure: your cheapest suspension

Honda's tyre guidance says every motorcycle has a specified air pressure, that low pressure is bad and that excessive pressure is also bad. On rough roads this matters twice. A soft tyre can pinch against the rim when it hits an edge, and an overinflated tyre bounces and loses grip. Honda's UK maintenance page says tyre pressure influences how a bike handles and recommends checking it at least once a week and before long rides.

Do this at the shop:

- **Ask for the correct pressure** for the front and rear on your bike, and note the numbers.
- **Look for a label** on the frame or swing arm, where it exists. Our guide to [reading a tyre size and pressure label on a Honda Wave](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/how-to-read-a-honda-wave-tyre-size-and-pressure-label/) explains how labels are laid out, although your 50cc bike may differ.
- **Use a gauge at a petrol station,** or ask staff to check.
- **Look at the sidewalls** for cracks, bulges and scratches. Honda advises stopping use if there are cracks or scratches in a tyre.

## After a hard hit

A big jolt can damage a small wheel, so check afterwards, particularly if the sound or the feel has changed. Pull over somewhere safe and look for:

- **A pull to one side** or a wobble in the bars.
- **A new rattle,** or a clunk when you steer.
- **A soft tyre,** a bulge or a dent in the rim.
- **A brake lever that feels different.**

## Common situations on city streets

Rough surfaces come in different forms, and each calls for a slightly different response.

### Sunken utility covers and drain edges

These are common at junctions and in lanes. A cover that sits lower than the road can catch a small front wheel at an angle. Cross it straight and slowly, and avoid braking while the wheel is on it.

### Patched and uneven roads

A patch that has settled can leave a step of a few centimetres at the edge. Slow before it, and cross it as close to square as you can, not at a shallow angle that lets the wheel slide along the edge.

### Speed humps

Humps are rarely a danger but can jolt a light bike. Slow almost to walking pace, then roll over with soft arms. Accelerate gently once the rear wheel is over.

### Standing water

After rain a puddle may be deeper than it looks. Slow down, keep the bike upright and avoid braking inside the puddle. If the water looks deep or fast, go around or wait.

### Gravel and loose edges

Loose stones at the side of a road reduce grip, and small wheels can slide more easily. Stay on the firmer part of the surface and keep your speed down.

## Riding with a passenger or luggage

Added weight lowers the clearance and increases the load on the tyres, so the bike will feel less forgiving over bumps. Honda's specification pages list its 50cc examples as one-person machines, and the shop's guidance is the one to follow on carrying a passenger. For luggage, keep the load low and centred, and avoid hanging heavy bags on the handlebars, which makes steering heavier over bad patches. ## Choosing routes that suit small wheels

You do not have to meet every bad road. Where you can, choose:

- **Main streets with fresh surfaces** over short cuts that look rough.
- **Daylight** over late rides for unfamiliar routes.
- **Slower speeds** in alleys, where surfaces vary, and in places where you cannot see the road clearly.

If your plan involves a long trip on poor roads, a 50cc is the wrong tool, and a larger automatic or a manual bike on bigger wheels would be better. ## FAQ: potholes and small wheels

### Are 10-inch wheels unsafe?

No. They are standard on many city scooters and handle slow urban traffic well. They simply need more care on rough surfaces than 17-inch wheels do.

### Should I brake hard when I see a pothole?

Brake smoothly before it, then ease off as you reach it. Heavy braking into the hole puts extra load on the front wheel.

### Can I choose a bike with bigger wheels?

Ask the shop which models it has. A classic Cub has bigger wheels than a small scooter, but its other features differ. Prices are on the [prices page](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/prices/).
