A rental scooter can usually enter the residential alleys of Long Bien, but access depends on three things you decide on the spot: whether the alley is wide enough for you to pass another bike at walking pace, whether any sign, gate or barrier says otherwise, and whether you can stop without blocking a door, a gate or the only lane. If any answer is no, park at the mouth and walk. Alleys are people's front yards as much as roads, so the best rule is to ride as a guest. This guide explains how Hanoi alleys are named and numbered, how to read one before entering, how to ride it, how to leave it safely, and how to park without making enemies of the neighbours.

## What an alley is, and how the address system works

Hanoi people say "ngo" for a lane that branches off a street, and "ngach" for a smaller passage that branches off a ngo. In southern Vietnam the word is "hem". Wikipedia's summary of a 2016 figure says that 88 per cent of Hanoi residents lived in this kind of alley, which is why a first-time visitor sees so many of them.

The numbering system follows rules that help you find a door. Under Circular 08/2024/TT-BXD, in force from 15 October 2024, houses on a street are numbered with odd numbers on the left and even numbers on the right, counting from the start of the street. An alley without its own name takes the number of the street house just before its mouth, and a ngach takes the number of the house in its ngo before the opening. In an alley with a single opening, houses inside are numbered from the mouth inward.

Nguyen Ha's address, Alley 5 Nguyen Van Cu Street, follows this pattern: an alley off Nguyen Van Cu labelled from the street numbering. A tip for any address: read it from the biggest unit down. Find the street, then the alley number, then the sub-alley, then the house. A pin on the map may place you at the wrong mouth, so look at the number plates at the corner before you commit.

## Is a scooter allowed in? How to tell

There is no single list of alleys that admit scooters. Most residential alleys are open to motorbikes because that is how people get home. Look for signs that suggest otherwise:

- a sign showing that motorbikes may not enter;
- a chain, bollard, post or gate across the mouth;
- steps or a stepped pavement at the entrance;
- a courtyard, apartment compound or a market area with its own rules;
- a barrier that is closed at night, which usually means residents manage access.

If the way is open, the next question is whether it is wide enough. Vietnam's Law on Road Traffic Order and Safety (Law 36/2024/QH15, effective 1 January 2025) says in Article 18 that no vehicle may stop or park where the carriageway is wide enough for only one lane of motor vehicles. That matters in a narrow alley: if you stop, nobody can pass. It also lists curves and the tops of slopes where visibility is blocked.

If you are unsure, ask. A neighbour or shopkeeper at the mouth can tell you in a few words whether the alley connects through or ends in a dead end.

## Reading an alley before you enter

Spend ten seconds at the mouth. You can learn a lot before you move:

1. **Straight line of sight.** Can you see to the end, or at least to the first bend? Blind corners are where two bikes meet nose to nose.
2. **Through or dead end?** A dead end means turning round or reversing, which is much harder than it sounds.
3. **People.** Children playing, an older person walking slowly and a group chatting at a doorway all set your speed, not the width.
4. **Parked bikes and tricycles.** A row of parked bikes can halve the usable width.
5. **Surface.** Wet tiles, drain covers, loose paving and ramps need respect.
6. **Overhead.** Cables, awnings and shop signs hang lower than they look.

If you cannot answer the first two quickly, walk the first few metres without the scooter.

## Riding at walking pace

In an alley your speed is not set by a number but by how soon you can stop. A safe scooter alley speed is about the pace at which you can put a foot down and stop within a bike's length. That means slower than most visitors expect.

Keep both fingers on the brakes and your weight centred, and keep your feet ready. Roll steadily rather than accelerating between obstacles. Give a pedestrian the full width if they are walking in the middle, and pass at a distance only after they have seen you.

The horn is a short tap, not a signal for others to move. Article 21 of the traffic law allows the horn to warn of a possible hazard or before overtaking, bars continuous use and bars any use from 22:00 to 05:00 in populated areas and around medical facilities, except for priority vehicles. For night arrivals use the headlight and a slow approach instead.

Do not ride with a passenger who cannot keep their feet clear of walls, and do not carry anything wider than the handlebars. Our guide to [carrying a passenger and luggage](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/scooter-for-two-riders-and-luggage-hanoi/) explains the load limits.

## Entering and leaving the main street

The riskiest few metres of an alley are at its mouth. Traffic on the main street cannot see you until you are almost out, and you cannot see them. Stop before the pavement, look in both directions, then roll across slowly. Under Article 77 of the traffic law the pavement is for pedestrians, so give way to anyone walking across your path and do not use it as a riding lane to bypass a queue.

When joining traffic, merge as a flow rather than waiting for a gap that never comes. Our explanation of [how Hanoi intersections actually flow](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/crossing-hanoi-intersections/) describes the technique. Signal where you can and do not accelerate hard just because the first gap appears.

## Parking and being a good guest

The simplest way to avoid trouble is to park at the mouth of the alley, on the main street in a marked spot, and walk the last section. If your accommodation host offers an indoor bay, ask whether it is for guests and whether a ticket or key is required.

Do not leave the bike:

- in front of a door, gate or garage;
- in the only passable lane of a narrow alley;
- on a corner where someone must swing wide;
- in a shared stairwell or corridor.

Lock the steering. Nguyen Ha does not provide an extra lock, so a guarded place matters. Photograph the position, and keep the key with you. For public parking, our guide to [parking a motorbike in Hanoi](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/parking-a-motorbike-in-hanoi/) covers tickets and guarded lots.

## When you meet someone head-on

Two-way alleys have a built-in problem: the first bike to reach a pinch point decides who backs up. The courteous habit is to stop early, put a foot down and let the other rider choose. If you are the one with the clearer route behind you, reverse by walking the scooter back, not by gunning it in reverse gear on a bike that may not have one. A smile and a hand gesture go further than the horn.

Look at what the other person is carrying. A delivery rider with boxes, a cyclist with a basket or someone pushing a handcart needs more room than a bare scooter, and they know their own alley's tight spots. Follow their line rather than inventing yours.

## Mornings, evenings and deliveries

Residential alleys keep a rhythm. Early in the morning people head to work and school, shops open their shutters and deliveries arrive. In the evening families sit outside, children play and bikes come home to their parking spots. None of these moments is a good time to hurry. If you can choose, ride in an alley between the morning and evening peaks, when there are fewer people and parked bikes in the way.

If a delivery vehicle is unloading, wait. Do not squeeze past a person carrying a load or insist that they move. A short pause costs you a minute and saves an argument.

## Night, rain and navigation apps

Alleys feel very different after dark. Lights are fewer, people may sit outside and animals may cross, so keep your speed low and the headlight on. Our guide to [riding at night in Hanoi](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/riding-at-night-in-hanoi/) lists the visibility problems, and the [rain guide](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/riding-in-hanoi-rain/) covers wet surfaces, which make tiled alleys slippery.

Navigation apps treat every passage as a possible shortcut. They may send a motorbike down a stepped lane, a gated courtyard or a dead end. Choose the main road for the long part of a journey and use the app only for the last few hundred metres. ## A fair test for getting off and walking

Walk when any of these is true:

- you cannot see a clear path through the next five metres;
- you would have to stop where only one lane remains;
- someone is carrying something large or a child is close;
- the surface is wet, steeply sloped or stepped;
- you feel you are in the way.

Walking a scooter a few metres is not defeat. It is the most considerate option and often faster than reversing. For a broader first ride, the [Long Bien and Red River guide](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/long-bien-red-river-first-ride/) suggests roads that are kinder to new riders.

## FAQ: Long Bien's residential alleys

### Can I take a rental scooter into any alley?

Not automatically. Most are open, but read signs and barriers, and do not enter if you cannot pass another bike safely.

### What do the numbers in an alley address mean?

They give the street, then the alley named after the street house just before its mouth, then any sub-alley, then the house. Odd numbers are on the left and even numbers on the right.

### Is it all right to use the horn in an alley?

Use a short tap only to warn of a hazard, never continuously, and not at all between 22:00 and 05:00 in populated areas.

### Where should I park if my guesthouse is down an alley?

Ask the host first. Otherwise park in a marked place on the main street and walk in.

### What if the app sends me down a dead end?

Stop where it is safe, and walk the bike out slowly or turn it with care. Next time use the main road.
