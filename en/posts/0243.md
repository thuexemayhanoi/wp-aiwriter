Compare electric scooters by the energy their batteries store, measured in watt-hours (Wh) or kilowatt-hours (kWh), not by amp-hours or by the headline range alone. Amp-hours (Ah) mean little without the voltage, because watt-hours are volts multiplied by amp-hours, and a larger watt-hour figure means more stored energy. Even then, battery size is only one input: weight, motor, speed limit, test conditions and the age of the battery decide how far a scooter really goes. This guide shows the conversion, works through real VinFast figures, explains charging time and battery chemistry in plain language and ends with a short list of questions to ask the shop before choosing a rental.

## The three numbers on a battery label

Battery specifications usually show some mix of volts, amp-hours and watt-hours.

- **Volts (V)** describe the electrical system, and higher voltage generally lets a motor deliver more power.
- **Amp-hours (Ah)** describe how much charge the pack holds at that voltage.
- **Watt-hours (Wh)** describe the total energy stored, and are the best single figure for comparing capacity.

The formula is simple: Wh = V × Ah. A 36 V pack of 15 Ah holds 540 Wh, and a 48 V pack of 12 Ah holds 576 Wh. Judged by amp-hours alone, the first looks larger, but the second stores slightly more energy. Technology writers make the same point: amp-hours are only useful for comparing batteries of the same voltage.

Kilowatt-hours are simply watt-hours divided by 1,000. A 3.5 kWh battery is a 3,500 Wh battery. Many Vietnamese electric motorbike listings give kWh directly, which saves the arithmetic.

If the shop names a battery size but no range, you can estimate the claimed figure yourself: divide the watt-hours by about 17.5. A 2,400 Wh pack suggests a claimed range near 137 km, close to the 134 km published for the Feliz Lite.

## Why the biggest battery is not automatically the best

A bigger battery adds range, but it also adds weight, charging time and cost. Several other things change the result.

- **Speed limit.** The Lite versions are capped at around 48 km/h so that riders who do not hold a motorcycle licence can use them, and the test range does not change that. A 70 km/h bike has a stronger motor and may use more energy at higher speeds.
- **Weight and load.** The test figure assumes a 65 kg rider. Two people or a heavy bag cut the range. See our guide on [electric scooter range with a passenger and luggage](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/electric-scooter-range-with-a-passenger-and-luggage/) for the sums.
- **Riding conditions.** Independent testers report that real-world results are often 50 to 70 per cent of claims for electric kick scooters, and mopeds in Hanoi traffic also fall short of lab figures.
- **Battery age.** A pack loses capacity with use. VinFast's brochure for the Evo200 describes a replacement policy when the capacity falls below 70 per cent, which shows the maker expects fading over time.
- **How much of the pack is usable.** Some riders and makers recommend living in the middle of the charge range for battery health. VinFast's guidance on older e-scooters says life is better when used between 20 and 80 per cent.

For a rental, the vehicle's present condition and the shop's handover record often matter more than the nameplate figure.

## Fixed, removable and dual batteries

Not all scooters keep the battery in the same way.

- **Fixed battery.** The Evo200 is described as having an integrated 3.5 kWh battery. You charge the whole bike at a socket.
- **Removable battery.** Some models let you take the pack off and carry it to a socket. That helps when parking is far from a plug.
- **Dual-battery tray.** The Evo Grand and the Feliz Lite accept a second 2.4 kWh pack, which takes the claimed range from 134 km to 262 km. The second battery is bought separately in the retail market, so a rental will not necessarily have it.

When a shop mentions two batteries, ask whether you get both, who carries them and how the charging works. Our guide on [charging an electric scooter in Hanoi](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/charging-an-electric-scooter-in-hanoi/) covers the practical side.

## Battery size and charging time

Charging time is roughly the battery size divided by the charger's power, plus some extra at the end of the charge. The published figures bear this out. A 2.4 kWh pack on a 400 W charger works out at about six hours, and the published time is 6 hours 30 minutes. A 2.0 kWh pack on the same charger is about five hours, and the published time is 5 hours 20 minutes.

For a 3.5 kWh battery the figure comes out near nine hours on a 400 W charger, and a published charging time of about 6 hours for the Feliz S implies a more powerful charger. Do not assume your charger matches; ask.

Charging also wastes some energy as heat. A commercial source for the Indian market puts charging losses at about 10 to 15 per cent, so the electricity drawn from the wall is somewhat more than the pack's rating. This is relevant for hotels that ask about electricity, and the shop confirms the charging arrangement at handover.

## What the battery chemistry tells you

VinFast's electric motorbikes use LFP, lithium iron phosphate, cells. Technical guides say LFP trades a little energy density for longer cycle life and better thermal stability than the NMC chemistry common in some other scooters. VinFast's Evo200 brochure describes its LFP battery as extending life up to 2,000 charges.

For a renter, chemistry matters less than it might for an owner. You will not charge the bike a thousand times. What matters is that the pack has been looked after, behaves normally on a charge and meets the range you need for your plan.

## A practical comparison method

Use this order when you compare two electric scooters or two rentals.

1. **Convert to watt-hours.** Multiply volts by amp-hours, or take the kWh figure times 1,000.
2. **Divide by about 17.5 for a claimed range.** Check the result against any published range to see if it makes sense.
3. **Cut the claimed range.** Plan on half to two thirds in city traffic, and less with two riders.
4. **Check the load limit.** Dealer listings for several VinFast models give 130 kg.
5. **Check the top speed class.** A lower cap can make a bike simpler to use, but it changes where you can comfortably ride.
6. **Ask about condition.** How old is the battery, and how does it behave after a day of use?
7. **Match to your plan.** Compare the cut range with the kilometres you actually intend to ride, plus a reserve.

Short example: a 2.0 kWh scooter with a 117 km claim, cut to about 70 km for city riding, easily covers a 30 km day with plenty left over. A 1.2 kWh scooter claiming 70 km, cut to about 40 km, still handles a 15 km day but leaves little spare for a detour.

## Questions to ask the shop

- What is the battery capacity in kWh or in volts and amp-hours?
- Is the battery fixed or removable, and is there a second one?
- What does the display show as the range at the current charge?
- What is the battery percentage now, and what percentage do you expect on return? The shop confirms battery level and charging at handover.
- Which model is this, and what is its top speed and load limit?

## FAQ: comparing batteries

### Is a higher amp-hour number always a bigger battery?

No. Amp-hours only compare fairly at the same voltage. Multiply by the voltage to get watt-hours.

### How do I convert kWh to Wh?

Multiply by 1,000. A 2.4 kWh battery is 2,400 Wh.

### Does a bigger battery always mean more range?

Usually more range under the same conditions, but weight, speed and riding style change the result.

### How long does a scooter battery take to charge?

Roughly the battery size divided by the charger's power, plus extra at the end. Published times for VinFast models with 2.0 and 2.4 kWh batteries are 5 hours 20 minutes and 6 hours 30 minutes on a 400 W charger.

### Why is my real range lower than the claim?

The claim is measured at a steady 30 km/h with a 65 kg rider. Stops, load, heat, hills and an ageing battery all reduce it.
