On a small manual motorbike such as a Honda Wave or Yamaha Sirius, use both brakes together and let the front do the larger share. The front lever is worked by your right hand and the rear pedal by your right foot, and Honda's safe-riding training document teaches riders to close the throttle, apply both brakes with the front stronger than the rear, keep the steering straight and then stop. Using only the rear brake can lock the rear wheel, and using only the front brake abruptly can lift the rear wheel. This guide explains why the two brakes behave differently, what each is best at, how road surface, hills and a passenger change the balance, how a drum or disc front brake changes the feel and how to practise safely in a quiet place. It is general information, not riding tuition.

## Which hand and which foot

The controls are simple, and it is worth fixing them in your mind before you ride.

The **front brake lever** is on the right handlebar, in front of the throttle grip. Your right hand therefore does two jobs: it holds the throttle and it works the front brake. The **rear brake pedal** is under your right foot. The **gear lever** is under your left foot. A small underbone bike like the Wave has no clutch lever, so the left hand is free for the left grip, the indicators and the horn.

The first consequence is practical. Because the same hand holds the throttle and the front lever, you must close the throttle before you squeeze. Honda's braking sequence starts with exactly that: release the throttle fully, then brake. A rider who squeezes the lever while still holding the throttle open is fighting the bike.

The second consequence concerns the foot. The right foot must find the rear pedal without looking, because your eyes should be on the road. Before your first ride, sit on the bike on its stand and find the pedal with the ball of your foot a few times. Our guide to [Yamaha Sirius controls for first-time renters](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/yamaha-sirius-controls-explained-for-first-time-renters/) explains how to tell the gear lever and the rear brake apart.

## Why the front brake does most of the work

When a bike slows, its weight does not stay evenly spread. It shifts forward, pressing the front tyre harder on the road and lightening the rear tyre. This is basic physics, and it is the reason behind Honda's instruction. The figure is not one Honda gives, and the exact balance depends on the bike, the load and the road, but the direction is always the same.

Because the front tyre is pressed down harder under braking, it can grip harder and take more braking force before it slides. The rear tyre is being unloaded at the very moment you ask it to stop the bike, so it reaches the limit of its grip sooner and locks more easily. That is why Honda's document warns that using only the rear brake locks the rear wheel, and teaches the front to be stronger than the rear.

The other side of the same physics explains the second warning. If you grab the front brake abruptly, the weight transfer is sudden and large, and the rear of a light bike can rise. Honda's document says that using the front brake alone, abruptly, can lift the rear wheel. The cure is not to avoid the front brake. It is to apply it progressively, so that the weight transfers smoothly and the tyre loads up before the force peaks.

## What the rear brake is good for

The rear brake is not useless. It has real jobs:

- **Balance and stability.** Used together with the front, it keeps the bike settled and helps it slow in a straight line.
- **Very slow speeds.** At walking pace in a queue, a light touch of the rear pedal is a smooth way to control speed, because it is gentle and does not pitch the bike forward. Our guide to [handling stop-and-go traffic on a semi-automatic bike](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/how-to-handle-stop-and-go-traffic-on-a-semi-automatic-bike/) describes how.
- **Holding the bike at rest.** Honda's training document says that once the bike has stopped, you keep the right foot on the brake and put the left foot on the ground. The rear pedal is what holds you while you wait.
- **Backing up the front** when you need to slow in a hurry.

Used alone at speed, though, it is the brake most likely to lock, and a locked rear wheel on a light bike can swing the back end sideways.

| | Front brake | Rear brake |
|---|---|---|
| Control | Right-hand lever | Right-foot pedal |
| Share of stopping | The larger share | The smaller share |
| Best for | Slowing and stopping on a firm, straight road | Low-speed control, steadying, holding at rest |
| If used alone | Abrupt use can lift the rear wheel | Can lock the rear wheel |
| What to do | Squeeze progressively | Press progressively, together with the front |

## The order Honda gives, and why each step matters

Honda's training document sets out the sequence, and each step has a reason.

1. **Release the throttle completely.** Your right hand cannot brake properly while also driving the bike forward.
2. **Use both brakes together, building pressure steadily, with the front stronger.** Steady pressure lets the weight transfer happen smoothly.
3. **Keep the bike upright and the steering straight.** Braking is most effective when the bike is vertical and the wheels are in line.
4. **Stop, then hold the brake with the right foot and put the left foot down.**

The document also teaches a rounded posture under braking, with the back curved, the weight pushed slightly rearward to resist the forward push and the arms straight so you do not slump onto the bars. These details help you stay in control while the bike slows. ## How the surface changes the balance

Grip is the limit on braking, and the surface sets it.

**Dry, clean tarmac.** This is the best case. Use both brakes with the front stronger, progressively, and keep the bike upright.

**Wet roads.** Grip is lower, so each brake reaches its limit sooner. Honda's Wave RSX FI manual notes that water in the brakes reduces their effectiveness and suggests applying them lightly at low speed to dry them. Brake earlier, press and squeeze more gently, and avoid sudden inputs. Our guide to [riding a semi-automatic bike in heavy Hanoi rain](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/riding-a-semi-automatic-bike-in-heavy-hanoi-rain/) covers wet-weather riding in more detail.

**Loose or dirty surfaces.** Gravel, sand, dust and mud give little grip. Slow down before you reach them, avoid braking hard on them and keep the bike upright. A front tyre that slides on a loose surface is hard to recover.

**Painted lines, metal covers and oily patches.** These are slippery, especially when wet. Brake before you reach them, not on them.

**In a bend.** Honda's cornering guidance is to reduce speed before the bend and to limit braking within it, because heavy braking in a turn can make a wheel slide. Our guide to [downshifting safely before corners on a small motorbike](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/downshifting-safely-before-corners-on-a-small-motorbike/) explains the sequence.

If you feel a tyre begin to slide on any surface, our practical advice, not a Honda instruction, is to ease the pressure a little and then apply it again more gently, rather than holding or increasing it.

## Hills and long descents

Brakes work by turning movement into heat. Honda's Wave RSX FI manual warns that braking continuously, as on a long steep descent, can overheat the brake system and reduce its effectiveness. The manual's remedy is engine braking: release the throttle and shift to a lower gear so the engine helps to hold the speed. Then use the brakes in short, firm applications rather than dragging them. Our guides to [engine braking on a semi-automatic motorcycle](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/engine-braking-on-a-semi-automatic-motorcycle/) and [riding a semi-automatic bike on steep mountain roads](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/manual-bikes-and-steep-mountain-roads-when-to-choose-another-vehicle/) explain how.

On a long descent, resist the temptation to rely on the rear brake alone to save the front. Dragging the rear brake continuously heats the drum and fades the brake, and it leaves you with less in reserve when you need it.

## A drum or a disc at the front

Small manual bikes can have a drum or a disc at the front, and a rental may be either. Yamaha Motor Vietnam lists Sirius FI versions with drum and with front-disc equipment, and Honda's aftersales material describes both types.

In a drum brake, the lever pulls a cable or rod that pushes shoes against the inside of a drum. The lever has free play, which Honda's Wave RSX FI manual gives as 10 to 20 mm at the lever for the version it covers. In a disc brake, the lever moves a piston in a master cylinder, and brake fluid pressure pushes pads against a disc. The disc system has a fluid level to check, and the lever may feel different from a drum.

You do not need to know the engineering. What matters is to find out which front brake your rental has, and to test it slowly before you rely on it. Do not assume a disc brake behaves like the drum brake you rode last time, or the reverse. ## With a passenger or luggage

A heavier bike needs more distance to stop. Honda's manuals warn that overloading affects handling and braking. With a passenger or bags, start slowing earlier, press the controls more gently and leave a larger gap. A passenger who leans or shifts at the wrong moment also upsets the bike, so brief them before you go. ## Three practice drills in a quiet place

Find a wide, clean, dry, legal area with no traffic, such as a quiet car park where you have permission to ride. Wear your helmet, long trousers and closed shoes. None of these drills asks you to lock a wheel.

**Drill 1: find the lever and pedal points.** With the engine off and the bike on its stand, squeeze the front lever slowly and note where the resistance begins. Press the rear pedal and note the same. Know where each bites before you ride.

**Drill 2: slow, progressive stops.** Ride at about the pace of a bicycle. Close the throttle, squeeze the front lever and press the rear pedal together, gently at first, and stop in a straight line with your right foot on the pedal and your left foot down. Repeat until the stop is smooth and the bike does not nod forward.

**Drill 3: a little faster, still progressive.** Repeat Drill 2 at a moderate speed, again building the pressure steadily. Notice how the front end dips and how the front lever needs a firmer squeeze. Stop if either tyre chirps or slides, and go back to a lower speed.

## Common mistakes

- **Grabbing instead of squeezing.** A sudden grab on the lever can lift the rear or slide the front.
- **Stamping on the pedal in a panic.** This is the quickest route to a locked rear wheel.
- **Using only one brake.** Honda's document names both single-brake mistakes.
- **Braking in the middle of a bend.** Slow before the turn, as Honda advises.
- **Braking while still holding the throttle open.** Close the throttle first.
- **Forgetting the brake light.** Engine braking alone does not light the brake lamp, so a rider behind may not know you are slowing. A light touch of a brake shows the lamp.
- **Ignoring a change in feel.** A lever or pedal that feels different today is worth a word with the shop. ## FAQ: front and rear brakes

### Which brake should I use first?

Use both together, with the front stronger. Honda's training document teaches both brakes together, built up steadily, after you close the throttle.

### Is it safe to use the front brake on a small bike?

Yes, if you apply it progressively. Honda warns that using it abruptly and alone can lift the rear wheel, so squeeze rather than grab.

### Why does the rear wheel lock so easily?

Because braking shifts weight forward, so the rear tyre is lightened just when you ask it to stop the bike. That is basic physics, not a Honda figure.

### Can I use only the rear brake to be safe?

No. Honda's document warns that using only the rear brake can lock the rear wheel, and the front brake is the stronger of the two.

### How do I brake on a long downhill?

Release the throttle and use a lower gear so engine braking helps, then use the brakes in short applications. Honda warns that continuous braking can overheat them.

### Does a disc brake change how I brake?

The technique is the same, but the feel differs from a drum. Find out which type your rental has and test it slowly first.
