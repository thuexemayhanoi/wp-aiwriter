Hills cost an electric motorbike energy on the way up and give only part of it back on the way down. Hanoi itself is almost flat, so a city rider will barely notice, but the mountains that people like to visit from the capital, Ba Vi and Tam Dao, change the sums completely. Climbing is a physics problem: the energy needed depends on the combined weight of bike, rider and passenger and on the height gained, and a battery has to supply all of it. This guide puts numbers on a climb, explains what regenerative braking and descents really return, shows how a short flyover differs from a mountain road, and gives a checklist for deciding whether a hill ride suits a rental electric bike. The calculations are the author's own, based on published specifications.

## Why Hanoi barely tests a battery

Official city information says Hanoi's plains cover about three quarters of its area and that the average altitude is 5 to 20 metres above sea level, with the hills concentrated in the north and west. The Red River delta is the reason: the city sits on flat alluvial land.

For a rented electric motorbike used inside the urban area, that is good news. Most of the energy goes into starting, stopping and moving the weight along the road, not into lifting it. The only real climbs are short ones, such as flyover ramps and bridge approaches, and they are over in seconds.

Hills become a serious question once you leave the plain. Our guides to [Hanoi to Tam Dao by motorbike](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/hanoi-to-tam-dao-by-motorbike/) and [Hanoi to Ba Vi National Park](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/hanoi-to-ba-vi-by-motorbike/) describe the routes for petrol bikes. This article asks what changes if the bike is electric.

## The energy a climb costs: a worked example

The energy needed to lift a mass is mass times gravity times height. In watt-hours, that is mass in kilograms times 9.81 times height in metres, divided by 3,600.

Take a scooter about the size of a VinFast Feliz S, listed at 110 kg with its battery, and a 65 kg rider. The combined mass is about 175 kg. A 100 m climb needs about 48 Wh just to lift that mass. Allowing for motor and controller losses, the battery may supply nearly 70 Wh, assuming roughly 70 per cent efficiency (an assumption, not a published figure).

How does that compare with flat riding? The published ratings of several VinFast models work out to about 17 to 18 Wh per km at their test conditions of 30 km/h and a 65 kg rider (3.5 kWh over 203 km for the Evo200, for example). So a 100 m climb costs about as much as four kilometres on the flat. Climb 400 m and you have spent the equivalent of roughly 15 km of cruising before counting the rest of the ride.

| Climb height | Energy to lift 175 kg | Battery draw at about 70% efficiency | Equivalent flat riding |
|---|---|---|---|
| 6 m (a flyover) | about 3 Wh | about 4 Wh | well under 1 km |
| 100 m | about 48 Wh | about 68 Wh | about 4 km |
| 400 m | about 190 Wh | about 270 Wh | about 15 km |
| 850 m | about 405 Wh | about 580 Wh | about 33 km |

These are illustrations, not measurements of a specific road. The efficiency figure and the 17 to 18 Wh per km rate are assumptions taken from other models and test conditions, and a rental bike may differ.

## Why a flyover is nothing and a mountain is something

Look again at the first row. A 6 m ramp costs a few watt-hours, a fraction of a per cent of a 3.5 kWh battery. You will never notice it on the gauge, and a day of Hanoi flyovers adds up to very little.

The mountain is the opposite. Tam Dao town sits at roughly 900 m above sea level, according to tourism and reference sources, and the national park is about 85 km from Hanoi. Climb from a plain at 50 m to the town and you gain about 850 m, which the table puts at around 580 Wh. That is about 17 per cent of a 3.5 kWh battery spent only on height, on top of the 85 km of riding to reach the foot of the range.

Ba Vi is higher. The highest summit in the national park is given as 1,296 m and the park is about 48 to 50 km west of the city. The road does not go to the summit, but even a partial climb adds up.

## What the descent gives back

Electric motors can run in reverse. Under regenerative braking, the wheels turn the motor, which acts as a generator and feeds some energy back into the battery. A press review of the VinFast Feliz Lite mentions regenerative braking, and other models vary, so ask what your bike has.

Do not expect the climb's energy to come back. Three reasons:

1. **Losses both ways.** An engineering study from Chalmers University notes that energy recovery is limited by the motor and power electronics and by stability requirements, and that recovery happens only through the driven wheels.
2. **Recovery depends on how you ride.** The same study says hard braking can restrict recovery, and real recovery depends strongly on traffic and style.
3. **Gentle slopes recover nothing.** On a shallow downhill, gravity may not even overcome rolling friction and wind, so there is nothing to recover.

A review in the journal Energies confirms the general picture: recovery rises on descents and energy use rises on climbs, but regeneration is nearly impossible on the uphill side, so over a full cycle the average recovery efficiency is reduced. The sensible assumption is that a descent returns a fraction of what the climb took.

## Heat, load and the battery gauge on a long climb

A long climb asks the motor and battery for high current for a long time. Several things follow.

- **The gauge can fall faster than the distance suggests.** Percentage readings may drop quickly under heavy load and settle back when the load eases. Treat the gauge as a guide, not a measurement.
- **Speed falls.** A scooter with a small motor may slow to a walking pace on a steep gradient, especially with two people, and some limit power to protect themselves.
- **Weight matters more.** A passenger and luggage add directly to the lifting energy. Our guide to [how manual bikes cope with steep mountain roads](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/manual-bikes-and-steep-mountain-roads-when-to-choose-another-vehicle/) explains the same issue for petrol bikes.
- **Brakes work hard on the way down.** Use engine braking or regeneration where the bike offers it, and brake in short firm applications rather than dragging the brakes. Drum rear brakes can fade on a long descent.
- **Climbing with a battery that is already low is a mistake.** Starting up the hill at a low percentage leaves you stranded with no charger.

VinFast's own guidance on its older e-scooters mentions that models like the Klara S could climb a 10-degree slope, about 18 per cent, but those are different, older vehicles and not the figures of whatever bike you hold.

## Hill or no hill: a pre-ride checklist

Before taking an electric rental toward the hills, run through these questions.

1. **How far is the round trip?** Tam Dao is roughly 85 km each way, so around 170 km return before any climbing. Many published headline ranges are 120 to 205 km, which is less than a comfortable margin.
2. **What is the real range?** Cut the headline figure sharply. Our [guide to renting an electric scooter](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/renting-an-electric-scooter-in-hanoi/) explains why.
3. **Where can you charge on the hill?** Charging at a hotel is slow, with several hours for a full charge.
4. **How much weight is aboard?** Add rider, passenger and bags, and check the load limit.
5. **Does the shop allow the route?** Our article on [taking a rental motorbike out of Hanoi](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/take-rental-motorbike-out-of-hanoi/) explains how to ask.
6. **Is there a petrol alternative for the day?** For a mountain ride, usually yes, and it is often the better choice.

## FAQ: hills and electric range

### Do hills reduce electric motorbike range?

Yes. Climbing uses extra energy in proportion to weight and height, and only part of it returns on the descent.

### How much battery does a mountain climb use?

By the physics, a 175 kg bike and rider climbing 850 m needs about 405 Wh to lift, or roughly 580 Wh from the battery at 70 per cent efficiency, which is about 17 per cent of a 3.5 kWh pack. That is on top of the riding itself.

### Does regenerative braking recover the energy used on a climb?

Only some of it. Losses, riding style and the slope all limit recovery.

### Is Hanoi hilly?

Not in the urban area. The average altitude is 5 to 20 m, and the hills are mainly in the north and west of the municipality.

### Should I take an electric rental to Tam Dao or Ba Vi?

It is rarely the best choice, because of the distance, the climbing and limited charging. Ask the shop which vehicle suits the route.
