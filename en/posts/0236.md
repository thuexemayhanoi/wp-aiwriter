An electric scooter's battery indicator is an estimate of the energy left, not a measurement you can trust to the last kilometre. Bars or a percentage show roughly how full the battery is, a range figure guesses how far you can go if you keep riding the way you have been, and all three can move quickly when you climb, accelerate hard, carry a passenger or ride in cold weather. For a rental, the practical way to read the indicator is to note it at pickup, watch the trend rather than a single glance, treat the last quarter as a reserve and head for a charger early. This guide explains what each type of display is telling you, why the numbers wobble, what a low-battery warning means and how to plan a ride so the display never becomes a surprise. It is general information; the bike in front of you may differ.

## What the indicator is really measuring

A battery does not have a fuel tank with a float in it. The scooter's battery management system, a small computer inside the pack, watches voltage, current and temperature and then estimates how much charge remains. The result is shown as a state of charge, expressed as bars, a percentage or both.

Simpler systems lean mostly on voltage. A guide from a battery seller, em3ev, describes the bars as being like a digital fuel gauge that reads voltage rather than energy: when you pull hard on the motor, the voltage dips and the display briefly reports a battery that looks emptier than it is. A guide from the e-bike brand Sixthreezero makes the same point in plainer words: battery displays are estimates, not lab instruments, and the most accurate gauge is your own experience on your own routes.

More advanced systems also count the current going in and out and use the history of charging and discharging, so their figures tend to be steadier. You cannot always tell from outside which system a rental scooter uses, which is one more reason to treat any reading as approximate.

## Bars, percentage and range: three kinds of display

### Bar graph

Most basic scooters and electric bicycles show four, five or ten segments. Each bar covers a wide slice, so the last bar can last a surprisingly long or short time depending on how you ride. A bar that flashes usually means the battery is low or near its protection cut-off.

### Percentage

A percentage looks precise, but it is only as good as the estimate behind it. A reading of 38 percent means about 38 percent of what the system thinks the full capacity is, and the system's idea of full capacity changes as the battery ages.

### Estimated range

A range number in kilometres combines the charge level with an assumption about how you will ride. If you have been cruising gently, it shows a generous figure. If you then climb a ramp or ride faster, it drops. Treat it as a rough guide for the next few kilometres, not a promise for the next fifty.

## Why the reading jumps around

Several ordinary things move the display without anything being wrong:

- **Hard acceleration and hills.** Heavy current makes the voltage sag, so the bars drop, then recover when you ease off. Battery guides describe this sag as normal behaviour.
- **Cold or hot batteries.** Cold raises the battery's internal resistance, so the display can read lower than the same battery would on a warm day. Hanoi's cool winter mornings can show this.
- **Load.** A passenger and luggage increase current, so both the percentage and the range figure fall faster.
- **Recalibration.** A battery management system can correct its estimate, often after a full charge, so a sudden step in the figure is frequently a recalculation, not lost capacity. A battery maker's explainer on LiFePO4 packs, a different battery type, describes the same effect.

None of this means the battery is faulty. A faulty one shows different symptoms, such as a scooter that cuts out when the display still shows a healthy level, a battery that is very hot or swollen, or a charge that never completes.

## The first minute at pickup

For a rental, the display is a record as well as a gauge. For electric bikes, the shop confirms the battery level and charging arrangements at handover, so use that moment.

1. Switch on and let the display settle for a few seconds.
2. Note the bars or percentage and any range figure.
3. Photograph the dashboard so you have the starting level.
4. Ask the staff what the level means in practice: how far they expect the bike to go from there, and what the display does when it is low.
5. Check for any warning icons that are lit before you move.

## What the warnings usually mean

Every manufacturer uses its own symbols, so ask the shop to point out the ones on your bike. In general:

| What you see | What it usually means | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Last bar or low percentage, steady | The battery is low | Head for a charger now |
| Last bar flashing or an empty battery icon | Very low or close to the protection cut-off | Ride gently and stop at the nearest safe charger |
| Power drops or the scooter slows by itself | Power limiting to protect the battery | Stop in a safe place; do not keep twisting the throttle |
| Display shows a full battery but the scooter feels weak | Possible fault, cold battery or display issue | Call the shop |
| Battery warning plus a hot smell, swelling or smoke | Possible battery fault | Stop, switch off, move away and call for help |

If a symbol is unfamiliar, do not guess. Take a photograph, stop somewhere safe and phone the shop on 0334 699 969 (+84 334 699 969), open 09:00 to 21:00 daily.

## Turning the display into a plan

A sensible habit for any rental is a margin, and a simple version is three steps.

First, decide your furthest point before you set off. Then ask how much of the indicator that journey is likely to use. Finally, plan to begin the return journey or reach a charger while a quarter or more is still showing. The quarter is not a rule from a manufacturer; it is a personal safety margin, because the last part of the display is where estimates are least reliable and where power limiting may begin.

Plan for the places you will actually be. ## Three situations, read step by step

These are illustrations, not measurements from a particular bike.

**Pulling away from a red light with a passenger.** The bars dip as you accelerate and recover a few seconds later. This is the sag described above. Do not count it as lost range, but do notice that a heavy start costs more than a gentle one.

**Crossing a long bridge on a cool morning.** The display may read a little lower than it did in the afternoon on the same battery. Give it a few minutes of steady riding before you decide the battery is weaker than you thought.

**Coming back with a flashing icon.** Here the display has stopped being a trend and become an instruction. Slow down, avoid hard acceleration and ride directly to the nearest safe place to charge or to the shop. If the bike slows by itself, do not fight it; pull over safely and call.

The difference between the first two and the third is the rule to remember: wobble that recovers is normal, and a warning that stays on is not.

## A note on battery health and the 20 to 80 percent range

VinFast's FAQ for three of its e-scooter models, the Klara S, Impes and Ludo, which are not necessarily the shop's rental bikes, says battery life lasts longer if the battery is used and stored between 20 and 80 percent of capacity, and that lithium batteries are sensitive to high temperatures, so you should avoid charging them when hot. For a renter this is a useful habit rather than a strict rule: you do not need to run the battery flat on purpose, and you should not be afraid of a partial charge.

When you bring the scooter back, look at the display again and photograph it. If the shop asks how much charge was left, you can answer exactly. ## Quick checklist

- Photograph the display at pickup and return.
- Ask what the bars or percentage mean on this bike.
- Watch the trend over several minutes, not one glance.
- Expect dips on hills and recovery on the flat.
- Treat the last quarter as a reserve.
- Head for a charger early, and do not ride with a flashing battery icon.
- Report a hot, swollen or smelly battery at once.

## FAQ: electric scooter battery indicator

### Why did the battery drop 10 percent on one hill?

Heavy current on a climb makes the voltage sag, and a voltage-based indicator reads that as less charge. Much of it often returns on level ground.

### Is the range figure accurate?

Only roughly. It assumes you will ride the way you have recently ridden, so it changes when speed, load or terrain change.

### Can I trust the last bar?

Treat it as a warning, not a countdown. The last stretch is where estimates are weakest.

### What if the scooter shuts off with some charge showing?

The system may be protecting the battery under load, or the estimate may be off. Stop in a safe place, switch off for a minute and call the shop if it happens again.

### Does the indicator show the battery's age?

Not directly. An older battery holds less, so the same percentage gives a shorter distance than when new.
