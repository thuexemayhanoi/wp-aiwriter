On most long-term electric rentals the renter pays for the electricity the bike uses, in the same way a petrol-bike renter pays for fuel, because the rent covers the vehicle and not its energy. The catch is that this is a custom, not a rule: the contract can say otherwise, and the cost depends on where you plug in. Nguyen Ha's published information confirms that battery level and charging are checked at handover, but it does not publish an electricity policy for weekly or monthly rentals, so ask and get the answer written down. This guide shows how much power a month of riding really uses, who normally supplies the socket, what Vietnamese tariff rules say about electricity in rented homes, and which questions to settle before you sign.

## Why a long rental raises the question

For a one-day rental nobody thinks about electricity. You ride away with the battery at the level recorded at handover and hand the bike back. Over a month the position changes. You will charge the bike dozens of times, usually somewhere you do not own: a guesthouse, a serviced apartment, a shared house or a friend's courtyard. Each charge uses power that somebody pays for, and that somebody is not always you.

The money is usually small beside the rent, but disputes about it are common because nobody agreed the rules in advance. A host who sees an extra line on the meter, a shop that finds a charger missing, and a renter who assumed "unlimited charging" was included can all be reasonable and still disagree. Fixing that takes one short conversation at the start.

## What the shop's information does and does not say

Nguyen Ha's price page lists monthly rental for the electric group, which covers electric bicycles, 50cc-equivalent electric scooters and some VinFast electric motorbikes, with the exact bike confirmed when you book. What the shop states about energy is limited to handover: the battery level and charging are confirmed when you collect the bike. For petrol bikes the shop is more specific: they are handed over with about 0.5 to 2 litres and come back with about 0.5 to 1 litre, and an empty tank at return costs VND 20,000.

The petrol rule shows the logic. The renter supplies the running energy and returns the bike with a sensible reserve. By analogy, an electric renter would supply the electricity and return the bike with a sensible battery level, but the analogy is not a promise. ## How much electricity a month of riding uses

You can estimate the energy without any prices. The example uses one VinFast model, the Evo200, because VinFast publishes both its battery size and its tested range. It is not necessarily the bike you will be given, and a rental bike with an older battery will do worse.

VinFast lists the Evo200 with a 3.5 kWh battery and a range of 145 km in its mixed-conditions test. Dividing the two gives roughly 2.4 kWh per 100 km at the battery. The table scales that figure for different daily distances.

| Daily distance | Distance in 30 days | Approximate energy used |
|---|---|---|
| 10 km | 300 km | about 7 kWh |
| 20 km | 600 km | about 14 kWh |
| 30 km | 900 km | about 22 kWh |
| 40 km | 1,200 km | about 29 kWh |

Treat these as the floor. Chargers waste some energy as heat, so the meter records more than the battery receives, and Hanoi stop-and-go riding with a passenger or luggage is harder than a test cycle. To turn the figures into money, multiply by the price per kWh on the bill or the host's rate. A bike used for short errands is a small cost; one that covers 40 km a day is a real line in the budget.

## Where the electricity actually comes from

There are four common sources, and each has a different answer to "who pays".

**A socket at your accommodation.** You pay, directly or through the host, according to whatever you agreed. This is the usual case and the one where the next section matters.

**The shop's own socket.** Some shops charge a bike at the premises before a handover or during a return. If the shop does this routinely, the cost is part of how it runs the fleet. If you are expected to bring the bike in for charging, that is a time cost for you and should be understood.

**Public charging points or battery-swap stations.** These typically bill the person using them, usually through an app. Ask the shop which networks suit the bike and whether any account in the shop's name is involved. Our guide to [charging an electric scooter in Hanoi](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/charging-an-electric-scooter-in-hanoi/) covers hotels, swap stations and timing.

**Electricity included in the rent.** Possible in principle, but not something to assume. If a shop says it is included, ask whether there is a limit, such as a charge per night or a monthly cap, and have the wording put on the contract.

## What Vietnamese rules say about electricity in rented homes

If you rent a room or flat for a month or longer, state tariff rules may apply to the electricity you use, including what you charge the bike with. The rules below concern people renting a home to live in. The sources use the phrase students and workers renting houses, and I found no source applying them to a hotel or homestay stay, so do not assume your host is bound by them.

- Household electricity in Vietnam is sold on a tiered scale: the more kWh a household uses, the higher the price of the extra units. The Ministry of Industry and Trade set the national average retail price in Decision 1279/QD-BCT of 9 May 2025. A Thu Vien Phap Luat summary says the household scale has since been cut from six tiers to five.
- EVN's tariff notes say that where a home is rented for under 12 months and the landlord has not declared all the users, the tier 3 price applies to the whole meter reading. Where the landlord declares the users, the electricity company allots a quota, with every four people counted as one household.
- In May 2026 the Hanoi electricity company reported that landlords who charge tenants more than the state price are penalised under Decree 133/2026/ND-CP, effective from 25 May 2026, and must refund the excess.

For a renter, the practical message is simple: when a landlord quotes a flat price per kWh, ask how it relates to the state tariff and whether the figures are on the meter. Do not quote rule numbers to a host in anger; ask for the meter reading and a simple note of what you used.

## A fair way to split charging costs

Three arrangements work well in practice.

1. **Charge where you sleep and pay at the host's stated rate,** with the meter or sub-meter read at the start and end. This is transparent and cheap if the host charges the state rate.
2. **Agree a flat monthly contribution** for charging if reading a meter is impractical. It is simple, but test it against the kWh table: a flat sum that suits a rider doing 10 km a day will not suit one doing 40.
3. **Use public charging or swap points for part of the month** and keep the receipts, if the host does not want a bike plugged in indoors.

Whichever you choose, put the figure and the method in a message or on the paper, and keep it with your copy of the contract.

## Costs that are not kWh

Electricity is the visible cost. Others can matter as much.

- **The charger.** Ask who owns it, whether it is handed over with the bike and what happens if it is lost or damaged. A missing charger is a different bill from a high meter reading.
- **Battery wear.** Normal ageing is the owner's problem; damage from misuse is usually yours. VinFast advises avoiding charging a hot battery and notes that a battery lasts longer used between roughly 20 and 80 per cent. Ask whether the contract expects you to follow any charging routine.
- **Overnight charging safety.** If the host forbids charging indoors or overnight, the practical cost of charging goes up. Ask about this before you take a long rental.
- **Late return.** Charging time at the end can matter if the bike must be returned at a recorded battery level. The shop's published late-return rule is VND 30,000 per hour for one to five hours. ## Questions to settle before you sign

Use this as a checklist, and write the answers down.

1. Is electricity included in the rent, or do I pay for it myself?
2. If included, is there a cap or a limit on where I may charge?
3. Which charger comes with the bike, and who pays for a lost or damaged one?
4. What battery level will be recorded at handover and expected at return?
5. May I charge at my accommodation, and has the host agreed?
6. Are there public charging or swap networks that work with this bike, and who holds the account?
7. What happens if the battery will not charge or loses capacity during the month?
8. Who pays if the problem is the bike's charging system rather than my use?

## FAQ: electricity on a long electric rental

### Does the rent usually include electricity?

Not necessarily. Many arrangements treat electricity like fuel and leave it to the renter. Nguyen Ha publishes no electricity term for long rentals, so ask.

### How much power does an electric scooter use in a month?

It depends on distance and the bike. Using one VinFast model's published figures, roughly 14 kWh for 600 km, before charger losses. Real use is higher.

### Can my landlord charge me more than the state price?

For homes rented to live in, the electricity company says landlords who charge above the state price are penalised and must refund the excess. Hotel and homestay stays were not covered by the sources I found.

### What if my host will not let me charge indoors?

Ask the shop about public charging or swap points, or choose a bike or accommodation that suits. Do not run an extension lead through a window.

### Who pays if the battery fails?

It depends on whether the fault is ageing or misuse, and on the contract. Ask before you sign.
