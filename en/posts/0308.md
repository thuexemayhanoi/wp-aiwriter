The safest place to leave a scooter at Quan Thanh Temple is an attended motorbike space on or near the temple's own corner, where a person hands you a ticket, not a stretch of pavement or the kerb at the junction. The temple stands where Quan Thanh Street meets Thanh Nien Street, beside West Lake and Truc Bach Lake, so the corner is busy and the rules on stopping there are strict. Visitor guides disagree on whether the temple has its own bike area, so treat any claim as something to confirm on arrival. This guide shows how to choose a spot, which spots to rule out under the 2024 road law, when the temple is calmer, and what to do with a helmet and a bag once you walk through the gate.

## Where the temple sits and why that decides your parking

Quan Thanh Temple, also called Tran Vu Temple, is at the corner of the old Co Ngu road, now Thanh Nien Road, and Quan Thanh Street, near West Lake and Truc Bach Lake, according to the national tourism administration. Vinpearl's visitor guide gives the street address as 190 Quan Thanh Street. The same tourism material describes Quan Thanh Street as a busy thoroughfare where temples and old villas stand next to office blocks and shops, and that matches what you see at the corner.

Three things follow from that position:

- **It is a junction.** The law bars stopping and parking at an intersection and within 5 metres of its edge, so the corner itself is not a parking place even if other bikes are leaning there.
- **Two roads feed it.** Quan Thanh Street carries traffic toward the Old Quarter and the Ba Dinh area, while Thanh Nien Road runs between the two lakes, so a bike left carelessly can block a cyclist, a pedestrian or a turning vehicle.
- **The lake side is scenic and crowded.** Walkers, photographers and ice-cream sellers use the Thanh Nien side, which means a parked scooter competes with people for space.

Travel guides put the temple roughly 3 to 4 kilometres from the Old Quarter, which they estimate at 10 to 15 minutes by motorbike outside peak hours. Treat that as a rough figure; rush hour and the lake roads change it.

## Four kinds of place a scooter can wait

You will not find a single official bike park for tourists, so think in types of place rather than a named lot.

1. **The temple's own motorbike area, if an attendant is there.** One travel agency's guide says dedicated motorbike parking is available at the temple. Another popular guide does not mention it. If a person in a vest or cap waves you in and gives you a ticket, use it; if nobody is there, do not leave the bike on a hope.
2. **An attended lot on a side street.** Look for someone with tickets, a rack of bikes in rows and an obvious entrance. Our [guide to parking a motorbike in Hanoi](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/parking-a-motorbike-in-hanoi/) explains how to recognise an authorised service and what the ticket is for.
3. **A cafe or hotel space.** If you are buying a drink, ask the staff whether you can leave the bike in their frontage and for how long. Do not assume.
4. **Farther away, then walk.** If the nearby streets look full, park at a proper lot a few minutes off and walk. Walking the last few hundred metres around the lakes is part of the visit anyway.

None of these needs a phone app or a guess about prices. If a fee applies, the attendant tells you or shows a board, and you pay when you collect the bike. Keep small notes ready.

## Spots to rule out

The 2024 Law on Road Traffic Order and Safety, which took effect on 1 January 2025, lists where drivers and riders must not stop or park. Several entries apply directly to a busy temple corner:

- on the left side of a one-way street;
- at an intersection or within 5 metres of its edge;
- on a pedestrian crossing;
- at a pick-up or drop-off point for passengers;
- on a bridge, unless traffic organisation allows it (not relevant here, but worth knowing);
- anywhere the bike hides a road sign or traffic light;
- on pavements and carriageways where local rules forbid it.

The law also says that on a street you stop and park close to the kerb or pavement on your right-hand side, with your nearest wheel no more than 0.25 metres from it, and that you must not endanger or obstruct people and vehicles. When you leave the bike, use the parking brake or another safe measure, and signal to traffic behind you when you pull in and out.

| Spot | Verdict | Why |
|---|---|---|
| The junction corner of Quan Thanh and Thanh Nien | Avoid | Within 5 metres of an intersection |
| Right in front of the temple gateway | Avoid | Blocks worshippers and pedestrians; ask staff first |
| Beside a bus stop or taxi bay | Avoid | Pick-up and drop-off points are listed as no-stopping |
| Attended space with a ticket | Good | Someone is responsible for the row |
| Marked bay on the right-hand kerb | Good if signed | Follows the 0.25 metre kerb rule |
| Pavement with a "no parking" sign | Avoid | Local signs and rules decide |

## Arriving without a last-second turn

A parking decision is also a route decision. Work out which side of the road the attended space is on before you arrive, so you are not tempted to turn across a busy lane at the last moment. The law requires you to signal, slow down, move gradually into the lane nearest the direction of the turn and give way to pedestrians, cyclists and oncoming vehicles. It also forbids U-turns on bridges and bridge approaches, at level crossings, on narrow roads and on one-way streets unless a sign or an officer allows them.

If you pass the spot you wanted, carry on to the next safe place to turn, or park at the next lot and walk back. That costs a few minutes and avoids the worst move at a crowded junction.

## Timing the visit

Opening hours vary by source, and temples change them for festivals. Vinpearl's visitor guide lists 8:00 to 17:00 every day, longer hours on the 1st and 15th days of the lunar month, and an all-day opening on Lunar New Year's Eve. Several travel guides give the same daytime hours. Confirm on the day, especially if you plan to arrive late.

- **Early morning** is usually calmer on the pavement, and the light on the lake is good.
- **The 1st and 15th lunar days** bring more worshippers, offerings and parked bikes. Choose a lot rather than looking for a space at the gate.
- **Weekend afternoons** add lakeside strollers and cafe traffic from Truc Bach.
- **Rain** makes painted road markings and polished kerbs slick. See our guide to [riding in Hanoi's rain](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/riding-in-hanoi-rain/) for tyre and braking habits.

Guides suggest allowing between one and two hours at the temple.

## Helmet, bag and bike security

One basic helmet per rider is included with a rental from Nguyen Ha Motorbike Rental. Carry it with you or ask the attendant whether helmets may be stored; do not hang it on the mirror, where it invites theft and can fall.

- Lock the steering before you walk away. The shop does not provide an extra lock, so the steering lock is your main protection, together with a guarded space.
- Take your phone, passport copy and wallet out of the seat box.
- Photograph where you parked, including the street name or a nearby shop sign, in case you forget.
- Keep the parking ticket somewhere safe, such as a zipped pocket with the key, and check that its number or plate matches your bike when you return.

Quan Thanh pairs naturally with other lakeside stops. A tourism guide says Tran Quoc Pagoda stands on a small peninsula in West Lake about 900 metres from the temple, which is walkable for most visitors. ## Respectful behaviour at the gate

Quan Thanh is a working temple, not only a sight. Visitor guides advise modest clothing that covers the shoulders and knees, and quiet behaviour in the courtyard. Do not ride into the courtyard or use the gateway as a turning space, follow the instructions of temple staff, and ask before photographing people who are praying. Leave the engine off while you wait for a friend near the entrance, and avoid revving beside the gate.

## A short checklist before you walk away

1. Is the spot more than 5 metres from the corner of an intersection?
2. Is there an attendant, a ticket or a marked bay?
3. Are you on the right-hand side of the street in the direction you rode?
4. Is the bike within a short distance of the kerb and not blocking a gate, ramp or pedestrian route?
5. Is the steering locked, with nothing valuable in the seat box?
6. Do you have the ticket, the key and a photo of the street?

## FAQ about leaving a scooter at Quan Thanh Temple

### Is there parking inside the temple?

Sources conflict. One travel guide says the temple has dedicated motorbike parking; others do not mention it. Ask the attendant or staff on arrival, and use only a space with a ticket system. Do not ride into the courtyard.

### Can I stop for a few minutes at the gate?

Stopping briefly to let a passenger get off is allowed in principle, but not at the junction, at a bus or pick-up point, or where you block pedestrians. It is simpler to park properly and walk.

### Is it better to take a taxi instead?

For a single temple stop in rush hour, a ride-hailing car can be easier. If your plan includes West Lake, Truc Bach and Tran Quoc Pagoda, a scooter and one parked spot is more flexible. The rental shop in Long Bien can explain how to plan the day; see the [contact page](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/contact/) or the [prices page](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/prices/) for current terms, and ask which bike will be assigned to you.

### Does the temple close for lunch?

Sources list a continuous day schedule, but temples can change hours. Check on the day.

### What if I cannot find a legal space?

Move on. Park at an attended lot a few minutes away or choose a different time. Never leave the bike at a corner because other riders have.
