Motorbikes are not allowed on Vietnam's expressways, so the alternative to a highway route near Hanoi is the older national highway or provincial road that runs beside it, often with a quieter river or hill road as a third option. For the south that means the old National Highway 1 instead of the Phap Van to Cau Gie expressway, for the east National Highway 5 instead of the Hanoi to Hai Phong expressway, for the north-east the Nhat Tan and National Highway 18 corridor, and for the west National Highway 6 instead of the Hoa Lac to Hoa Binh expressway. Expect slower, busier roads with more trucks and junctions. This guide, general planning information as of October 2026, shows how to pick an alternative corridor, what to expect and how to test it before you ride.

## What "highway" means in Vietnam, and why the word causes trouble

English speakers use "highway" for any fast main road. In Vietnam two different kinds of road are involved, and the difference decides where you may ride.

- **Expressway (cao toc, shown as CT on maps and signs).** A controlled-access road with ramps, tolls and a minimum speed. Law 36/2024/QH15, effective 1 January 2025, says in Article 25 that motorcycles and mopeds may not use expressways, apart from people and vehicles serving road maintenance. Size and power make no difference.
- **National highway (quoc lo, shown as QL, for example QL1A or QL6).** An ordinary public road that may have tolls, lanes for different vehicles and heavy freight, but where motorbikes are generally allowed unless a sign says otherwise.
- **Provincial road (shown as DT or TL).** A smaller road that links towns and villages, usually slower and narrower.

A rider guide puts the warning in one line: roads labelled CT are expressways, and bikes are not allowed on them. A toll is not the test, because some tolled roads are open to motorbikes and some untolled ones are not. Our [guide to Hanoi's ring roads and highways](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/hanoi-ring-roads-highways-motorbikes-allowed/) lists the main closed roads, and our guide to [checking a navigation app for expressways](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/how-to-check-if-a-navigation-app-suggests-an-expressway/) shows how to catch them on your phone.

### South: the old National Highway 1

For Ninh Binh, the old National Highway 1 is the default alternative. Our [Hanoi to Ninh Binh guide](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/hanoi-to-ninh-binh-by-motorbike/) budgets about 95 km to the city and two and a half to three and a half hours of riding. One rider's guide calls QL1A heavily used by trucks and freight, and suggests following the Red River on either bank for a quieter approach, at the price of extra time. That is an opinion rather than a rule, but it matches what many riders report, and it illustrates the central trade-off: the direct road is the stressful one.

If a one-day Ninh Binh trip tempts you, read the [one-day itinerary](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/hanoi-ninh-binh-one-day-motorbike-itinerary/) first, since the extra time on the old road eats into the day.

### East: National Highway 5

National Highway 5 runs about 102 km from Hanoi through Hung Yen and Hai Duong to Hai Phong. Its operator describes it as built to a Class II standard on flat ground, with four lanes for motor vehicles and two lanes for bicycles and motorbikes, and it carries factory and container traffic from the port. That makes it a legitimate motorbike route, with a dedicated lane in places, but not a relaxing one. It is also a tolled road, so look at the signs at toll stations to see how motorbikes are handled rather than assuming.

### North-east: leaving by the east bank

Riders based at the shop are well placed for the north-east, since one of the plan's routes leaves from Chuong Duong Bridge along Nguyen Van Cu and Ngo Gia Tu to Duong Bridge, avoiding the west bank. Bridge conditions matter, though: Long Bien Bridge and Chuong Duong Bridge have both had restrictions in recent years, and our guide to [Long Bien and the Red River](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/long-bien-red-river-first-ride/) is a good starting point for the local roads.

### West: National Highway 6

Almost all traffic to Hoa Binh and Mai Chau follows Highway 6 through Ha Dong and Xuan Mai, according to one tourism guide, which puts Hoa Binh city at roughly the halfway point. The same guide warns of curves, trucks and fog at the Thung Khe pass. Our [Hanoi to Mai Chau guide](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/hanoi-to-mai-chau-by-motorbike/) covers the road in detail, and our guide to [manual bikes on steep roads](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/manual-bikes-and-steep-mountain-roads-when-to-choose-another-vehicle/) will help you decide whether your bike is right for it.

## Quieter roads beside the main ones

When the national highway is too heavy, three kinds of road often offer a calmer ride, each with its own cost.

1. **River and dyke roads.** The Red River approach to Ninh Binh is the example above. These roads can be pleasant, but they are narrower, surfaces vary, and villages break up the flow.
2. **The Ho Chi Minh road.** Parts of it carry less freight than the old highways and pass through hill country. Duong Lam ancient village, for example, sits beside its junction with National Road 32, according to the national tourism administration.
3. **Provincial roads.** These link towns in a patchwork. They can save a stretch of national highway but are easier to get lost on, so download an offline map first.

None of these is guaranteed to be better. A quiet road can have worse surfaces, fewer fuel stations and slower repair help. Choose the calmer road on a route you can finish by daylight, not on a route where one wrong turn leaves you stranded.

## Plan the extra time

An alternative road is nearly always slower than the expressway beside it, and cars and vans that use the expressway will arrive long before you do. One rider guide suggests an overall average near 40 km/h once junctions, trucks and stops are counted, while a tourism guide's four to five hours including a break for the roughly 140 km to Mai Chau implies nearer 30 km/h. The first 20 or 30 km out of the city are the slowest.

Use these habits:

- **Add 30 to 50 percent to any time a car-based app gives you.** That is a rule of thumb, not a measurement, but it protects against optimism.
- **Break the route into legs,** each ending at a town where you can eat, refuel and rest. Our guides to [planning breaks on a long ride](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/how-to-plan-breaks-on-a-long-semi-automatic-bike-ride/) and [when to refuel on a day trip](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/when-to-refuel-a-manual-motorbike-during-a-day-trip/) explain how.
- **Leave early,** so the slowest section falls in daylight.
- **Check speed limits and lanes.** Our guides to [speed limits](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/speed-limits-motorbikes-vietnam/) and [lane rules](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/lane-rules-motorbikes-vietnam/) apply on these roads.

## How to test an alternative before you commit

Spend ten minutes at the shop, or in your hotel, with the map on your phone:

1. **Draw the route in a two-wheel mode** and zoom out to the whole journey.
2. **Read the road numbers.** QL and DT are ordinary roads; CT means expressway, which you must avoid.
3. **Look for ramps and toll plazas** in the satellite or street view. A ramp that leaves a main road at a shallow angle usually leads to an expressway.
4. **Compare two routes.** Use the less direct one if it keeps you on ordinary roads, even if the app prefers the faster one.
5. **Save three checkpoints** (towns or junctions) so you can recheck your position.
6. **Check recent news** for closures or repairs on bridges along the route.

If any step produces a doubt, ask the shop before you leave, and remember that signs outrank apps. Our guide to [taking a rental motorbike out of the city](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/take-rental-motorbike-out-of-hanoi/) lists what to ask, and the shop's [prices page](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/prices/) shows how rental days are counted.

## When the alternative is still too much

Sometimes the honest answer is that no motorbike-friendly road is easy. A long, truck-heavy highway may be the wrong place for a first trip on a small scooter. In that case consider a shorter destination:

- [Ba Vi National Park](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/hanoi-to-ba-vi-by-motorbike/), about 60 km away.
- [Tam Dao](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/hanoi-to-tam-dao-by-motorbike/), about 70 to 85 km away.
- [Bat Trang and the east bank](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/gia-lam-east-bank-bat-trang-by-scooter/), close to the shop.

Or use a bus or a train for the long leg, and rent a bike at the destination for local riding, as our guide on [riding from Hanoi or renting in Ha Giang](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/hanoi-to-ha-giang-ride-or-rent-there/) suggests.

## FAQ: highway alternatives near Hanoi

### Is the old National Highway 1 allowed for motorbikes?

It is an ordinary national highway, not an expressway, and the Hanoi traffic plan lists it as the alternative to the Phap Van to Cau Gie expressway. Follow signs and any local restrictions.

### Are tolled roads closed to motorbikes?

Not necessarily. A toll and an expressway are different things. Check the signs at the toll station and at ramps.

### Which road should I use to reach Hai Phong?

Old National Highway 5 is the ordinary-road corridor named in the Hanoi plan, and its operator describes lanes for bicycles and motorbikes. Expect heavy truck traffic.

### Can I use Thang Long Avenue to go west?

Only the service roads, since the central carriageway is closed to motorbikes. See our ring-road guide for details.

### How much slower is an alternative road?

Usually much slower than a car on an expressway. Add generous time and plan breaks.

The shop on 0334 699 969 (+84 334 699 969), open 09:00 to 21:00 daily, and the road signs on the day.
