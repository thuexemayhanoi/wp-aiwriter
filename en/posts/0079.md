The way to ride a speed bump on a small manual motorbike is to finish your braking before you reach it, cross it in a straight line at a walking or jogging pace with a steady throttle, and keep your weight balanced and your hands light on the bars. Speed bumps, humps and raised crossings are common on Hanoi's side streets, near schools and on provincial roads, and many of them are poorly marked, worn or painted the same colour as the road. A light bike with narrow tyres and short suspension travel feels every one of them. This guide explains how to approach a bump, what to do with the throttle, brakes and gears, how a passenger or luggage changes things, why night and rain make bumps dangerous, what to check on the bike afterwards and the rule about road signs. It is general riding advice, not a substitute for training.

## What a speed bump does to a small bike

A bump forces the front wheel up, then the rear, and compresses and releases the suspension in quick succession. A light bike has little suspension travel, so a hard hit transmits a jolt to the rider's wrists, back and seat. If you hit it with the front brake still applied, the front fork compresses and the rider is thrown forward. If you hit it with too much throttle, the rear wheel can leave the ground for a moment and land hard. If you hit it at an angle, the wheel can slip sideways. None of this is dramatic at low speed, but it hurts at higher speed and can damage the bike.

Hanoi adds a few extra risks. Bumps are often unmarked, cut across the road with uneven edges or built from patched asphalt that has worn into ruts. A bump can sit just after a bend or beyond the crest of a rise. ## Slow down before, not on, the bump

Honda's safe-riding training document sets out the braking sequence: release the throttle, use both brakes together with the front stronger than the rear, keep the steering straight and build the braking gradually. Apply that before the bump, in a straight line, and finish before you reach it. Do not brake hard on the bump itself, because the front fork is already compressed and the wheel can lose grip.

A good approach has three steps:

1. **See it early.** Look well ahead. Signs, painted stripes, a car ahead rocking over something and a line of slowing bikes are all clues.
2. **Slow in a straight line.** Ease off the throttle, brake lightly with both brakes and change down if needed. A gentle slowdown leaves the bike balanced.
3. **Release the brakes as the front wheel meets the bump.** A light, steady throttle then carries the bike across.

If the bump is very tall or steep, slow almost to a walking pace. A bike crossing a hump at 5 or 10 km/h feels a mild lift. At 30 km/h it feels a hard jolt.

## Cross it square

Cross a bump at right angles, with the bike upright and the handlebars straight. A wheel that meets the edge of the bump at an angle can be deflected sideways, and on a wet or dusty surface the tyre can slip. If the bump runs across the whole road, cross where it is lowest or has a gap, but only if you can do so safely without leaving your lane. Do not weave to the side to avoid a bump in traffic.

Keep your head up and your eyes beyond the bump. Looking down at the hump is a good way to steer into it. Keep your arms relaxed, with a slight bend at the elbow, so that the bars can move a little without transmitting the shock to your shoulders.

## Throttle and gears

At the right speed, the bike should be in a gear that lets you keep a very light, steady throttle across the bump, not a sudden open or shut. In most cases that means first or second gear. A closed throttle at the moment of impact brings sudden engine braking and can unsettle the rear wheel, and a fully open throttle can launch the rear wheel into the air. Honda's Wave manual says to reduce the throttle before changing gear and to press the lever gently, so make any gear change before the bump, not on it.

After the bump, roll the throttle on gently to resume speed. If the road has several bumps in a row, stay in the same low gear and keep the pace steady. ## Your body position

Sit upright with your knees against the tank and your feet firmly on the footrests. Keep your weight centred, not on the handlebars. Some riders rise slightly off the seat to let the legs absorb the shock, but on a small bike with a flat seat and footrests this is not necessary at low speed and can reduce your control. If you do lift, keep your knees bent, your feet planted and your grip on the bars soft. Avoid leaning forward, which loads the front wheel at the wrong moment.

Do not take your feet off the footrests unless you are about to stop. A foot dangling near a bump can catch on the surface.

## Passengers and luggage

A passenger and luggage change how the bike takes a bump. The extra weight sits over the rear wheel, which compresses the rear suspension more and can bottom it out. A passenger who is not expecting the bump may slide forward or backwards. Brief them before you ride: tell them to keep their feet on the footrests, hold the grab rail or your waist and brace when you say "bump". Slow down more than you would alone.

Honda's Wave manual says to tie all luggage down tightly, balanced and as close to the middle of the bike as possible, and warns that overloading affects handling, braking and stability. A loose bag can fly off or shift on a bump, so check the straps first. ## Wet and dark conditions

In the rain, painted stripes and the polished top of a bump are slippery. Slow earlier, keep the bike upright and avoid braking on the bump. Honda's manual says wet roads are very slippery and wet brakes lose effect, so give yourself extra distance. Our guides to [riding in Hanoi rain](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/riding-in-hanoi-rain/) and [riding a semi-automatic bike in heavy Hanoi rain](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/riding-a-semi-automatic-bike-in-heavy-hanoi-rain/) explain the margins.

At night, a bump can be almost invisible until you are on it. Honda's night-riding advice is to make sure the lights work, ride slowly, keep a larger gap from other vehicles, avoid looking at oncoming headlights and use the high beam when appropriate, switching to dipped when vehicles or pedestrians approach or in urban areas. If you cannot see far ahead, reduce speed to what the visible road allows.

## Signs and the law

Warning signs and painted markings near a bump are there to be obeyed. Under Article 7 of Decree 168/2024/ND-CP, which we read on the Government portal, a rider who does not follow a traffic sign or road marking is fined VND 200,000–400,000 as of October 2026, and a rider who rides at a speed above the limit faces higher fines. Decree 238/2026/ND-CP, which amends the decree from 15 August 2026, does not change Article 7. Our guide to [speed limits for motorbikes in Vietnam](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/speed-limits-motorbikes-vietnam/) explains the speed rules. A speed bump is a sign that the road is meant to be taken slowly, usually because there are people nearby.

## Check the bike afterwards

A hard hit can damage a small bike. After any bump you took too fast, and certainly after any that jarred you, stop in a safe place and check:

- **Tyres.** Honda's aftersales advice says to inspect your tyres after riding over large bumps or hitting a kerb, looking for cuts, bulges and objects. Our guide to [choosing tyres for a manual bike rental in wet weather](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/choosing-tyres-for-a-manual-bike-rental-in-wet-weather/) explains what to look for.
- **Wheels.** Look for a bent rim or a wobble when you spin the wheel.
- **Handlebars and fork.** The bars should be straight and the fork should move smoothly.
- **Luggage and straps.** Check they are still tight.
- **Chain and sprocket cover.** Make sure nothing has come loose. Our guide to [checking chain tension before a longer ride](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/checking-chain-tension-before-a-longer-ride/) explains what to look for.

If you notice damage or a new noise, tell the shop on 0334 699 969 (+84 334 699 969), open 09:00 to 21:00 daily. Our guide to [returning your rental motorbike in Hanoi](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/returning-your-rental-motorbike-hanoi/) explains how damage is assessed at return.

## Common mistakes

- **Braking on the bump.** Finish braking before it.
- **Hitting it at an angle.** Cross square.
- **Looking at the bump instead of beyond it.**
- **Snapping the throttle shut or open.**
- **Changing gear on the bump.**
- **Carrying loose luggage.**
- **Ignoring a passenger.** Brief them first.
- **Following a car too closely.** A car's brake lights may be the first sign of a hump.

## A quick practice

Find a quiet street with a known speed hump and no traffic. Approach in second gear at a modest speed, ease the throttle, brake lightly with both brakes, release as the front wheel reaches the bump and roll over with a steady throttle. Repeat at a slightly lower speed and then slightly higher, always within a safe range. Notice how the bike behaves. Prices are on the [prices page](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/prices/).

## FAQ: speed bumps on a manual bike

### Should I brake on a speed bump?

No. Finish braking before the bump, in a straight line, then release the brakes as the front wheel meets it.

### Which gear should I be in?

Usually first or second, so you can keep a light, steady throttle across the bump.

### Is it safe to take a bump with a passenger?

Yes, if you slow more than you would alone and brief them to keep their feet on the footrests and hold on.

### What if I cannot see the bump at night?

Reduce speed to what the visible road allows and use dipped or high beam as appropriate, as Honda's night-riding advice says.

### Should I check the bike after hitting a bump hard?

Yes. Stop and look at the tyres, wheels, bars and luggage. Tell the shop about any damage.

### Is there a fine for ignoring a warning sign?

As of October 2026, not following a traffic sign or road marking is fined VND 200,000–400,000 under Decree 168/2024/ND-CP.
