For most visitors riding inside Hanoi, the bigger engine does not matter much, but the licence it requires matters a great deal. The Honda Air Blade 160 and the Air Blade 125 share almost the same body, seat and storage, and in city traffic you will rarely use the extra power. The real difference sits on paper: under Vietnam's licence classes, a two-wheeler up to 125 cc falls in class A1, while anything above 125 cc needs class A. If your licence or permit only covers the small class, the 160 is the wrong bike for you, however tempting it looks.

This guide is narrower than our [Honda Vision vs Air Blade comparison](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/honda-vision-vs-air-blade-which-scooter-to-rent-in-hanoi/). That article helps you choose between a small and a mid-size scooter. This one assumes you have already chosen the Air Blade family and need to decide which engine to ask for, what that choice means legally, and when the 160 genuinely earns its place. Rules and model details are as of October 2026.

## Same scooter, two engines: what actually changes

Honda Vietnam sells the Air Blade as a single model line with two engine options, listed on its site as the Air Blade 160/125. The frame, bodywork, under-seat box and riding position are shared, so from the outside the two versions are hard to tell apart. Badges on the side panels and the trim level are usually the giveaway.

The changes are mechanical. Third-party specification sheets put the larger engine at about 157 cc and the smaller one at just under 125 cc, both single-cylinder, liquid-cooled and fuel-injected, with four valves on recent generations. The 160 produces noticeably more power and torque, which shows up as quicker acceleration from a standstill and a more relaxed feel above city speeds. Equipment also differs by version: Honda states that front-wheel ABS comes on the 160, while on the 125 it is limited to the sport version, with other 125 trims using combined braking instead.

Honda updates the line regularly, including a refreshed 2026 range announced alongside new SH and Lead models, so any rental fleet will contain a mix of model years. Treat every figure here as a guide to the family, not a promise about the bike in front of you.

## The licence line at 125 cc is the deciding factor

The Law on Road Traffic Order and Safety, No. 36/2024/QH15, effective from 1 January 2025, reorganised Vietnam's driving licence classes. Article 57 defines class A1 as covering two-wheeled motorcycles with a cylinder capacity up to 125 cc (or an electric motor up to 11 kW). Class A covers two-wheeled motorcycles above 125 cc (or above 11 kW) and also everything in class A1.

That places the Air Blade 125 in A1 and the Air Blade 160 in A. The boundary is drawn by cylinder capacity, so it does not matter that both bikes look identical or that a police officer at a checkpoint might not notice the badge at first glance. What counts is the registered capacity on the vehicle papers.

For a foreign visitor, the practical question is what your own documents cover. A licence and an International Driving Permit issued under the 1968 Vienna Convention can allow you to ride in Vietnam, but only within the motorcycle category that the permit actually shows. Some permits and home licences restrict holders to smaller machines; others cover motorcycles of any size. Our full guide to [licence rules for foreigners in Vietnam](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/motorbike-licence-rules-for-foreigners-in-vietnam/) explains which permits are recognised and why a 1949-convention permit is a problem.

## Older Vietnamese licences and the 175 cc exception

You may read online that "the old A1 covers up to 175 cc". That is partly true and causes a lot of confusion. Under the transition rules of the 2024 law, a Vietnamese A1 licence issued before 1 January 2025 stays valid. When it is exchanged or reissued, it becomes a class A licence with a restriction: the holder may ride two-wheelers only up to under 175 cc. The old A2 converts to class A without that limit.

This matters mainly to residents and long-stay expats who already hold a Vietnamese licence. If that is you, an old-style A1 still lets you ride the Air Blade 160. If you are a tourist relying on a foreign licence and permit, the exception does not help you: your entitlement is whatever your home documents and permit cover, matched against the new A1 and A classes.

When in doubt, ask for the 125. It removes the question entirely for anyone whose papers clearly cover small motorcycles.

## What riding the wrong class can cost you

Riding a motorcycle that your licence does not cover is treated much like riding without a licence for that class. Under Decree 168/2024/ND-CP, effective from 1 January 2025, the fine band for riding without a valid licence is set higher for two-wheelers above 125 cc than for those at or below 125 cc, so the 160 carries the heavier penalty. Decree 238/2026/ND-CP has since amended parts of Decree 168, in force from 15 August 2026, so check the current figures in our guide to [traffic fines that catch foreign riders out](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/traffic-fines-vietnam-foreign-riders/) rather than relying on an old forum post.

The fine is not the biggest risk. Travel insurance policies commonly exclude claims when the rider was not properly licensed for the vehicle, and a crash on a bike your documents do not cover can leave you paying medical and repair costs yourself. That consequence lasts much longer than a roadside penalty, and it is the strongest argument for matching the engine to your paperwork.

## Does the extra power help in Hanoi traffic?

Inside the ring roads, rarely. Hanoi traffic moves in dense, slow streams. You spend most of the day below moderate speeds, filtering past cars, waiting at lights and turning across other riders. In those conditions the 125 accelerates briskly enough to clear a junction, and the extra torque of the 160 is mostly felt for a second or two when the light turns green.

Where the 160 does help is on wide arterial roads and bridge approaches when you need to merge or overtake a slow truck, and when you carry a passenger. With two adults on board, the bigger engine pulls more willingly from low speed and on ramps. Some riders also find the 160 calmer at a steady cruising speed because the engine is working less hard.

What does not change is handling. The two versions share the same size and seat height, so the weight you feel when wheeling the bike out of a narrow alley or lifting it onto the centre stand is essentially the same. If the Air Blade body already feels large to you, the smaller engine will not make it feel any lighter, and the [Vision comparison](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/honda-vision-vs-air-blade-which-scooter-to-rent-in-hanoi/) is the better place to start.

## Fuel use and range: closer than you might expect

Honda's published consumption figures put both engines close to each other, at a little over two litres per 100 km under standard test conditions, with the 160 slightly thirstier than the 125. Honda's own pages show small differences between model years and trims, and real-world consumption in stop-and-go Hanoi traffic will be higher than the test figure for either version.

Both versions share the same small tank, roughly four and a half litres according to published specifications, so range is similar too. In practice the difference in fuel spend over a week of city riding is minor and should not drive your decision. Read [our fuel guide](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/fuel-in-hanoi-petrol-stations-rental-scooter/) for how staffed petrol stations work.

## Braking, ABS and why trim matters more than engine

Because ABS availability varies across the line, braking equipment can differ more between two Air Blade rentals than engine power does. Honda fits front-wheel ABS on the 160 and on the sport version of the 125, while other 125 trims use a combined braking system that applies some rear brake force when you squeeze the left lever. Older fleet bikes may predate these updates.

ABS does not shorten every stop, but it helps keep the front wheel turning on wet paint, manhole covers and sudden rain, all of which are common in Hanoi. If ABS matters to you, ask for it specifically when you book instead of assuming the bigger engine comes with it.

At handover, test both brakes at walking pace, look at the tyre tread and check the lights and horn. Our [10-minute inspection checklist](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/rental-scooter-inspection-checklist/) covers what to look for and what to photograph.

## Day trips out of Hanoi: where the 160 makes sense

The case for the 160 grows once you leave the city. On national and provincial roads you hold higher speeds for longer, overtake more often and climb real gradients. A confident rider with the right licence, a passenger and luggage will find the 160 less strained on those stretches.

Keep two limits in mind whichever engine you choose. Motorbikes are not allowed on Vietnam's expressways, so the route is set by the national roads regardless of power; our guide to [checking whether a navigation app suggests an expressway](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/how-to-check-if-a-navigation-app-suggests-an-expressway/) explains how to avoid that mistake. And taking a rental beyond the city needs the shop's agreement first, as covered in [can you take a Hanoi rental out of the city](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/take-rental-motorbike-out-of-hanoi/).

For a single relaxed day to a nearby destination, the 125 is perfectly adequate. The 160 is a comfort upgrade for longer, faster, two-up riding, not a requirement.

## Which version to ask for: a simple decision guide

**Choose the Air Blade 125 if** your licence or permit covers only small motorcycles, or you are unsure exactly what it covers; you will ride mainly inside Hanoi; you ride alone or with a light passenger; or you simply want the paperwork question closed.

**Choose the Air Blade 160 if** your documents clearly cover motorcycles above 125 cc; you will often ride two-up; you plan several day trips on national roads; and you want ABS without having to hunt for a specific 125 trim.

**Consider a different bike if** the Air Blade's size already feels like too much in a narrow lane, or you want to carry a passenger and luggage in a more upright, roomy layout. ## FAQ: Air Blade 160 vs 125 for renters

### Can I ride the Air Blade 160 with an A1 licence?

Under Law 36/2024/QH15, a new class A1 licence covers two-wheelers only up to 125 cc, so it does not cover the 160. The exception is a Vietnamese A1 licence issued before 1 January 2025, which converts to a restricted class A allowing bikes under 175 cc.

### My International Driving Permit has a motorcycle stamp. Is that enough for the 160?

Only if the permit and your home licence cover motorcycles above 125 cc and the permit was issued under the 1968 convention. Some categories are limited to small machines. Check the exact wording before choosing the 160.

### Is the Air Blade 160 much faster in the city?

Not in a way most riders notice. In dense Hanoi traffic both versions keep up comfortably; the 160 feels stronger mainly when merging, overtaking or carrying a passenger.

### Does the 160 use a lot more petrol?

No. Honda's published figures show only a small difference between the two engines, and the fuel tank is the same size, so range and weekly fuel costs are similar.

### Can I tell the two versions apart before riding?

Look for the engine badge on the side panels and ask to see the capacity on the registration papers. Since both share the same body, the papers are the reliable check.

### Can I reserve a specific engine size?

Ask when you book. The shop's model list names the Air Blade without an engine size, and availability of a particular version depends on the fleet at the time.
