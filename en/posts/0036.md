Travel insurance and a rental motorbike in Vietnam can work together, but far less automatically than most visitors assume. Many policies will pay for a scooter accident only when a short list of conditions is met: you held a licence valid in Vietnam for that bike, the engine was within the insurer's own size limit, you wore a helmet, you had not been drinking, and you were riding as an ordinary tourist rather than racing or working. Break one condition and a claim can be refused in full, even if the crash was not your fault. Separately, the compulsory motorbike insurance that every Vietnamese bike carries protects other people, not you or the rental bike. This guide explains the usual gaps, how to read your policy before you ride, and what to keep if you ever need to claim.

## Two kinds of cover that are easy to confuse

A rider in Vietnam is usually dealing with two completely different insurance products, and mixing them up is the root of most unpleasant surprises.

The first is the compulsory civil liability insurance attached to the motorbike. Vietnamese law requires the owner of every motor vehicle, scooters and mopeds included, to carry it, and the rental shop buys it in the bike's name. Its job is to compensate third parties: a pedestrian you hit, another rider, damage to someone else's property.

The second is your own travel insurance, bought at home. Its job is to pay for your medical treatment, a possible evacuation or repatriation, and sometimes your personal liability. It is a private contract, and the insurer decides what it will and will not pay for in the policy wording.

Neither product is a damage waiver for the rental bike. Repairs to the scooter itself are governed by your rental contract, which is why the [motorbike rental deposit](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/motorbike-rental-deposit-hanoi/) exists. Keep those three things, third-party insurance, personal travel cover and the rental contract, in separate boxes in your head.

## What the compulsory motorbike insurance in Vietnam actually does

The current rules come from Decree 67/2023/ND-CP on compulsory insurance, issued and effective on 6 September 2023. As of October 2026, it has been amended by Decree 220/2026/ND-CP, in force from 1 July 2026; the published summaries we found describe that amendment in general terms, so check the consolidated text if you need the details.

Under Decree 67, the insurer pays for death or injury caused to third parties by the vehicle and for damage to third parties' property, with a fixed maximum per person for injury and a separate, lower maximum per accident for property damaged by a two-wheeler. Legal summaries also note that the cover extends to the health and life of passengers carried on the insured vehicle. The caps are modest compared with the cost of serious hospital treatment, and they are designed around compensation to victims, not around a foreign rider's own medical bills.

What this cover does not do is just as important:

- It does not pay for your injuries as the rider.
- It does not pay to repair or replace the rental bike.
- It does not cover theft of the bike or your belongings.
- It does not replace emergency evacuation or flights home.

The rider must be able to show a valid insurance certificate at a police stop. Under Decree 168/2024/ND-CP, effective from 1 January 2025, riding a motorbike without a valid compulsory insurance certificate carries a fine of VND 200,000–300,000. At a reputable shop the certificate travels with the bike; check it is there during your [pre-ride inspection](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/rental-scooter-inspection-checklist/).

## Exclusion one: a licence that is not valid for that bike

This is the gap that ends more claims than any other. Most travel policies cover motorcycling only if the rider holds a licence that is valid in the country where the accident happens and that covers the type of vehicle ridden. The insurer is not asking whether the shop rented you the bike; it is asking whether you were legally entitled to ride it in Vietnam.

For most visitors that means a full motorcycle licence from home plus an International Driving Permit issued under the 1968 Vienna Convention, with the motorcycle category stamped. A car licence, a learner licence, or a 1949-convention permit typically does not satisfy that condition. Australia's official Smartraveller advice, for example, tells Australian travellers that their home licence and the permit issued in Australia are not accepted, and that a Vietnamese licence is needed for a bike of 50cc or above.

We cover the legal side in detail in our guide to [licence rules for foreigners in Vietnam](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/motorbike-licence-rules-for-foreigners-in-vietnam/). For insurance purposes, the practical takeaway is simple: if the police would treat you as unlicensed, assume your insurer will too.

Small mopeds add a twist. Vietnamese law lets riders use a vehicle under 50cc without a licence, but a travel policy may still demand a motorcycle licence regardless of engine size. Whether a [50cc scooter](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/renting-a-50cc-scooter-in-hanoi/) is covered depends on your policy's wording, not on Vietnamese law alone.

## Exclusion two: engine size limits in the policy wording

Many insurers apply their own engine size limit to motorcycling, separate from what your licence allows. A policy might cover small scooters as standard and treat larger bikes as an optional extra, or not cover them at all. Some limits are stated in cubic centimetres; others use phrases such as "mopeds and scooters" without a number, which leaves room for argument after an accident.

Before you choose a bike, find the exact limit and compare it with the engine size written on the bike's registration, not the badge on the bodywork. A Honda Vision, Lead or Air Blade sits in the scooter class, but a semi-automatic or manual bike may still fall inside or outside a limit depending on the model. If the policy is vague, ask the insurer in writing which engine sizes are covered and keep the reply.

Electric scooters are worth a separate question. Some policies treat them like mopeds, some say nothing, and a few exclude them. Do not assume an electric bike is outside the motorcycling clause just because it has no petrol engine.

## Exclusion three: helmets and what counts as wearing one

Helmets are compulsory for riders and passengers on Vietnamese roads, and many travel policies make helmet use a condition of cover. Even where the wording does not say so explicitly, a head injury suffered without a helmet is a strong reason for an insurer to reduce or decline a claim.

Wearing a helmet means wearing it properly: on your head, strap fastened. A helmet hanging from the mirror does not count, and neither, in most insurers' eyes, does a novelty or obviously sub-standard shell. Read our [helmet rules in Vietnam](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/helmet-rules-in-vietnam/) for what the law requires.

At Nguyen Ha, each rider receives one basic helmet free with the bike. That meets the legal requirement for city riding. If your policy refers to a recognised safety standard, or if you are planning longer rides, consider bringing or buying a better helmet and keep proof of it.

## Exclusion four: alcohol and drugs

Almost every travel policy excludes claims arising while the insured person was under the influence of alcohol or drugs. Many link this to the legal limit of the country concerned, and for motorbike riders in Vietnam the legal limit is zero. That means a single drink with dinner can be enough to put a claim at risk if a blood test is taken after a crash.

Hospitals and police may record alcohol levels after a road accident, and that record will usually reach your insurer when you claim. The safe approach is to treat riding and drinking as completely separate parts of the day. Our article on [drink-driving rules for motorbike riders](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/drink-driving-rules-motorbike-vietnam/) explains the testing and penalties.

## Passengers, pillion riding and motorbike taxis

Pillion passengers are often treated differently from riders, and the differences cut both ways.

If you sit on the back of a bike ridden by a friend, your own policy may cover you as a passenger, but only if the rider was licensed, sober and wearing a helmet, and you were wearing one too. Some policies exclude passengers when the rider is not properly licensed, even though the passenger did nothing wrong.

If you carry a passenger yourself, check whether your policy covers liability to them. As noted above, the compulsory insurance on the bike is described as extending to passengers' health and life, but that cover is capped and comes with its own claim process. Your passenger's own travel insurance is a separate question they should answer before getting on.

Motorbike taxis, booked through an app or hailed on the street, are generally treated as public transport by many policies, but some wording requires the operator to be licensed and the passenger to wear a helmet. Read the clause rather than assuming a ride-hailing trip is automatically covered.

## The rental bike itself: damage, theft and the deposit

Most travel policies do not cover damage to or theft of a hired motorbike. A few offer a limited "rental vehicle excess" benefit, and that is often written for cars only. If your policy mentions rental vehicles, check whether two-wheelers are included before you rely on it.

In practice, the bike is governed by your rental contract. At Nguyen Ha the cash deposit, between VND 2,000,000 and VND 5,000,000 depending on the bike, is held against damage, loss and unpaid charges and is separate from the rent. Your documents are checked and photographed for the contract; originals are not kept. Read the [rental guide](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/rental-guide/) for how repairs and returns are handled, and remember that a deposit is not an insurance policy and does not set a ceiling on liability unless the written terms say so.

Theft deserves its own caution. Many policies exclude belongings left unattended or stored on a parked vehicle, so a bag stolen from under the seat may not be covered either.

## Other wording that quietly narrows cover

Beyond the big four exclusions, look for clauses that reduce cover without using the word "motorbike" at all:

- **Reckless or illegal acts.** Riding on the wrong side, through red lights or on an expressway, where motorbikes have been barred since 1 January 2025, can be described as a deliberate breach of the law.
- **Off-road and racing.** Dirt trails, river crossings and organised events are often excluded or need an add-on.
- **Remote areas and long trips.** Some policies limit evacuation from remote mountain areas or require pre-approval for treatment.
- **Excess.** Even when a claim is paid, the first part of each claim is usually yours.
- **Pre-approval for hospital costs.** Many insurers expect you or the hospital to contact their emergency assistance line before major treatment.
- **Working while riding.** Delivering goods or carrying paying passengers is outside a tourist policy.

None of these is universal, which is exactly why reading your own wording matters more than reading general advice.

## How to read a policy before you ride: a checklist

Download the full policy wording, not just the summary, and search it for "motorcycle", "moped", "scooter", "licence" and "helmet". Then answer each of these questions in writing:

1. Is motorcycling covered as standard, or does it need an add-on?
2. What licence must I hold, and must it be valid in the country of the accident?
3. Is there an engine size limit, and how is it stated?
4. Are passengers covered, and on what conditions?
5. Is a helmet required, and does it need to meet a particular standard?
6. What does the policy say about alcohol and legal limits?
7. Are electric scooters treated as motorcycles?
8. Is there any cover for damage to or theft of a rented vehicle?
9. What is the excess, and when must I call the emergency assistance line?

If any answer is unclear, email the insurer and keep the reply with your travel documents. A written answer before the trip is worth far more than an argument after it. If you plan a trip beyond the city, our article on whether you can [take a rental motorbike out of Hanoi](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/take-rental-motorbike-out-of-hanoi/) lists the questions to ask the shop as well.

## If something happens: keeping a claim on solid ground

After any accident, safety comes first: get injured people medical help and keep everyone away from traffic. Then start collecting what your insurer will ask for.

- Contact your insurer's emergency assistance line as soon as you can, especially before major treatment or a transfer between hospitals.
- Keep every medical report, receipt and discharge summary.
- If police attend, ask for any written record and note the officer's unit and the location.
- Photograph the scene, the bike, your helmet and the road.
- Keep copies of your licence, IDP, passport, the bike's registration and its insurance certificate.
- Phone the rental shop on 0334 699 969 and keep the rental contract.

Be honest with the insurer about what happened. A claim that later turns out to omit a missing licence or a drink is far more likely to be refused outright than one that is declared from the start. For the police side of a stop, see our guide to [traffic fines that catch foreign riders out](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/traffic-fines-vietnam-foreign-riders/).

## FAQ: travel insurance and rental motorbikes in Vietnam

### Does my travel insurance automatically cover riding a scooter in Vietnam?

Not automatically. Many policies cover motorcycling only with conditions such as a valid licence, a helmet and an engine size limit, and some need an add-on. Check the wording before you ride.

### Does the rental shop's insurance cover my hospital bills?

Generally not. The compulsory insurance on the bike is civil liability cover aimed at third parties. Your own medical costs as the rider come from your travel insurance.

### Will I be covered on a 50cc moped without a motorcycle licence?

Vietnamese law allows riding under 50cc without a licence, but your insurer may still require one. It depends entirely on your policy wording, so ask in writing.

### Is the rental bike covered if it is damaged or stolen?

Usually not by travel insurance. Damage and theft are handled under the rental contract and the deposit, unless your policy clearly includes hired two-wheelers.

### Am I covered as a passenger on a friend's motorbike?

Possibly, but many policies require the rider to be licensed and sober and both of you to wear helmets. Some exclude passengers when the rider is unlicensed.

### Does one beer really affect my insurance?

It can. The legal alcohol limit for motorbike riders in Vietnam is zero, and many policies exclude claims when you were over the legal limit. Do not ride after drinking.
