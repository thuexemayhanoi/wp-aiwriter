Vietnam sets motorbike speed limits by road type rather than by a single national number. As of October 2026, under Circular 38/2024/TT-BGTVT (effective from 1 January 2025), a motorcycle above 50cc may ride at up to 50 km/h on an undivided two-way road inside a residential area and 60 km/h on a dual carriageway or a one-way road with two or more motor lanes there. Outside residential areas the caps rise to 60 km/h and 70 km/h on the same two road types. Mopeds, the legal class that covers 50cc bikes and most low-powered electric scooters, are capped at 40 km/h everywhere. Motorbikes of any size are not allowed on expressways at all. Posted signs always come first, and in Hanoi they are often lower than the general limit. This guide explains each rule, how to read the signs and what the penalties look like.

## The short version for riders in a hurry

If you remember only four things, make them these. First, a speed sign beats the general rule: whatever number is on the red circle is the limit until a sign tells you otherwise. Second, inside towns and cities the general maximum for a normal rental motorbike is 50 or 60 km/h depending on whether the road is divided. Third, once you pass the "end of residential area" sign on an open road, the general maximum becomes 60 or 70 km/h. Fourth, a 50cc bike or a moped-class electric scooter must never exceed 40 km/h, on any road.

In practice, real traffic in Hanoi rarely lets you approach even the lower figures. Most city journeys happen at 25 to 40 km/h, and the speed limit matters most on ring roads, bridge approaches, national highways and the long straight stretches outside the city where cameras and police checkpoints are common.

## Motorcycle or moped: why the class of your bike matters

Vietnamese traffic law draws a sharp line between two classes of two-wheeler. A motorcycle is a two-wheeled motor vehicle with an engine larger than 50cc or an electric motor above the moped threshold. Every typical rental bike in Hanoi, from the Honda Wave and Yamaha Sirius to the Honda Vision, Air Blade and Lead, is a motorcycle in this sense, so the higher limits in this guide apply to it.

A moped is the lighter class: a petrol engine of 50cc or less, or a low-powered electric motor, with a design speed of no more than 50 km/h. Many 50cc scooters and 50cc-equivalent electric scooters fall into this group. The licence rules differ too, which is explained in our guide to [renting a 50cc scooter in Hanoi](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/renting-a-50cc-scooter-in-hanoi/). If you are not sure which class your bike belongs to, ask at handover and look at the registration document; the answer decides whether your ceiling is 40 km/h or something higher.

## Speed limits inside residential areas

A "residential area" in the legal sense is not simply anywhere with houses. It is the stretch of road that begins at a "start of residential area" sign and ends at an "end of residential area" sign. In practice, almost all of central Hanoi, the suburbs and most town centres along national highways sit inside such zones.

Within them, Circular 38/2024/TT-BGTVT sets these general maximums for motorcycles:

| Road type inside a residential area | Motorcycle (over 50cc) | Moped (50cc or low-power electric) |
|---|---|---|
| Dual carriageway, or one-way road with two or more motor lanes | 60 km/h | 40 km/h |
| Two-way road without a central divider, or one-way road with one motor lane | 50 km/h | 40 km/h |

A dual carriageway here means a road where traffic in each direction is separated by a central reservation, barrier or similar physical divider, not just a painted line. Most Old Quarter streets, residential lanes and many inner-city avenues fall into the slower group. Wide avenues with a planted median, such as parts of the ring roads at ground level, may fall into the faster group, but many of them carry their own signs with a lower number.

## Speed limits outside residential areas

Once you pass an "end of residential area" sign, the general limits rise. For a motorcycle the circular sets 70 km/h on dual carriageways and one-way roads with two or more motor lanes, and 60 km/h on undivided two-way roads and single-lane one-way roads. Mopeds stay at 40 km/h.

These higher figures apply to the open stretches of national highways and provincial roads between towns, for example parts of the routes towards Ninh Binh or into the northern hills. They are ceilings, not targets. Rural roads often carry trucks, buffalo, children on bicycles, drying rice on the shoulder and sudden potholes. On mountain passes, steep bends and narrow village roads, a safe speed is usually well below the legal limit, and the law separately expects riders to slow down where conditions demand it.

Be aware that the residential zone can start again with little warning. A single highway can pass through a dozen small towns between Hanoi and your destination, and each one brings back the lower urban limits. Watching for the residential-area signs is the simplest way to know which rule applies. ## Why motorbikes cannot use expressways

Vietnam's expressways are closed to motorcycles and mopeds. The Law on Road Traffic Order and Safety (Law 36/2024/QH15), in force since 1 January 2025, keeps expressways for cars, buses and trucks, and entrances are marked with signs banning two-wheelers. This means there is no "expressway speed limit" for a motorbike, because a motorbike has no legal right to be there at any speed.

Around Hanoi this matters on several major routes, including the expressways heading south towards Ninh Binh, east towards Hai Phong and north-west towards Lao Cai, as well as elevated sections of the ring roads where motorbikes must use the road below. Riding onto an expressway is a serious offence, it is extremely dangerous because traffic is moving far faster than a scooter can, and it is one of the violations most likely to bring a police stop on a long-distance trip.

The most common way visitors end up near an expressway ramp is by following a navigation app set to car mode. ## Reading speed signs on Vietnamese roads

Signs follow the international pattern, so most visitors recognise them quickly:

- **Maximum speed sign:** a white circle with a red border and a black number. This is the limit from that point until another speed sign, an end-of-limit sign, or the next junction where a new rule applies.
- **End of maximum speed:** a white circle with the number crossed out by diagonal lines. After it, the general limit for that road type applies again.
- **Minimum speed sign:** a blue circle with a white number. This is rare on roads open to motorbikes, but if you see one you must not ride below it except when traffic forces you to.
- **Start and end of residential area:** rectangular blue signs, often with a silhouette of buildings. The end sign shows the same picture crossed through.
- **Lane-specific limits:** on some wide roads a sign above or beside the carriageway gives different limits for each lane or each vehicle type. Motorbikes usually belong in the rightmost lanes, and the limit for those lanes applies.

Signs can also carry a small plate underneath that limits them to certain vehicles or times. If a plate shows a car or truck only, the number does not apply to you, but the general limit for your road type still does.

## Hanoi in practice: where the limits actually matter

Inside the Old Quarter and around Hoan Kiem Lake, the limit is almost academic: dense traffic, pedestrians and parked bikes keep everyone slow. The places where riders are actually caught for speeding tend to be the wide, fast-looking roads that tempt you to open the throttle.

Ring roads at ground level, the approaches to the Red River bridges, long avenues leading out to the suburbs and the stretches of national highway leaving the city are where cameras and patrols concentrate. Some of these roads have lower posted limits than the general 50 or 60 km/h, and some have separate lanes where motorbikes must stay. On the bridges themselves, motorbikes are often confined to specific lanes or decks, and the posted limits there can be low.

Night riding adds another layer. Empty roads at midnight invite speed, but visibility, unlit obstacles and other riders who have been drinking make this the worst time to push it. ## Fines for speeding on a motorbike

Penalties come from Decree 168/2024/ND-CP, which took effect on 1 January 2025. Decree 238/2026/ND-CP, in force from 15 August 2026, amended parts of Decree 168, but the published guidance on it describes mainly wording changes to align with the new traffic law, and we found no report of a change to the motorbike speeding bands. For a motorcycle or moped rider, Article 7 of Decree 168 sets these fines:

- **5 km/h to under 10 km/h over the limit:** a fine of VND 400,000–600,000.
- **10 km/h to 20 km/h over the limit:** a fine of VND 800,000–1,000,000.
- **More than 20 km/h over the limit:** a fine of VND 6–8 million, and published guidance indicates a deduction of points from the driving licence as well.

Speeding that causes a crash is punished more heavily, and serious cases can move from administrative fines into criminal liability. Licence points are a newer feature of the system; how they apply to visitors is explained in our overview of [traffic fines that catch foreign riders out](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/traffic-fines-vietnam-foreign-riders/), which covers the full range of common offences rather than speeding alone.

Being under 5 km/h over the posted limit is not an offence under these bands, but do not treat that margin as a cushion. Speedometers on small scooters are rarely precise, and the camera reading is what counts.

## Cameras, checkpoints and fines after the trip

Hanoi and the national highway network use fixed and mobile speed cameras, and traffic police also run roadside checks with handheld radar. A camera violation is sent to the registered owner of the bike, which for a rental is the shop. The rental contract records who had the bike at the time, so a fine can follow you even after you have returned it.

Keep your contract and note your handover times. ## Riding at a safe speed, not just a legal one

The legal limit is the most you may ride, not the speed you should ride. Vietnamese law also requires riders to slow down, and if necessary stop, in conditions such as narrow roads, bends, junctions, school gates, markets, bad weather, poor visibility and road works.

A few habits keep you well inside the limits without much effort:

- Ride with the flow of scooters around you rather than weaving past them.
- Slow down well before junctions, because cross traffic in Vietnam rarely stops completely.
- On wet roads, add distance and reduce speed; braking distances grow sharply in rain.
- On rural highways, expect slow vehicles, animals and people on the shoulder around every bend.
- With a passenger or luggage, brake earlier and corner more gently.

## FAQ: motorbike speed limits in Vietnam

### What is the speed limit for a scooter in Hanoi?

For a normal rental scooter above 50cc, the general limit inside residential areas is 50 km/h on undivided two-way roads and 60 km/h on dual carriageways, unless a sign shows a lower number. Most of Hanoi is inside residential zones, and traffic is usually slower than the limit anyway.

### Is the limit different for 50cc bikes and electric scooters?

Yes. Mopeds, which include 50cc petrol scooters and most low-powered electric scooters, are capped at 40 km/h on every road. Our guide to [renting an electric scooter in Hanoi](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/renting-an-electric-scooter-in-hanoi/) explains range and charging.

### Can I ride a motorbike on an expressway in Vietnam?

No. Motorcycles and mopeds are banned from expressways, whatever their engine size. Use national highways and provincial roads instead, and check that your navigation app is not routing you onto an expressway.

### Do speed signs override the general limits?

Yes. A posted maximum speed sign applies until an end sign or a new limit replaces it. Where no sign is posted, the general limits for the road type apply.

### How much is the fine for speeding on a motorbike?

Under Decree 168/2024/ND-CP, 5 to under 10 km/h over brings a fine of VND 400,000–600,000, 10 to 20 km/h over VND 800,000–1,000,000, and more than 20 km/h over VND 6–8 million, with licence points also at stake in the most serious band.

### Can a speeding fine reach me after I return the rental bike?

It can. Camera fines are sent to the registered owner, and the rental contract shows who had the bike. Keep your paperwork and ask the shop how later notices are handled.
