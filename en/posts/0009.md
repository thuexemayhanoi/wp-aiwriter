The Old Quarter is the reason most visitors come to Hanoi and the hardest place in the city to ride. Its thirty-six streets are narrow, mostly one-way, crowded with pavement kitchens, parked scooters and tour groups, and on weekend evenings a large part of it is closed to traffic altogether. Riding in is entirely possible, and on a weekday morning it is the quickest way to cross from the river to the lake; but the rider who does best is the one who knows which streets flow, where to leave the bike, and when to park on the edge and walk. This guide covers the approach from Long Bien, the pattern of the streets, parking with a numbered ticket, the pedestrian zone, and the moments when the bike is better left behind, as of October 2026.

## Getting there from Long Bien

Nguyen Ha's shop in Bo De sits on the east bank of the Red River, and two bridges lead to the Old Quarter. Chuong Duong bridge carries cars and motorbikes in dedicated lanes and lands you at the northern end of the quarter in a few minutes; it is the practical route in both directions. Long Bien bridge, the old iron railway bridge a little upstream, carries only motorbikes, bicycles and pedestrians beside the tracks, and it is famous for a quirk: traffic on it runs on the left, the opposite of every other road in Vietnam. Follow the riders in front of you, keep your speed down on the steel plates, and you will come off the bridge at the market end of the quarter with the best view of the river in the city.

Either way, allow ten to twenty minutes from the shop depending on the hour, and avoid the two rush periods, roughly seven to nine in the morning and five to seven in the evening, when the bridge approaches crawl.

## How the streets work

The Old Quarter grew as a grid of guild streets, each once selling one trade, and the names still say so: Hang Bac for silver, Hang Gai for silk, Hang Ma for paper offerings. For a rider the useful facts are simpler. Most streets are one-way, the signs are small and placed at the entrance to each block, and the direction changes from one street to the next, so a route through the quarter is a zigzag rather than a straight line. Follow the flow of the scooters around you; a street where every bike points towards you is one you should not enter.

Speeds are low, usually under 20 km/h, and the hazards are the ones that need attention rather than nerve: a pavement kitchen whose plastic stools spill into the road, a cyclo turning without looking, a delivery scooter stopping dead to unload, tourists stepping off the kerb mid-photograph. Ride in the middle of your lane, cover both brakes, tap the horn gently as you approach a blind corner, and never ride on the pavement, which is both an offence and the surest way to hit someone.

The main west-east axis of Hang Bong and Hang Gai, the north-south line of Hang Dao, Hang Ngang and Hang Duong up to Dong Xuan market, and the dyke road of Tran Nhat Duat along the river carry most of the traffic. The lanes between them, Hang Buom, Ta Hien, Ma May and their neighbours, are where the bars and the crowds are, and they are better walked than ridden at any hour after sunset.

## Parking with a ticket

Parking in the Old Quarter is a trade of its own. Guarded parking areas occupy marked stretches of pavement and small lots, each run by an attendant who hands you a numbered ticket and writes the number on your seat or mirror in chalk; you pay a small fee when you collect the bike, and the attendant checks the ticket against the mark. Use these every time. A bike left unattended on a pavement, however briefly, is at risk of a fine, of being towed, or of leaving with someone else, and the deposit on your rental is what pays for the consequence.

Lock the steering, take your helmet with you or lock it under the seat, and photograph the ticket number if you expect to be gone for hours. Some lots close at a fixed time in the evening, so ask when you park. Hotels in the quarter usually keep scooters in the lobby or a nearby lot overnight for a flat fee; ask at check-in, because street parking after midnight is not an option.

## The weekend walking streets

Since 2016 the streets around Hoan Kiem Lake have formed a pedestrian zone every weekend, under a schedule set by the Hoan Kiem authorities: from 19:00 on Friday to midnight on Sunday the lakeside roads and the streets nearest them are closed to motor traffic, and the night market and walking streets of the Old Quarter, from Hang Dao north to Dong Xuan market and through the bar lanes around Ta Hien, close on Friday, Saturday and Sunday evenings from 19:00. The hours are extended over public holidays and occasionally suspended for events or elections, and each change is announced in advance; check the latest notice before a weekend ride.

For a rider the rule of thumb is simple. Between Friday evening and Sunday night, treat the lake and the central Old Quarter as a place to walk to, not through. Park on the edge of the zone, along the dyke road, near Dong Xuan market or on the western streets around Hang Bong, and go in on foot; barriers and police at the entrances turn scooters away, and the lanes inside are shoulder to shoulder with people.

## When to walk instead

Even on a weekday, three situations call for leaving the bike.

- **Evenings in the bar lanes.** Ta Hien, Luong Ngoc Quyen and the streets around them fill with stools and people from dusk; a scooter gets through at walking pace, annoying everyone, and has nowhere to stop.
- **Sightseeing in a small area.** The lake, the Ngoc Son temple, the cathedral and the old houses on Ma May are within fifteen minutes' walk of one another. Park once at a guarded lot and see them on foot; you will see more and spend less time hunting for a second parking place.
- **Rain and festivals.** A downpour turns the narrow streets into a slow, slippery procession, and a festival or a market day closes streets without much notice. Wait it out over a coffee.

Riding earns its place for the trips the Old Quarter is bad at: getting to and from it. From a hotel near the lake, a scooter reaches the Temple of Literature, West Lake, the Ho Chi Minh mausoleum area or the shop in Long Bien in a fraction of the time a taxi takes in traffic, and parking at those destinations is easy. Ride to the edge, park with a ticket, walk the middle.

## A short checklist for the quarter

- Approach by Chuong Duong bridge, or by Long Bien bridge for the view, outside the rush hours.
- Follow the one-way signs at each block and the direction of the scooters around you.
- Keep off the pavements, cover the brakes, tap the horn at blind corners.
- Park only with an attendant and keep the numbered ticket.
- From Friday 19:00 to Sunday midnight, park on the edge and walk into the lakeside zone and the walking streets.
- Take your helmet, lock the steering, and ask your hotel about overnight parking.

## FAQ

### Can I ride a scooter in Hanoi's Old Quarter?

Yes, on weekdays and on weekend daytimes outside the pedestrian zone. The streets are narrow and mostly one-way, so follow the signs and the flow, keep your speed down and park only in guarded areas.

### When are the walking streets closed to traffic?

The pedestrian zone around Hoan Kiem Lake runs from 19:00 on Friday to midnight on Sunday, and the Old Quarter walking streets and night market close on Friday, Saturday and Sunday evenings from 19:00. Hours are extended on public holidays and sometimes changed for events; check the latest announcement.

### Where do I park a rental scooter in the Old Quarter?

In a guarded parking area with an attendant, who gives you a numbered ticket and marks the bike. Pay the small fee when you collect it, lock the steering and take your helmet.

### Is Long Bien bridge open to motorbikes?

Yes, in lanes beside the railway, and traffic on the bridge runs on the left. Cars use Chuong Duong bridge instead.

### What happens if my rental is towed or damaged while parked on the street?

The charges come out of your deposit under the contract, so park with an attendant every time and keep the ticket as proof.
