Yes, a tourist can legally ride a motorbike in Vietnam, but only with the right paperwork, and the paperwork is where most visitors go wrong. The short version: you need a valid driving licence from your home country that covers motorcycles, plus an International Driving Permit (IDP) issued under the 1968 Vienna Convention on Road Traffic. A permit issued under the older 1949 Geneva Convention, which is the only kind the United States, Canada, Australia and Japan issue, is not recognised in Vietnam. Without the right combination you are riding unlicensed, which means fines, a possible impound of the bike, and a travel insurance policy that will not pay if something goes wrong.

This guide explains the rules as they stand after the Law on Road Traffic Order and Safety (Law 36/2024/QH15) and the penalty Decree 168/2024/ND-CP, both effective from 1 January 2025, what the under-50cc exception really covers, and how Nguyen Ha handles licences at the shop. It is written for visitors, not lawyers; where the rules depend on your nationality or your length of stay, we say so, and the official sources are listed at the end. Information is current as of October 2026.

## The two documents you need

Vietnam does not accept a foreign driving licence on its own. The licence has to travel with a permit that translates it into the format Vietnamese police recognise, and the only permit Vietnam recognises is the one defined by the 1968 Vienna Convention. Vietnam joined that convention in 2014 and set out how foreign permits are used in Circular 29/2015/TT-BGTVT; the circular has since been amended, but the principle has not changed.

So the checklist at a roadside stop is:

1. **Your national driving licence**, valid and covering the motorcycle category you are riding.
2. **An International Driving Permit issued under the 1968 convention**, with the motorcycle category (A or A1) ticked and stamped. An IDP with only the car category does not cover a scooter.
3. **Your passport**, or at least a copy, together with the bike's registration card, which the rental shop gives you with the contract.

Carry the originals of the licence and the permit. A photo on your phone is not accepted as the document itself.

## Which countries' permits are valid

The 1968 convention is the one most European countries, the United Kingdom and many Asian and African states belong to, so their permits are valid in Vietnam. The United Kingdom issues both types; ask for the 1968 version. The United States, Canada, Australia, New Zealand and Japan issue permits only under the 1949 convention, and those permits have no legal effect on a Vietnamese road, however official they look.

If you hold a licence from a country that cannot issue a 1968 permit, your legal options are narrow: ride a vehicle that needs no licence (see the under-50cc section below), or, if you are staying long enough, convert your licence to a Vietnamese one. Conversion is possible for foreigners with a residence card or a visa long enough to justify it, through the provincial transport department, and takes paperwork and time; it is not something to arrange on a two-week holiday.

Check the current list of countries for yourself before you travel: the UN treaty collection lists the parties to each convention, and your national motoring organisation can tell you which type of permit it issues.

## Licence classes since 1 January 2025

The licence class you need depends on the engine. Law 36/2024/QH15 redrew the classes for two-wheelers when it took effect on 1 January 2025:

- **Class A1** covers two-wheel motorcycles up to 125 cc, and electric motorcycles up to 11 kW. Almost every rental scooter in Hanoi sits here: the Honda Vision, Air Blade 125, Lead, Click, the Yamaha scooters, and semi-automatic bikes like the Honda Wave and Yamaha Sirius.
- **Class A** covers two-wheel motorcycles above 125 cc and electric motorcycles above 11 kW. The Honda Air Blade 160 and the SH 150 and 160 need it.
- **Class B1** covers three-wheel motorcycles.

Licences issued before 2025 under the old classes stay valid until they expire; the new classes matter when you exchange a licence and when you read the categories on an IDP. For a visitor the practical rule is simple: your home licence and permit must cover motorcycles of at least the engine size you are renting. A permit limited to small motorcycles covers a Vision but not an Air Blade 160. If you are not sure, rent the smaller scooter; our comparison of the [Honda Vision and the Air Blade](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/honda-vision-vs-air-blade-which-scooter-to-rent-in-hanoi/) explains the difference in practice.

## The under-50cc exception

Law 36/2024/QH15 lets anyone aged 16 or over ride a moped with an engine under 50 cc (or an electric motor under 4 kW) without a driving licence. This is why Hanoi rental shops offer "50cc" scooters to visitors who cannot produce a valid permit, and why Nguyen Ha lists a 50cc class in its [price guide](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/prices/) at VND 200,000 per day.

Three cautions. First, the exception applies to the engine size written on the registration card, not to the sticker on the bodywork; a "50cc" scooter is only exempt if its registration says it is under 50 cc. Second, a moped is still a motor vehicle: the helmet rule, the alcohol rule, traffic lights and one-way streets apply to you exactly as to everyone else, and the fines are the same. Third, insurers treat mopeds differently from policy to policy, so read yours; "no licence needed" in Vietnam does not mean "covered" by your travel insurance at home.

Nguyen Ha's own house rule adds one more layer: customers aged 20 and over are asked for a licence of the right class for any bike that legally needs one, and riders aged 18 and 19 asking for a 50cc scooter are considered case by case at the shop. The shop does not sign contracts with 16 and 17 year olds; a parent or responsible adult has to take the contract. These are the shop's service conditions, not a statement of the minimum legal age for every vehicle.

## What happens if you ride without a licence

Decree 168/2024/ND-CP, in force since 1 January 2025, sets the penalties. For a rider who has no licence at all, or whose licence does not cover the vehicle, the fine is VND 2–4 million for a motorcycle up to 125 cc and VND 6–8 million for a motorcycle above 125 cc. The police can also hold the vehicle temporarily, which in practice means you lose the rental bike for days and the shop charges you for them. Forgetting a valid licence at the hotel is a different, much smaller offence, fined at VND 200,000–300,000, but you still need to produce the licence afterwards.

Two things make the real cost higher than the fine. A 1949-convention permit, or a car-only permit, counts as no licence for the vehicle, not as a technicality; the fine is the full amount. And almost every travel insurance policy excludes injuries sustained while riding without a licence that is valid in the country concerned, so an accident while unlicensed is paid for out of your own pocket, including medical evacuation. Being stopped is a nuisance; being injured without cover is the risk worth planning around.

## How a police stop works

Checkpoints on the main roads out of Hanoi and spot checks at big junctions are routine, and foreigners on rental scooters are stopped like everyone else. Pull over when signalled, switch off the engine, remove your helmet if asked, and hand over the licence, the permit and the registration card. Officers may speak little English; a photo of your contract and the shop's phone number help. Fines are paid at a treasury or bank counter against a written decision, not in cash at the roadside; ask for the written decision if one is issued.

If the bike is held, the written decision says where and for how long. Call the shop at once: Nguyen Ha can tell you what the next steps are and what the contract says about a vehicle held by the police.

## FAQ

### I have a 1949-convention IDP from the United States. Can I ride a scooter in Vietnam?

Not legally. Vietnam recognises only permits issued under the 1968 Vienna Convention. Your options are a moped under 50 cc, which needs no licence, or converting your licence to a Vietnamese one if you are staying long term.

### My IDP is a 1968 one but it only has the car category. Is that enough?

No. The permit must carry the motorcycle category for the size of bike you ride. A car-only permit leaves you unlicensed on a scooter.

### Does a tourist need a Vietnamese licence?

No, as long as you have a valid home licence and a 1968-convention IDP with the right category. A Vietnamese licence is only needed if you cannot produce that combination and want to ride something larger than an under-50cc moped.

### How much is the fine for riding without a licence?

Under Decree 168/2024/ND-CP, effective 1 January 2025, the fine is VND 2–4 million for a motorcycle up to 125 cc and VND 6–8 million above 125 cc, and the bike can be held. Not carrying a licence you do hold is fined at VND 200,000–300,000.

### Can I rent a 50cc scooter without any licence?

Yes, for a moped whose registration shows an engine under 50 cc, if you are at least 16. Nguyen Ha applies its own age conditions at the shop and will explain them when you book.
