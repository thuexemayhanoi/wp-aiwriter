For most 50cc day trips around Hanoi you will need only a few litres of petrol, but the safe way to budget is to work in litres first and money second: estimate the distance, divide by a cautious figure of kilometres per litre, add a reserve and then multiply by the price on the pump. Honda's current Super Cub 50 has a 4.3-litre tank and a published economy of 105 km per litre at a steady 30 km/h, and 69.4 km per litre in the standard WMTC test, yet Honda itself says real results vary with weather, traffic, riding style and the bike's condition. A rental bike in stop-and-go traffic will not match a laboratory number, so this guide shows a simple table, a worked example and the shop's handover and return rules, and explains why the 40 km/h limit for this bike class keeps day trips short.

## What Honda's figures tell you, and what they do not

Honda's Super Cub 50 specification lists a fuel tank capacity of 4.3 litres. For economy it gives two measured figures: 105 km per litre as a steady-state test at 30 km/h with one person, and 69.4 km per litre in the WMTC mode, a test cycle that mixes speeds. Honda adds a footnote that fuel consumption is calculated under pre-determined test conditions, and that performance may vary with the customer's environment (weather and traffic), riding style and the bike's condition and maintenance.

For a trip budget, use these numbers as an upper bound and not a promise. A rental 50cc in Hanoi may be a different make or an older bike, it carries a rider and perhaps a bag, and it spends much of its time at junctions and in low gears. Those conditions reduce the distance per litre. Plan with a more cautious figure than the catalogue and you will rarely be caught short.

## Three planning figures

The table below does not claim to be your bike's economy. It shows what a given distance needs at three assumed efficiencies, so that you can see how sensitive the answer is.

| Round trip | Cautious (30 km per litre) | Middle (45 km per litre) | Honda WMTC figure (69.4 km per litre) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 40 km | 1.3 litres | 0.9 litres | 0.6 litres |
| 60 km | 2.0 litres | 1.3 litres | 0.9 litres |
| 80 km | 2.7 litres | 1.8 litres | 1.2 litres |
| 100 km | 3.3 litres | 2.2 litres | 1.4 litres |

The 30 and 45 figures are planning assumptions, not manufacturer data. Use the cautious column when you do not know the bike, when you carry a passenger or luggage, when the route includes hills or heavy traffic, or when it is raining. A rule of thumb for a cautious budget is to assume the highest litres in the row, then add a reserve.

## Add a reserve, then check against the tank

A reserve matters more than the exact estimate. For a day trip, plan to finish with at least a litre in the tank, so that a detour, a closed road or a headwind does not strand you. A 4.3-litre tank, if your bike has a similar one, leaves room to cover the cautious 100 km estimate with a litre or so to spare. Because rental bikes vary, ask the shop the tank size and how to read the fuel level, since small bikes may have no gauge, or an unreliable one.

## Check the level before you leave, and again at midday

Before you ride away, ask the shop to show you how the fuel level is read. Some small bikes have a needle gauge, some have a single warning light and some have nothing at all. If there is no gauge, note the trip meter reading at handover, if the bike has one, and reset it so that you know how far you have travelled since the last fill. Look at the level again before you head back, not when the engine first stutters.

A simple midday routine works well: at your lunch stop, or at the turning point of the loop, check the tank and top up if you are below the reserve you planned. Topping up with a litre or two at a petrol station costs little, and it removes the worry from the return leg. If the bike hesitates or the engine dies on a flat road, do not keep pressing the starter; pull over safely and call the shop.

## Turning litres into money

The conversion is simple: litres multiplied by the price per litre on the pump. Prices change, so read the board at the station instead of relying on a figure in an article. Take the cautious litres from the table, add your reserve, multiply by the posted price, and you have a budget with a margin. If you actually use less, you simply spend less. ## The shop's fuel rules for handover and return

These are facts from the shop's published terms, and they shape your budget:

- **At handover,** petrol bikes come with roughly 0.5 to 2 litres of fuel.
- **At return,** the shop expects roughly 0.5 to 1 litre in the tank.
- **If the tank is empty at return,** there is a charge of VND 20,000.
- **Electric bikes** are different: battery level and charging are confirmed at handover.

The consequence is that you should check the tank before you leave and budget the first fill-up into your day. If the bike starts with 0.5 litre and your trip needs 2 litres, you will need to refuel early on. Do not rely on the handover fuel to carry you far. At the end, return with about half a litre or more, not an empty tank. ## How the 40 km/h limit shapes the trip

Vietnam's Circular 38/2024/TT-BGTVT, effective 1 January 2025, sets a maximum operating speed of 40 km/h for mopeds on roads other than expressways. A bike with an engine of no more than 50 cm3 and a design speed of no more than 50 km/h falls in this class. That cap matters for budgeting in two ways.

First, it limits distance in a day. At an average of about 30 km/h including junctions, 100 km takes over three hours of riding, and the figure is higher once you add stops. A realistic 50cc day trip is therefore a loop of tens of kilometres, not a long highway run. Our guide to [Long Bien and the Red River](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/long-bien-red-river-first-ride/) shows an easy outing that suits a small bike, and the one on [taking a rental motorbike out of Hanoi](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/take-rental-motorbike-out-of-hanoi/) explains the questions to ask first.

Second, it keeps the engine in a fuel-friendly range. Holding the bike at a moderate speed uses less fuel than hard acceleration, and Honda's steady-speed figure is measured at 30 km/h for that reason.

## Habits that lower fuel use

- **Roll the throttle gently,** since hard acceleration burns fuel for little gain on a small engine.
- **Avoid long idling** at red lights if you can switch off safely where it is permitted and the bike restarts reliably.
- **Keep tyres at the pressure the shop recommends,** because soft tyres increase rolling resistance.
- **Travel light,** since extra weight uses more fuel.
- **Ride at a steady pace** and anticipate junctions, which reduces repeated acceleration.
- **Plan the route** to avoid long detours and heavy traffic.

## A worked example

Suppose you plan a 60 km loop on a rental 50cc, and you do not know its economy. Use the cautious row: 2.0 litres. Add a one-litre reserve: 3.0 litres in total. If the bike was handed over with about 1 litre, you need to buy roughly 2 litres early on, then check the level again before the return leg. At the end, you should have at least about half a litre left, per the shop's return expectation. If your actual economy is better, you finish with more fuel than planned and spend less. The point of the method is that your worst case is covered, not that the estimate is exact.

## FAQ: fuel for a 50cc day trip

### How much petrol does a 50cc bike use per 100 km?

Honda's published figures imply roughly 1 to 1.5 litres per 100 km in a test, but a rental bike in traffic may use about 2 to 3 litres or more. Budget cautiously and refuel when needed.

### How big is the tank?

Honda's Super Cub 50 has a 4.3-litre tank. Rental bikes vary, so ask the shop.

### How much fuel will my rental have at handover?

Petrol bikes come with about 0.5 to 2 litres, per the shop's terms, and should return with about 0.5 to 1 litre.

### What if I return with an empty tank?

The shop charges VND 20,000 for an empty tank at return, so refuel before you bring the bike back.

### Can a 50cc bike do a long highway trip?

Not comfortably. The speed cap of 40 km/h and the small engine make long trips slow, and expressways are closed to motorbikes anyway.
