Photograph a rental motorbike in a fixed order, in good light, with the shop staff present: one wide shot of each side, close-ups of every mark you can find, then the odometer, fuel gauge, plate, frame number, tyres, keys and accessories, finished with a slow video lap of the whole bike. Aim for about forty photos in five minutes, keep them on your phone with their date and time intact and repeat the same set when you return the bike. The point is not to start an argument; it is to give you and the shop the same neutral record of the bike's condition on the day it left. This guide is about camera technique and file handling, so it does not repeat the mechanical checks covered elsewhere.

## Why photographs do the job paper cannot

A handover form usually has a few words such as "scratches on left side" and a tick. A photograph shows where, how long and how deep. It also settles the question that causes most disputes at return: was this mark here before? The Civil Code points the same way. Article 482 of the Civil Code 2015 requires a lessee to return leased property in the condition in which it was received, normal wear and tear excepted, so the condition at the start is the baseline for everything that follows.

Shop guides and travel advice written for visitors repeat the same habit. A Vinpearl guide to renting in Nha Trang advises taking photos of existing scratches and testing the brakes, lights and tyres before leaving, and a rental operator's published guide recommends a slow walkaround video that includes the odometer. These are commercial sources, so treat them as general practice, not rules. The logic is simple enough to stand on its own.

## Before you take a single photo

**Ask first, in a friendly way.** Say that you would like to photograph the bike so both sides have the same record. A fair shop will agree and may take its own photos at the same time. Nguyen Ha's own terms treat photographs of identity documents as part of the contract paperwork, so a photographic record is normal there.

**Choose the light.** Daylight outdoors is best. Direct midday sun creates glare on glossy plastic and hides scratches; open shade or an overcast sky shows marks clearly. In the evening, use the phone torch held at an angle to the panel so scratches throw a small shadow. Hanoi's shop hours run 09:00 to 21:00, so you may be collecting after dark; if so, ask to roll the bike under a light.

**Clean the lens.** A smeared lens turns every shot to haze. Wipe it on your sleeve first.

**Turn on the date.** Check that your phone's clock and time zone are correct, because the timestamp in the file details is your evidence of when the pictures were made. Do not edit, crop or filter the originals.

## The order of shots

Work around the bike in the same direction every time, so nothing is skipped and the return set can be compared like for like. The sequence below takes about five minutes.

| Step | What to capture | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Whole bike, left side, then right, front and rear, at hip height | Shows overall condition and the plate in context |
| 2 | Number plate, close and readable | Ties the photos to this exact vehicle |
| 3 | Each body panel, one close shot per panel | Records scratches, cracks and scuffs |
| 4 | Any existing mark with something for scale, such as a finger beside it | Shows how long it is |
| 5 | Mirrors, both sides, and the levers | Mirrors and levers are common damage points |
| 6 | Headlight, tail light and indicators | Cracked lenses are easy to miss |
| 7 | Front and rear tyres, plus the sidewalls | Shows tread and cuts |
| 8 | Wheels, rims, brake discs or drums | Shows kerb marks and bends |
| 9 | Seat, floor mats and footrests | Tears and cuts to the seat |
| 10 | Dashboard with the ignition on | Warning lights, fuel gauge, odometer |
| 11 | Frame and engine numbers, if reachable | Identifies the machine |
| 12 | Keys, spare key, helmet and anything handed over | Settles what was provided |
| 13 | Slow video lap, ending on the plate | Context for the stills |

Take each important mark twice: once close enough to see the surface and once far enough to show where on the bike it sits. A close-up of a scratch with no location is almost useless as evidence.

## Shots that people forget

**The odometer.** Honda's Vision owner's manual describes the odometer as showing the total distance the vehicle has travelled, and says it rolls back to zero after 99,999.9. For a rental, the reading at handover proves how far you rode, which matters for a week-long or month-long rental. Photograph the odometer and the fuel gauge together in one frame with the ignition on.

**The frame and engine numbers.** The same manual explains that the frame number and engine number identify the bike and are needed for registration and ordering parts, and it recommends recording them. You may not need these shots, but on an older bike with a plate that has been changed they tie the pictures to one machine. If the numbers are hard to find, do not hunt for them; ask the staff to point.

**Under the seat.** Open the seat storage and photograph it with the contents, such as a raincoat, tool roll or papers, if present. Nguyen Ha does not include a raincoat for short rentals, so the box may be empty, and an empty box on camera is also a record.

**The registration paper.** Many riders are required to carry the vehicle registration certificate, and Law No. 36/2024/QH15 lists it among the documents a rider carries. Ask whether the copy stays with the bike, and photograph it so you know what you were given. Photograph only the vehicle paper, not other customers' documents.

**Tyre wear.** Tyres show wear as flattened or smooth rubber, not as damage, and a puncture is a different question. Photograph both tyres so that wear at the start is visible.

## Stills, video or both?

Use both. Stills give you resolution for small marks; video gives you the sweep of the whole bike and keeps your narration. Try this routine.

1. Take the stills in the order above.

2. Start a video at the front, walk slowly round the bike, and say the date, the plate and the shop's name aloud.

3. Zoom the video slowly on any mark while the staff member watches.

4. End with the dashboard.

Keep the video under about two minutes so that it is easy to send and easy to review. If your phone offers a higher resolution setting, use it for the stills but keep the video at standard resolution to save space.

## Do it with the shop, not behind its back

The photographs are most useful when the shop has seen them. Two simple steps turn private pictures into a shared record.

**Point as you shoot.** When you find a mark, say "this scratch on the left panel" and let the staff member see it. If they say it is normal or already known, ask them to note it on the form.

**Send them.** Message a few key photos to the shop's number, 0334 699 969 (+84 334 699 969), or show them on your screen and ask the staff member to confirm "yes, these are the bike's current marks". A message from the shop's number or a reply of "OK" gives the photographs a second witness.

If the staff are unhappy about being photographed, you can photograph only the bike. The point is the bike, not them.

## Storing and labelling the files

A good set of photos is worthless if it is lost or cannot be found. Do the following the same day.

- **Make an album** named for the plate number and rental date, such as the shop name and the day you collected.
- **Back it up** to cloud storage or send it to yourself, so that a lost or broken phone does not erase it.
- **Keep the originals** unedited, including the file details.
- **Do not delete anything** until your deposit is back in your hands and the shop has confirmed the return.

## Repeat the set at return

Take the same shots, in the same order, when you return the bike, before you hand over the key. Lining up the two sets is the fastest way to resolve a question such as "was this crack there before?". If you have had a fall in between, photograph the damage straight away and tell the shop at once; our guide on [what to do after dropping a rented semi-automatic bike](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/what-to-do-after-dropping-a-rented-semi-automatic-bike/) explains the steps. For the handback itself, see [returning your rental motorbike in Hanoi](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/returning-your-rental-motorbike-hanoi/).

## Privacy and good manners

Do not photograph other customers or their documents. Ask before including staff faces. Do not post pictures of the shop's premises or its customers' bikes online without permission. And keep copies of your own passport and licence pictures private: send them only to the shop that needs them for the contract, not to chat groups.

## What the photos cannot do

Photographs record visible condition. They cannot show a worn clutch, a slipping chain or a weak brake, so they do not replace a short test ride and a check with the staff. ## FAQ: photographing a rental bike

### How many photos should I take?

About forty is comfortable: a few wide shots, one close shot per panel, and the instruments, tyres, plate and accessories. Add a short video.

### Do I need the shop's permission?

It is polite to ask, and most shops agree. If a shop refuses to let you record the bike's condition at all, take that into account when deciding whether to rent.

### Should I photograph the odometer?

Yes. It records the starting distance and helps if the rental runs for days or weeks.

### What if the phone clock is wrong?

Correct it before shooting. The timestamp is part of the evidence, so do not edit the files later.

### How long should I keep the pictures?

Until the deposit has been returned and the shop has confirmed the return. Keeping them for a few weeks longer costs nothing.
