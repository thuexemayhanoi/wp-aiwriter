The point of a rental handover is not to prove that you are a mechanic. It is to notice the problems a rider can see or feel **before** taking responsibility for an unfamiliar motorcycle. That distinction matters especially with a semi-automatic bike such as a Honda Wave, Honda Blade or Yamaha Sirius. You have foot-operated gears instead of a scooter's twist-and-go transmission, a more exposed chain, and a rear brake control that may be different from what you normally use. A short, methodical check at the shop can help you decide whether the bike is comfortable, complete and ready for the planned journey. This English guide focuses on semi-automatic motorcycles, rather than repeating the general scooter inspection article, and uses manufacturer guidance alongside Nguyen Ha's rental handover practice as of October 2026.

## First confirm what kind of bike is in front of you

A semi-automatic motorcycle uses a foot-operated gear selector but usually has no hand-operated clutch lever. It is not the same as a fully manual motorcycle with a clutch. Check the actual model name and the engine capacity recorded on the vehicle documents; rental adverts sometimes group different transmissions under the word "manual". You should not learn an unfamiliar gear pattern for the first time while surrounded by city traffic.

Stand next to the bike with the engine off. Ask a member of staff to identify the ignition and steering lock, the front and rear brake controls, the indicators, lights, horn, engine cut-off if fitted, and how the gear indicator works. Some bikes use a heel-and-toe selector. The pattern varies across models and production years, so let the owner demonstrate the exact motorcycle rather than assuming every Honda and Yamaha shifts in the same way.

Ask where the fuel filler is and what the fuel gauge indicates. Confirm which key and helmet are supplied, and whether the bike has mirrors on both sides. Look for a securely attached luggage rack only if you intend to use one; a loose phone mount or overloaded rack is not an accessory benefit.

If the bike feels too tall or heavy to hold still, say so immediately. You are choosing a vehicle to ride and park repeatedly, not completing a test you must pass.

## Check both tyres, not merely the front one

Tyres are easiest to inspect while the bike is stationary, parked securely on level ground. Look at the contact surface for embedded glass, nails, cuts, unusual bulges and worn tread. The front and rear tyres carry different loads, and an underinflated rear tyre can feel especially vague with a passenger or luggage. A sidewall that looks damaged is a reason to stop and ask for another tyre or another bike.

A visual check is not an accurate tyre-pressure measurement. Ask the shop to use a gauge and the correct **cold pressure for that model and load**, as shown in the motorcycle's manual or label. Do not copy a number from a stranger's scooter because wheel sizes and recommended pressures differ. Honda's Wave Alpha product documentation, for example, identifies different front and rear tyre sizes; it is not a universal specification for every Wave or Sirius.

Have the staff turn each wheel if this can be done safely on a suitable stand. Listen for obvious rubbing and check that the tyre does not wobble visibly. You need not touch a wheel while someone operates the engine. If the bike has recently been ridden, keep clear of hot parts around the engine and exhaust. If a tyre looks suspect or loses pressure soon after inflation, do not accept a promise to "just ride slowly"; ask for it to be checked first.

## Test the front and rear brakes separately

Do not confuse the controls on a semi-automatic with those of an automatic scooter. A typical semi-automatic has a front brake lever on the right handlebar and a foot-operated rear brake; the shop should confirm the layout on the actual vehicle. With the engine switched off and enough room around the machine, gently move the bike by hand and apply each brake separately. The front should stop the wheel when its lever is pressed, and the rear should respond when you operate the correct pedal.

Brake feel matters as much as whether a wheel eventually stops. A lever that comes all the way back without resistance, a pedal that sticks, or a harsh grinding noise deserves immediate attention. Squealing by itself does not diagnose a particular defect, but it should be mentioned so the shop can assess it. You should not try to repair or adjust an unfamiliar rental brake yourself.

If the initial stationary check is satisfactory, ask whether you can make a short, controlled test ride in a safe permitted area with the owner's agreement. Use a walking pace, test smooth stopping and return if the controls feel unpredictable. Do not test emergency braking in crowded street traffic, and never ride a machine you suspect is unsafe simply to see if the problem disappears.

## Look at the chain, selector and transmission without taking anything apart

A chain-driven semi-automatic has moving parts you can inspect visually while the engine is **off**. Look for an obviously hanging, damaged, very dry or badly misaligned chain, a broken or loose cover, and anything that might interfere with the rear wheel. The correct chain tension is model-specific. Do not put your fingers into the sprocket area or ask someone to start the engine while you are standing close to the chain.

Check that the gear pedal is firmly attached and not bent into an awkward position. Ask the shop to show you how to find neutral and interpret the gear indicator. Trying to force a shift with a stationary engine or an unfamiliar gear pattern is not a useful inspection technique. A short supervised demonstration is a safer way to understand how the gears engage.

During an agreed slow test ride, pay attention to a gear that repeatedly jumps out, excessive clunks beyond the motorcycle's usual shifting sound, or a strong hesitation when moving off. New riders sometimes mistake their own abrupt throttle use for a fault, which is why a shop demonstration matters. If you cannot tell whether something is normal, ask staff to ride the motorcycle and explain its operation. You should not start a long trip with an unresolved drivetrain concern.

## Lights, horn, mirrors and steering controls

Turn on the ignition in the way shown by the shop and check the headlamp, rear lamp, brake lamp, turn signals and horn. Some newer Honda models have a headlamp that remains on when the engine is running, so do not assume an automatic daytime headlight is a defect. Ask another person to stand safely behind the bike while you activate the brake and indicators; this can reveal a brake light that does not respond to one of the controls.

Adjust the two mirrors before leaving. Each should offer a useful rearward view without requiring you to twist your body. A cracked lens, loose mirror stem or damaged indicator is worth documenting even if it does not prevent the engine from running. Check the steering moves smoothly with the bike stationary and that the handlebar does not contact a loose accessory when turned.

Try the steering lock while stationary only, then confirm it is fully released before riding. Do not ride away with the handlebar restricted. The supplied key should operate the functions the owner has demonstrated. If an electric starter does not work normally, ask about the approved starting method rather than improvising.

## Record condition, fuel and the actual start time

Rental misunderstandings often concern old scratches, bent plastic, fuel levels or the agreed return hour rather than major mechanical faults. Take photographs of the front, back and both sides of the bike, then close-ups of any marks that already exist. Record the dashboard and the fuel gauge or ask staff to note the estimated fuel quantity. Photograph the number plate and the relevant line of the rental document if allowed.

Nguyen Ha's published fuel handover reference for petrol bikes is approximately half a litre to two litres; the expected return amount is around half a litre to one litre, depending on the agreement. The exact handover condition is more useful than an assumption based on the needle position. A tank returned empty may incur a VND 20,000 fee under the published reference terms. Ask for the fuel condition to be clearly understood on both sides.

Read the agreement's start timestamp. A standard rental day is twenty-four hours from handover. That timestamp matters more than what a website calls a "day rental." Ask about late-return terms, the separate cash deposit and what happens if a tyre is punctured during your hire. The [English rental guide](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/rental-guide/) explains the overall process and the [price page](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/prices/) gives the current reference amounts.

Bring suitable identity and driving documents. The shop may check and photograph the necessary details for the contract and returns the originals after rent and deposit are paid. If you are unsure whether your licence and international permit cover this exact engine size, resolve that before accepting the bike; our [licence article for visitors](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/motorbike-licence-rules-for-foreigners-in-vietnam/) explains where to begin. As of October 2026, the written agreement and the applicable rules govern the rental, not a generic claim that all geared motorcycles are equivalent.

## Which problems should stop the handover?

Not every scratch means a motorcycle is unsafe. Cosmetic marks can be photographed and acknowledged. A missing or ineffective brake, visibly damaged tyre, steering that binds, cracked load-bearing part, unsecure wheel component or malfunctioning essential lighting needs proper attention before the bike leaves the premises. A problem with a critical control is not something to compensate for by riding more slowly.

If the bike is unsuitable, ask for another available model. A renter who finds the controls uncomfortable can also choose an automatic scooter, provided it matches their existing riding ability. The [manual-versus-automatic comparison](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/manual-semi-automatic-or-automatic-motorbike-hanoi/) helps with that choice.

For any later breakdown, stop in a safe location and contact the shop promptly. Agree on repair instructions and costs before arranging significant work with a roadside mechanic. Keep receipts and photographs if a minor repair has been authorised. Returning an unsafe bike without mentioning the defect creates unnecessary risk for the next rider.

## FAQ

### Is a Honda Wave fully manual?

Usually the familiar rental Wave is a semi-automatic motorcycle with a foot-operated shifter and no hand clutch lever. Verify the particular model with the shop; a fully manual clutch motorcycle is a different kind of machine.

### Can I check tyre pressure just by squeezing a tyre?

No. You can notice an obviously soft tyre visually, but a correct pressure check uses a gauge and the figure for that exact motorcycle and load. The shop should handle the measurement and any adjustment.

### Should I test the rear brake with the same lever as an automatic scooter?

Not without checking the control layout. Many semi-automatics have a rear brake pedal operated by the right foot, whereas many automatic scooters use a left handlebar lever. Have the owner demonstrate the controls first.

### Is a scratched plastic panel a reason to reject the rental?

Not automatically. Photograph the mark and record it at handover. A cracked part that is loose, touches moving components or suggests structural damage should be examined before the bike is ridden.

### What if the chain sounds noisy after I collect the bike?

Stop somewhere safe, switch off the engine and contact the shop. Do not touch the moving chain or attempt adjustment without the correct tools and instructions. The shop can advise whether an inspection or replacement bike is needed.
