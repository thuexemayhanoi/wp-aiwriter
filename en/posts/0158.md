Larger wheels usually ride rough roads more comfortably, because a bigger wheel meets a pothole or a raised edge at a gentler angle and rolls over it instead of dropping in. On the scooters you are likely to rent in Hanoi that means a 14-inch wheel at both ends, as on a Honda Air Blade or a standard Vision, tends to feel smoother over broken asphalt than the 12-inch front and 10-inch rear on a Honda Lead. The Lead pays you back with a low seat and a large storage box. Wheel size is only one part of the story, though: tyre pressure, tyre condition, suspension, your speed and the way you approach a bump matter just as much. This guide explains the physics in plain terms, shows the sizes Honda actually lists, tells you what to expect on typical rough surfaces and gives simple habits that make any scooter more comfortable.

## What wheel size changes on a bump

Think of a wheel meeting a step, such as a raised manhole cover or the edge of a pothole. A small wheel hits the face of the step nearly head on, so much of the force goes backwards into the bike and into your hands and seat. A larger wheel touches the edge higher up and at a shallower angle, so more of the force lifts the wheel up and over.

The same idea explains why larger wheels roll over a pothole instead of dropping into it. A British tyre retailer's guide to 125cc scooters makes this point in general terms: larger wheels tend to give a better quality of ride because they roll over potholes rather than drop into them, and they are less likely to follow cracks and grooves in the road, a trait called tramlining. The guide notes that wheels of 14 inches are very common on modern scooters, while 16-inch wheels make a scooter feel more like a motorcycle over bumps and cracks.

There is a trade-off. A bigger, heavier wheel takes more effort to turn and to steer through tight gaps, while a smaller wheel feels more nimble at walking pace. Both sides of that bargain show up in Hanoi traffic.

### Reading the code

A code like 90/90-12 means a tyre about 90 mm wide, with a sidewall height of 90 percent of that width, mounted on a 12-inch rim. Working this out gives a rough overall diameter, which is the number that decides how the wheel meets a bump:

- Lead front, 90/90-12: about 467 mm.
- Lead rear, 100/90-10: about 434 mm.
- Vision front, 80/90-14: about 500 mm.
- Air Blade rear, 100/80-14: about 516 mm.
- Vision Sport front, 80/90-16: about 550 mm.

These are our own calculations from the size codes, rounded, and real tyres vary a little by make and wear. They show that the Lead's rear wheel is smaller than the other bikes' wheels by a visible margin, and that the 16-inch Vision front is the tallest of the group.

## What this means on real rough surfaces

A few examples from city riding, in general terms:

**Potholes and broken patches.** This is where a larger wheel helps most. A 14-inch wheel will often roll over the edge of a hole that a 10-inch rear wheel would thump into. On a Lead you feel the rear first.

**Speed bumps.** The bump itself is gentle, but the edge of a painted or cut bump can be sharp. Slowing down matters more than wheel size here. Our guide to [riding over speed bumps](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/riding-a-manual-motorbike-over-speed-bumps/) was written for manual bikes, but the advice on slowing early and relaxing your arms applies to scooters too.

**Cracks, grooves and rail lines.** Larger wheels tend to follow grooves less. Take rails and cracks as straight across as you can, whatever the bike. If you plan a ride over the Long Bien area, see our [easy first ride out of central Hanoi](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/long-bien-red-river-first-ride/) for what to expect.

**Gravel and village roads.** On loose surfaces, a taller wheel helps, but the tyre and your speed count for more. For craft villages and quiet east-bank lanes, our guide to [Gia Lam and the Bat Trang area](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/gia-lam-east-bank-bat-trang-by-scooter/) suggests what the roads are like.

**Standing water.** Rough roads hide potholes under puddles. Our guide to [riding through standing water](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/riding-a-manual-motorbike-through-standing-water/) explains why you slow down and pick a line, and it applies to any wheel size.

## Where small wheels earn their place

It would be wrong to call small wheels bad. The Lead's 12-inch front and 10-inch rear are part of a design with a low seat height of 760 mm and a large under-seat box that Honda lists at more than 37 litres. A smaller rear wheel can leave more room under the seat. It also makes the bike easy to put on its stand and to push around in a crowded parking lot.

A smaller wheel also helps in tight turns, in narrow alleys and when you are filtering slowly through traffic, because the bike turns more eagerly. For a rider who sticks to flat city streets and values storage, a Lead can be the more comfortable choice overall, even if it is slightly harsher on a broken road. Our guide to [Honda Vision versus Air Blade](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/honda-vision-vs-air-blade-which-scooter-to-rent-in-hanoi/) compares two 14-inch models in more detail.

## What matters as much as wheel size

Wheel diameter is the first thing people notice, but a good-ride list is longer.

### Tyre pressure

Pressure has a bigger effect on ride feel than most riders expect. Honda's manuals ask you to check pressure at least once a month and whenever a tyre looks soft, with cold tyres. The Vision manual gives 200 kPa at the front and 225 kPa at the rear. The Lead manual gives 175 kPa at the front and 225 kPa at the rear.

A tyre that is too soft feels vague, wears at the edges and can be damaged by a hard hit. A tyre that is too hard feels harsh over bumps. Do not let air out to soften the ride. Honda's manual warns that tyres that are worn or not inflated correctly are a cause of crashes. Ask the shop to check pressure at handover. Our guide to [reading a tyre size and pressure label](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/how-to-read-a-honda-wave-tyre-size-and-pressure-label/) shows where the numbers are on a Honda Wave, and the idea is the same on a scooter.

### Tyre condition

An old, hard tyre with a worn tread rides worse than a newer one. Look for cracks, uneven wear and the tread wear indicator mark that Honda says should lead you to replace the tyre when it shows. If two bikes have the same wheel size, the one with fresher tyres will usually feel better.

### Suspension

The fork and the rear shock absorb bumps, and their condition changes from bike to bike. A bounce that continues after you press down on the seat or a clunk from the front end suggests tired suspension. Mention it at handover.

### Weight and load

A heavy bag or a second rider adds load on the rear shock and tyre. Honda's manuals limit loads, and our guide to [a scooter for two riders and luggage in Hanoi](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/scooter-for-two-riders-and-luggage-hanoi/) looks at how much is sensible.

### Seat and posture

A relaxed posture with soft elbows absorbs bumps far better than stiff arms. Keep your weight in your seat and your knees loose, and look ahead, not at the front wheel.

## Habits that make any scooter more comfortable

1. **Slow down before the bad patch,** not on it. Braking hard over a bump can make the front wheel skip.
2. **Pick a line** around the worst holes if traffic allows, and do not swerve suddenly.
3. **Cross rails and ridges square on,** at a right angle.
4. **Keep your hands light on the bars** and your elbows bent.
5. **Release the brakes just before a bump** if you can do so safely, so the suspension is free to move.
6. **Avoid riding on rough roads at night** when you cannot see the holes. See our guide to [riding at night in Hanoi](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/riding-at-night-in-hanoi/).
7. **Allow extra time** for roads you do not know.

## Choosing a bike for a rough-road ride

If you plan to leave smooth city streets, choose with the route in mind:

- **Mostly city streets and short hops:** any automatic works, and a Lead's storage may matter more than its small wheels.
- **Mixed city and suburban roads:** a 14-inch bike such as an Air Blade or standard Vision is a safe default.
- **Village lanes, patched roads or longer rides:** prefer larger wheels, fresh tyres and a rested rider.
- **Steep or very rough mountain tracks:** a small automatic scooter is a poor tool. Our article on [manual bikes and steep mountain roads](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/manual-bikes-and-steep-mountain-roads-when-to-choose-another-vehicle/) explains when to choose something else.

## FAQ: scooter wheel size and rough roads

### Are 14-inch wheels always more comfortable than 12-inch ones?

Usually, on broken roads, but tyre pressure, suspension and tyre condition can outweigh wheel size.

### Does a Honda Lead ride badly on rough roads?

It has smaller wheels, so the rear can feel firmer over holes. It also has a low seat, a large storage box and good manoeuvrability, which many riders value more.

### Should I lower tyre pressure for comfort?

No. Use the pressure the shop gives for your bike. Soft tyres feel vague and wear badly.

### Does a 16-inch front wheel make a difference?

It stands taller than the 14-inch version and Honda says it gives a high, upright position. It should also meet bumps at a gentler angle.

### Can I choose a bike by wheel size when I rent?

You can ask, but the bike actually available is confirmed when you book.

### What should I tell the shop if the ride feels harsh?

Say where you feel it, front or rear, and ask them to check the tyre pressure and the suspension.
