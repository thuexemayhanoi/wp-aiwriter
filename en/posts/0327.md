Riding through Hanoi's university neighbourhoods is an easy way to see everyday city life, as long as you treat the area as a place where thousands of people live, study and cross the road on foot. The main campuses sit along a few corridors, mainly Xuan Thuy in Cau Giay, Dai Co Viet and Giai Phong in the south, and Chua Lang in Dong Da. The sensible approach is to ride slowly, keep out of campus gates, park in a marked or guarded place, avoid honking at night and cross the wide boulevards at junctions rather than between them. This guide explains where the university areas are, what changes through the day, how gates and parking work, what the 2026 low-emission plans mean for the south-central campuses and how to plan a half-day ride from Long Bien.

## Why a campus is more than a gate

A large campus works like a small town. HUST says its campus covers 26 hectares and includes a library, lecture halls, laboratories and dormitories. The Hanoi National University of Education says its site also holds a gifted high school, a lower and upper secondary school, a primary school and a kindergarten, which means that school-run traffic and student traffic share the same streets. That mix, with teenagers, young children, parents on scooters and staff arriving in cars, explains why the roads outside can feel busier and less predictable than the streets of an ordinary business district.

It also means that the traffic at a campus is not just commuters. People walk across at all angles, bikes stop suddenly at stalls and food carts, and students cross in groups. Ride as though someone may step out at any moment, because someone often does.

## How the day tends to run

University streets follow the timetable. As a general pattern, expect heavier mixed traffic when teaching blocks begin and end, around midday when students go for lunch and in the evening when cafes and eateries fill. Between those periods the same roads can feel calm. Exam weeks and the start of a new academic year change the pattern again, as do holidays, when some areas empty out.

Exact hours differ by campus and by term, so a good habit is to watch the street for a minute before you commit. If a road is full of bikes streaming in one direction, that is a signal. Slow down, keep a gap and wait until the flow has passed instead of cutting across it. If you can choose your time, ride through between the peaks.

## Campus gates, visitors and where not to stop

Each university sets its own rules about who may enter, and whether a visitor on a scooter can ride inside. Do not assume that a gate is open to you. Ask the guard before you go in, follow any instructions about where to leave the bike and expect to leave it outside if the answer is no.

Outside the gate, the traffic law is clear. Vietnam's Law on Road Traffic Order and Safety (Law 36/2024/QH15, effective 1 January 2025) says in Article 18 that you must not stop or park in front of the gate of an agency or organisation with a vehicle entrance, or within 5 metres either side of it. A university gate usually fits that description, so treat the rule as applying. The same article also bars stopping at pedestrian crossings, within 5 metres of a junction, at pick-up and drop-off points and where only one lane of motor vehicles fits. Parents' and students' bikes often cluster at gates, but that is no reason to join them.

## Student lanes, dormitories and rented rooms

Behind the main roads are the narrow lanes where many students live in rented rooms and small apartments. These are residential alleys first and student streets second. Ride at walking pace, give way to people on foot and watch for bikes pulling out of doorways. At night keep noise down. Article 21 of the traffic law bars continuous use of the horn and any use of it from 22:00 to 05:00 in populated areas, except for priority vehicles, so rely on your headlight and a slow approach. Before turning into any narrow lane, look for the end of it, for oncoming bikes and for signs or barriers, and walk the bike if you cannot see a clear way through.

Late-evening foot traffic can be heavy near eateries and dormitory gates. Dim light and crowded kerbs are a bad combination, so reduce your speed more than seems necessary.

## Parking near campus: kerb, bay or lot

Parking near universities is a mix of marked roadside spots, small guarded lots and, near some gates, bikes crowded onto pavements. The safest choice is a guarded lot or a marked bay with a posted price and a ticket. Article 77 of the traffic law says roadways are for traffic and pavements are for pedestrians, and using them for other purposes requires permission. A pavement full of bikes is not evidence that parking there is allowed.

Hanoi's city government issued a decision on 15 October 2026 that, according to VnExpress, doubled the per-trip price ceiling for motorbikes at roadside spots built with public funds. Do not rely on older price lists you may find in student forums; check the board and ask. Lock the steering, because Nguyen Ha does not provide an extra lock, and photograph the bike. Our guide to [parking a motorbike in Hanoi](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/parking-a-motorbike-in-hanoi/) explains tickets and what to do if the bike is missing.

## Crossing the boulevards

The corridors named above are wide roads with heavy traffic, and the gap between two junctions can be long. The risk is crossing between junctions, where a rider on a wide road cannot see you until the last moment. Use the junctions, signal early and merge with the flow instead of waiting for a clear road that rarely appears. Our [guide to Hanoi intersections](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/crossing-hanoi-intersections/) explains the technique, and our guide to [Hanoi ring roads and highways](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/hanoi-ring-roads-highways-motorbikes-allowed/) explains which roads motorbikes may use.

## Ring Road 1 and the low-emission plan

Dai Co Viet is one of the roads that VnEconomy lists among the boundaries of Ring Road 1, the area where Hanoi's low-emission zone will eventually apply. As of October 2026 the first phase covers only part of Hoan Kiem ward, and in its first area motorbikes and cars are barred from 19:00 to midnight on Fridays, Saturdays and Sundays. Phase three, planned for 2028 to 2029, extends the zone across the whole Ring Road 1 area, and the city's announcement says that motorbikes that do not meet emission level 3 will not be allowed to circulate there. For a rider in 2026 this changes nothing at the campuses, but it explains why older petrol scooters may become harder to use in the inner city later.

## A half-day orientation ride from Long Bien

A sensible first outing covers one or two corridors, not all of them. From Nguyen Ha's shop the usual way west is along Nguyen Van Cu to Chuong Duong Bridge, as city buses do. Plan the rest with a map before you leave and prefer wide roads to shortcuts.

1. Pick a corridor: Dai Co Viet and Giai Phong in the south, or Xuan Thuy in the west.
2. Ride past the campus on the main road and look at the gates from a distance.
3. Park at a guarded lot or a cafe with bays and walk the perimeter.
4. Eat where students eat, which is usually near the gates, then ride back before the evening peak.

## FAQ: riding in university neighbourhoods

### Can I ride a scooter into a university campus?

Each university decides. Ask the guard at the gate, and do not assume you may enter.

### Where is the main cluster of universities?

There are several: Xuan Thuy in Cau Giay, Dai Co Viet and Giai Phong in the south and Chua Lang in Dong Da, plus other sites across the city.

### Is it all right to stop in front of a campus gate?

No. The traffic law bars stopping or parking in front of the gate of an organisation with a vehicle entrance and within 5 metres either side.

### Will the low-emission zone affect these areas in 2026?

Not at the campuses. The 2026 pilot covers part of Hoan Kiem ward, and wider phases are planned for later years.

### Is Hoa Lac a good place for a half-day ride?

No. It is far outside the urban core and needs a separate plan.
