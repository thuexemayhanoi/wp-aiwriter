Co Loa Citadel is a realistic half-day ride from Long Bien: it lies in Dong Anh, north of central Hanoi, published guides put it roughly 16 to 24 kilometres from the city centre depending on where they measure from, and most of the sights are temples, a lake and village lanes that you walk once you have parked. It is the oldest known fortified capital in Vietnam, built in the third century BC, and its earth ramparts still run through the village. Leave in the morning, plan on one to two hours on foot at the site, and be back before the afternoon heat and the evening rush. This guide explains what you will see, how to plan the route without trusting a single set of directions, where to leave the scooter and how to behave in a place that is still a working village and a place of worship.

## What Co Loa actually is

Co Loa is a spiral-shaped citadel, which is why it is also called the Snail Citadel. Vietnam's national tourism administration describes it as the oldest citadel in the country, attributed to King An Duong Vuong, with a conservation area of about 830 hectares and some 60 relics. Legend speaks of nine spirals, but only three rings of rampart survive: an outer wall of roughly 8 km, a middle wall of about 6.5 km and an inner enclosure of about 1.6 km. In places the earth walls rise several metres and the moats beside them are still visible.

The site mattered twice. It was the capital of the Au Lac state in the third century BC and, in the tenth century, the seat of Ngo Quyen. In August 2026 Vietnamese state media reported that specialists had met to build the scientific case for putting Co Loa forward for UNESCO's World Heritage Tentative List. That is a proposal being prepared, not a listing, but it explains why the site is being restored and talked about more than a few years ago.

## What to see once you arrive

Co Loa is not a single ticketed enclosure with one gate and a route. It is a village containing several relics, and the usual visit links them on foot.

- **An Duong Vuong Temple (the Upper Temple).** Built in 1687 on a hill that, by tradition, once carried the king's palace. A pair of finely carved stone dragons stands before it, and the temple houses a bronze statue and objects excavated from the site, including bronze arrowheads and pottery.
- **The Trong Thuy and My Chau well.** In front of the temple, in a semicircular lake, the well is described by the tourism administration as the highlight of the site. The water looks reddish against the lake, and local legend ties it to the tears of Princess My Chau.
- **Cao Lo Temple,** dedicated to the general said to have supervised the building of the citadel.
- **The shrine to Princess My Chau and the communal house,** both at the heart of village life.
- **Bao Son Pagoda,** a working Buddhist temple within the relic complex.

You do not need to see all of it. Two or three stops, taken slowly, give a better picture than a rushed tour.

## Planning the route

You can plan with a map application, and three habits help.

1. **Check the route type.** Some applications offer a faster route over the newer highways. Motorbikes are not allowed on every expressway-class road around Hanoi, so read [which ring roads and highways allow motorbikes](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/hanoi-ring-roads-highways-motorbikes-allowed/) and [how to check whether a navigation app suggests an expressway](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/how-to-check-if-a-navigation-app-suggests-an-expressway/) before you start.
2. **Choose the main road, not the shortcut.** Village lanes near Dong Anh can be narrow, and a map application cannot tell you which are busy with market stalls or parked vehicles.
3. **Save the destination as the temple area.** Search for the An Duong Vuong temple rather than the whole citadel, which covers hundreds of hectares and several villages.

## Parking and moving around on foot

I could not find a published, official description of visitor parking at Co Loa, so plan for the normal pattern in Vietnamese heritage villages: a guarded space near the main temple or a family-run yard beside the road, usually with a numbered ticket. Ask before leaving the scooter, take the ticket, lock the steering and keep the helmet with you or in the seat storage if it is secure. Our guide to [parking a motorbike in Hanoi](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/parking-a-motorbike-in-hanoi/) explains how guarded lots work, and the shop's rental terms ask you to park in guarded places, as it does not supply an extra lock.

Once parked, walk. Travel guides describe the temples as a short stroll from one another, and the best views of the rampart and the lake come from the path, not the saddle. A scooter in the narrow lanes between houses is both slow and unwelcome.

## Behaving well at a living relic

Co Loa is a village of residents, farmland and shrines, so a few habits matter.

- **Dress modestly for temples.** Cover shoulders and knees, and remove sun hats at the doorway.
- **Keep your voice down** near altars, and do not touch offerings or the statues.
- **Ask before photographing people** at prayer or in their homes.
- **Treat the ramparts as heritage,** not a playground. Walk on established paths and keep off planted slopes.
- **Take your litter with you,** and do not feed or hassle animals.
- **Ride slowly through the village.** Children, bicycles and elderly pedestrians share the lanes.

## When to go, and when not to

The cooler months, roughly November to March, are the most comfortable for a day outdoors, according to travel guides. Hanoi can be hot and humid in summer, and sudden downpours are normal, so read [riding in Hanoi rain](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/riding-in-hanoi-rain/) if the forecast looks changeable.

The main local festival falls in the first lunar month, centred on the sixth day, when villagers honour King An Duong Vuong. That is a fascinating time to visit, but expect crowds, ceremonies in the temple courtyards and more demand for parking, so go early and be patient. On an ordinary weekday morning the site is far quieter.

## If you are short of time

You can still make a worthwhile visit in two hours on site if you focus: park near the temple, see the lake and well, enter the Upper Temple, then walk a short rampart section if the path is clear. ## FAQ: Co Loa Citadel by scooter

### How far is Co Loa from Long Bien?

Published guides give 16 to 24 kilometres from the centre of Hanoi, and the exact figure depends on the starting point and route. Check it in a map application.

### How long should I allow on site?

One to two hours is enough for the main temple, the lake and well and a short walk. Allow more if you want to follow the ramparts.

### Is there an entrance charge?

Travel guides mention a small admission fee, but I could not confirm the current amount from an official source, so ask at the entrance.

### Can I ride a scooter inside the village?

You can ride on the village roads, but the lanes around the temples are for walking. Park and go on foot.

### Is Co Loa a UNESCO World Heritage Site?

No. In August 2026 specialists were preparing a case for the Tentative List, which is an early step and not a listing.

### What should I wear?

Comfortable shoes, light clothing that covers shoulders and knees for the temples, a hat and sun protection.
