A Hanoi intersection at rush hour looks like a collision that never quite happens. Hundreds of scooters, cars, buses and bicycles pour into the same patch of asphalt from four directions, and somehow almost everyone comes out the other side. The secret is not luck. Traffic here flows by a mix of written law and very consistent habits: steady speed, no sudden moves, gaps filled gradually, and a short horn tap to say "I am here". Once you understand both layers, crossing a junction on a rented scooter stops being frightening and becomes a matter of patience. This guide explains who has priority under Vietnamese law, how roundabouts work, how to turn left across oncoming bikes, how to merge, and what the horn is really for, as of October 2026.

## Why the chaos is more predictable than it looks

The single most important habit in Hanoi traffic is predictability. Local riders rarely stop dead, rarely swerve, and almost never accelerate hard into a space. Instead, everyone moves at a pace that lets the people around them read their intention a second or two ahead. A scooter drifting slowly to the left is telling you it wants to turn left. A rider easing off the throttle is making room. The flow works because each person adjusts a little to the person beside them, much like a crowd walking through a station.

For a newcomer, this has two practical consequences. First, your own movements must be smooth and readable: hold a line, change speed gently, and signal well before you act. Second, the most dangerous thing you can do is hesitate unexpectedly. Braking hard in the middle of a junction because a bike passes close in front of you creates exactly the surprise the system cannot absorb. Riders behind you have already planned around your speed. If you are unsure, slow down early on the approach rather than inside the intersection itself.

Pedestrians crossing on foot follow the same logic, which is why locals tell visitors to walk slowly and steadily across a street. On a bike you are part of that same moving pattern, just faster.

## The legal order of priority at a junction

Behind the habits there is a clear legal framework. The Law on Road Traffic Order and Safety, Law 36/2024/QH15, has been in force since 1 January 2025 and was later amended, with a consolidated text published by the National Assembly Office in 2026. Its rules on intersections set an order that every rider should keep in mind, even when the traffic around them seems to ignore it.

At any junction, instructions from a traffic police officer come first, then traffic lights, then signs, then road markings. Where there is no officer and no signal, the law's provision on giving way at intersections, Article 22, applies. A rider approaching a junction must watch, slow down and give way. Traffic coming from a non-priority road or a side road must give way to vehicles on the priority road or main road, whichever direction they come from. Where both roads are equal and there is no roundabout signage, you give way to vehicles approaching from your right.

In practice, the "main road" is usually obvious in Hanoi: it is the wider street carrying the steady stream, and the alley or side street feeding into it is the one that waits. A diamond-shaped priority road sign or a give-way triangle confirms it where one is posted. When you come out of a narrow lane onto a broad avenue, assume you have no priority at all and wait for a genuine gap.

Ignoring a red light or a junction officer is one of the most heavily fined offences under Decree 168/2024/ND-CP, effective from 1 January 2025 and amended by Decree 238/2026/ND-CP from 15 August 2026. Failing to give way at an intersection is also listed among the penalised offences. We do not repeat the fine bands here; our guide to [traffic fines that catch foreign riders out](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/traffic-fines-vietnam-foreign-riders/) covers them in detail.

## Roundabouts: give way to traffic on your left

Roundabouts confuse many visitors because the rule is the opposite of the general junction rule. Where an intersection is signed for circulation around a roundabout, you must give way to vehicles approaching from your left, which in right-hand traffic means the vehicles already circulating. Where there is no roundabout sign, the general rule of giving way to the right applies, even if the junction has a central island or a statue.

Traffic in Vietnam drives on the right, so you circulate anticlockwise. Approach slowly, look left over your shoulder, and join when the circulating stream leaves a gap. Inside, keep a steady speed and avoid weaving across the circle. Bikes heading for the first exit tend to stay near the outer edge; vehicles going further round move towards the centre and then work their way back out. Signal right before your exit and drift out gradually, checking your right mirror for scooters undertaking you on the inside of the curve.

Some large Hanoi roundabouts also have traffic lights at their entry points, and in that case the lights take precedence over the give-way rule. Watch for the signal heads mounted at each entry, which can be hard to spot among signs and advertising.

## Turning left across oncoming scooters

Turning left is the manoeuvre that worries most new riders, because it means crossing the path of oncoming traffic. Article 15 of Law 36/2024/QH15 sets out the basic sequence: observe, keep a safe distance from vehicles behind you, slow down, switch on the indicator, and move gradually towards the lane nearest the direction you are turning. The indicator should stay on through the whole turn, and you may only turn when it is safe and does not obstruct other road users. While turning, you must give way to pedestrians, cyclists and oncoming vehicles.

On a busy multi-lane street, move to the left side of the carriageway well before the junction, not at the last moment. Lane discipline deserves its own explanation, so here it is enough to say that your position should match your intention early. At a signalised crossing, stop behind the line on red and wait for green. Then edge forward into the junction and pause where you will not block straight-through traffic coming the other way.

From there, the Hanoi technique is a slow, continuous arc rather than a sprint. Oncoming riders see your position and indicator and will often flow around you, while you look for a gap that opens between them. Keep your speed low and constant so they can predict your path. Do not cut the corner early by riding on the wrong side of the road before the junction; locals do this constantly, but it puts you head-on with traffic that is legitimately there. If a green arrow or a dedicated left-turn phase exists, use it and the problem largely disappears.

## Turning right and the pedestrians beside you

Turning right looks easy because you do not cross oncoming traffic, but it has its own traps. Pedestrians and cyclists are often crossing the street you are turning into, and the law requires you to give way to them. Scooters also tend to undertake on the right-hand side of the road, so a quick look over your right shoulder before you turn prevents you from cutting across someone squeezing through.

Right turns on a red light are not permitted unless a sign or green arrow specifically allows it, which surprises riders from countries where the opposite is true. Wait for the green or the arrow. At many junctions the right-turn lane is separated by a small island or marked with an arrow; follow the markings and do not force your way into it from the middle of the road.

Take the turn at walking pace in dense traffic. A right turn into a narrow street often brings you straight into parked bikes, street vendors or a delivery van unloading, and you need room to stop.

## Merging into a moving stream

Merging is where Hanoi riding differs most from what many visitors know at home. Riders rarely wait for a large empty gap, because in peak traffic that gap never comes. Instead, they join gradually. A rider entering from a side road rolls slowly to the edge of the main stream, makes eye contact or at least holds a clear line, and eases in at a slight angle so that oncoming riders can see the intention and adjust by half a metre. The stream absorbs them without anyone braking hard.

To do this safely as a visitor, follow a simple routine:

1. Stop or slow right down at the mouth of the side street and check both directions, including the pavement edge where scooters sometimes run.
2. Pick a moment when the nearest approaching vehicles are scooters at moderate speed, not a bus, truck or car.
3. Indicate, then move out slowly at an angle, matching the stream's speed as quickly as you can without lurching.
4. Hold your line once you are in. Do not stop halfway across the flow.

Never merge in front of buses or trucks. Their drivers have limited visibility, cannot react quickly and will not adjust their line for a small scooter. Wait for the large vehicle to pass and then join behind it. The same applies when the stream is made up of cars moving faster than scooters; it is better to wait thirty seconds than to rely on a car driver noticing you.

## What the horn means in Hanoi and when it is banned

The horn is part of everyday communication on Hanoi streets, but its meaning differs from what many visitors expect. A short tap is rarely angry. It usually means "I am behind you" or "I am coming through on your left", and it is aimed at riders who cannot see you in their mirrors. Long, insistent blasts are less common and do carry irritation, especially from buses and trucks.

The law takes a narrower view than street custom. Under Article 21 of Law 36/2024/QH15, the horn may be used only to warn other road users when a situation could become unsafe, or to signal that you are about to overtake. Continuous horn use is prohibited, as is a horn louder than the permitted level. In residential areas and around hospitals and clinics, the horn may not be used between 22:00 and 05:00 the next morning, except by priority vehicles on duty. Using the horn in those hours in an urban or residential area is a finable offence under Decree 168/2024/ND-CP.

For a visitor, a sensible approach is to use one short tap when you are about to pass close to a rider who may not have seen you, or when approaching a blind corner in a narrow lane in daytime. Do not lean on the horn at a junction to force a way through; it gains you nothing and annoys everyone. At night, rely on lights and slower speed instead, as our guide to [riding at night in Hanoi](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/riding-at-night-in-hanoi/) explains. Also check that the horn works before you leave the shop, as part of a [10-minute rental scooter inspection](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/rental-scooter-inspection-checklist/); riding without a working horn is itself an offence.

## Unsignalled junctions and alley mouths

Most junctions in Hanoi have no lights at all. They are crossings of residential streets, alley mouths opening onto busier roads and small squares where three or four lanes meet. These are where crashes involving tourists often happen, because riders arriving from different directions each assume the other will stop.

Approach every unsignalled junction as if someone is about to appear. Slow down well before it, look right first (the side with priority under the general rule) and then left, and keep a finger over the brake. Alley mouths deserve special care: a child on a bicycle, a delivery rider or a reversing motorbike can emerge from a gap barely wider than a doorway. A short daytime horn tap before a completely blind corner is one of the legitimate warning uses the law allows.

In the Old Quarter many streets are one-way and junctions come every few dozen metres, so the same principles apply at walking pace. Our guide to [riding in Hanoi's Old Quarter](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/riding-in-hanoi-old-quarter/) covers the street layout and parking there.

## A first-week plan for building junction confidence

Nobody needs to tackle a six-way rush-hour junction on day one. Build up in stages.

On your first ride, choose a quiet time, such as mid-morning on a weekday or early Sunday, and practise on residential streets and a few simple four-way crossings. Concentrate on smooth braking, indicating early and holding a line. A gentle route along the river is a good start; see [Long Bien and the Red River: an easy first ride](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/long-bien-red-river-first-ride/).

Over the next few days, add signalised junctions on larger streets, again outside peak hours. Practise left turns until the slow arc feels natural, and spend a few minutes simply watching a busy junction from the pavement to see how riders merge and give way. Leave the busiest junctions at peak commuting times, roughly early morning and late afternoon into the evening, until you feel genuinely comfortable.

Choose a bike you can handle easily, too. An automatic scooter lets you focus on traffic rather than gears; our [rental guide](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/rental-guide/) explains the handover and what a Nguyen Ha rental includes, and you can ask at the shop in Long Bien, open 09:00–21:00 daily, for advice on easy first routes.

## Common mistakes that make junctions dangerous

A handful of errors account for most trouble at Hanoi intersections:

- **Stopping suddenly inside the junction.** Slow down on the approach instead, so you never need to brake hard mid-crossing.
- **Following the rider in front through a red light.** The bike ahead may be breaking the law or turning on an allowed arrow; your own signal is what counts.
- **Cutting left turns across oncoming traffic.** Enter the junction first, then turn in a steady arc.
- **Forgetting your right shoulder.** Scooters pass on both sides, so check before any right turn or lane shift.
- **Riding too fast for the gaps.** Moderate speed lets the stream adjust to you; speed removes that margin.
- **Looking at the phone map in the junction.** Mount the phone and decide your turn before you reach the crossing.

## FAQ: crossing and turning at Hanoi junctions

### Who has priority at a Hanoi intersection without lights?

Under Law 36/2024/QH15, traffic from a side road or non-priority road gives way to the main or priority road. Between equal roads with no roundabout sign, you give way to vehicles approaching from your right. Always slow down and be ready to yield regardless of what the rule says.

### Do I give way to the left or the right at a roundabout in Vietnam?

At a junction signed as a roundabout, give way to vehicles coming from your left, which are usually those already circulating. If the junction is not signed as a roundabout, the general give-way-to-the-right rule applies. Traffic lights at roundabout entries override both.

### Is it rude to use the horn in Hanoi?

A single short tap to warn a rider who may not have seen you is normal and generally accepted. Continuous honking is prohibited, and in residential areas and near hospitals the horn may not be used from 22:00 to 05:00. Use it sparingly and never to force your way through a crowd.

### Can I turn right on a red light in Hanoi?

Only where a sign or a green arrow allows it. Otherwise, wait for the green light. Many visitors assume right on red is permitted, and it is a common reason for being stopped or caught on camera.

### How do I turn left on a busy street without getting hit?

Move to the left side of the road well before the junction, indicate early, enter the intersection when your light is green, and then turn in a slow, steady arc through gaps in the oncoming stream. Avoid cutting the corner on the wrong side of the road.

### What is the best time for a beginner to practise junctions?

Mid-morning on a weekday or early on a weekend, when traffic is lighter. Avoid the morning and evening commuting peaks and late-night riding until you are comfortable with the basic routines.
