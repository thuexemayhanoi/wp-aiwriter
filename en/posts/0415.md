Document existing scratches by walking round the bike once with a staff member and recording every mark three ways: a wide photograph that shows where it is, a medium photograph that shows which part it is on and a close-up that shows the mark itself, with a finger or coin beside it for scale. Then ask for the same marks to be written on the contract and keep your photographs until the deposit is back in your hand. The reason is simple. A rental bike has to be returned in the condition in which it was received, apart from natural wear, so the record made at pickup is the baseline against which any later mark is judged. A mark that nobody recorded is easy to blame on the person holding the bike at the end. This guide gives the method in order, a vocabulary for naming parts, a list of the places scratches hide, advice on lighting and storage and what to do if the shop does not want to note small marks.

## Why the record matters

Vietnam's Civil Code (Law No. 91/2015/QH13, in force since 1 January 2017) treats a motorbike rental as a lease of property. The article on returning leased property says the lessee must return the property in the condition in which it was received, apart from natural wear or as otherwise agreed, and that the lessor may claim compensation if its value has dropped compared with its condition on receipt, again apart from natural wear. A separate article says the lessee is not responsible for natural wear from use but must compensate if the property is lost or damaged. As of October 2026 these are the general rules, and a rental contract fills in the details.

What that means in practice is that the bike's condition on receipt is the reference point. If the contract says nothing about a scratch and you have no photograph, you have nothing to show that it was there first. ## What counts as "existing"

Anything that was on the bike before you rode it, however small. That includes:

- **Scratches and scuffs** on painted plastic panels.
- **Dents and creases** in metal or plastic.
- **Cracks** in lenses, mirrors, panels or the seat.
- **Missing or loose parts,** such as a cover, a screw, a mirror cap or a footrest rubber.
- **Rust and stains** on the exhaust, the chain cover or the frame.
- **Tears and cuts** in the seat cover.
- **Bent levers or handlebar ends.**

You are not trying to prove that the bike is perfect or to complain about it. You are building a shared, neutral record of what is already there.

## Before you start: light, time and tools

Do this in daylight, not in a dim shop or at dusk. Scratches show best in even, bright light with no strong glare. If the shop is dark, ask to move the bike outside or to the doorway.

You do not need special tools. A phone with a working camera and enough storage is enough. A coin or your finger gives scale for close-ups, and a piece of paper and a pen let you sketch the marks. Give the process ten minutes, and do not let anyone hurry you. ## The three-photo method for each mark

For every mark you find, take three photographs.

1. **Wide.** Stand back so the whole bike, or at least a whole side of it, is in the frame. This shows which bike it is and roughly where the mark is. Include the number plate in at least some of the wide shots.
2. **Medium.** Move closer so that the part fills the frame, for example the whole left mirror or the whole front mudguard. This shows exactly which part carries the mark.
3. **Close.** Move in on the mark itself. Hold a finger or a coin next to it so that its size is visible. Focus carefully, and take the photograph at a slight angle if glare hides the scratch.

Taking three views of each mark means that anyone who sees the photographs can tell which bike, which part and how big the mark is. A single close-up of a scratch could be on any bike.

## Walk round in a fixed order

Without a route, you will miss areas. Use the same order every time.

1. Start at the **front** of the bike: headlight, front mudguard, front fairing or apron, number plate.
2. Go to the **left side** from front to back: mirror, handlebar end, lever, front panel, footrest, side panel, exhaust guard, rear mudguard.
3. Continue to the **rear:** tail light, rear panel, grab rail or rack, number plate, indicators.
4. Come up the **right side** from back to front: the same parts on the other side, including the chain cover.
5. Look at the **top:** seat, instrument panel, handlebar grips, mirrors.
6. Finish with the **underside and wheels:** both tyres, rims, the stand and the footrests.

Call out each mark as you go, for example "left mirror, scratch on the stem", so that the staff member can write it down while you photograph.

## A vocabulary for naming the parts

Describing a mark's location precisely helps if the paper says only "scratch". Use the rider's left and right, meaning the sides as you sit on the bike facing forward, and say so on the note. The table lists common parts on small manual bikes and scooters.

| Area | Typical parts |
|---|---|
| Front | Headlight, front mudguard, front apron or leg shield, front fork cover |
| Handlebar | Grips, levers, mirror stems, switch housings, handlebar covers |
| Sides | Side panels, leg-shield edges, floor mat or running board, chain cover |
| Rear | Tail light, rear panel, rear mudguard, grab rail, rack |
| Seat | Seat cover, seat hinge, seat edges |
| Lower | Footrests, exhaust and exhaust guard, side stand, centre stand |
| Wheels | Tyres, rims, valves, brake drum or disc covers |

Add a rough location, such as "upper left", "lower right edge" or "near the bottom corner", and a rough size, such as "about 3 cm long" or "thin line".

## Where scratches hide

Experienced renters check the parts that touch the ground or other things most often.

- **Footrests and their rubbers.** They scuff first when a bike is dropped.
- **The ends of the handlebars and the levers,** which scrape on a fall.
- **Mirror stems and mirror backs.**
- **The exhaust guard.** It is metal or plastic and shows scrapes.
- **The lower edges of side panels.**
- **The tail panel and grab rail,** where bags rub.
- **The seat edges,** where cuts and tears start.
- **The side stand and its spring,** which can be bent.
- **The number plate,** which can be bent or scratched.

Look at these parts closely even if the rest of the bike looks tidy.

## Record a short video as well

A video is not a replacement for photographs, but it adds a useful second record. Walk round the bike at a steady pace, filming each side, and narrate as you go: "This is the Honda Wave, plate number such-and-such, the date is today, left side, scratch on the footrest rubber." Film the odometer and the fuel gauge at the end. Keep the video short, a minute or two, and save it with the photographs.

## Put the marks on the contract

Photographs on your phone are yours. A note on the contract is shared. Ask the staff member to write each mark on the handover sheet or the contract, or sketch the bike from the side and top and mark the spots. If the form has no space, write the list on your copy and photograph the page with the staff member's signature or initials. Our guide to [reading a motorbike rental agreement in English](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/reading-a-motorbike-rental-agreement-in-english/) explains how to add notes before you sign.

Keep the wording factual. "Left mirror stem, scratch about 2 cm" is better than "mirror damaged".

## If the shop says small marks do not matter

Some staff will say "do not worry about small scratches". It is a friendly remark, but it does not replace a record. A polite way to answer is: "Thank you, I would just like them written down so we both agree what was there." The note takes a minute, and it protects the shop too, because it shows what the bike looked like when it left. If a shop refuses to note any marks at all, treat that as a signal to be extra careful with your own photographs, and consider choosing another bike or another shop.

## Back up and keep the evidence

- **Back up the same day** to a cloud account, so that a lost or broken phone does not lose the record.
- **Do not edit or filter** the photographs. Keep the originals, with their dates.
- **Keep them until the deposit has been returned** and for a few weeks afterwards.
- **Do not delete** them to free space.

## FAQ: documenting scratches on a rental bike

### Do I need to record tiny scratches?

Yes, if you can see them. A small mark that is not recorded can be mistaken for new damage.

### How many photographs should I take?

Three per mark: a wide shot, a medium shot of the part and a close-up with a finger or coin for scale.

### Should the marks be on the contract?

Yes. A note on the contract is shared, and a photograph on your phone is only yours. Photograph the signed note too.

### What if the staff say it does not matter?

Politely ask for the marks to be written down anyway. It protects both sides.

### Is a video enough?

It helps, but photographs are clearer for small marks. Use both.

### How long should I keep the evidence?

Until the deposit has been returned, and for a few weeks afterwards.
