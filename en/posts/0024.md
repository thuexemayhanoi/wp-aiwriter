Ba Vi National Park sits on the western edge of Hanoi, roughly 60 kilometres from the city centre, and it is one of the few genuine mountain rides you can finish in a single day on a rented scooter. The short answer is simple: leave the city on ordinary roads such as National Road 32 through Son Tay, follow the signs toward the park gate near Tan Linh, then ride the winding park road of about 12 kilometres up to the parking area near the 1,100-metre level and walk the last stretch to the temples. What makes the trip different from a flat day ride is that final climb. It is narrow, steep in places, often wrapped in cloud and much cooler than the city. This guide covers which approach roads a motorbike may actually use, what happens at the park entrance and how to handle the summit road in both directions.

## Why Ba Vi works as a single-day mountain ride

Most mountain destinations around Hanoi ask for an overnight stop. Mai Chau, for example, crosses a pass on a busy national road and is better treated as a two-day trip, as our [Hanoi to Mai Chau motorbike guide](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/hanoi-to-mai-chau-by-motorbike/) explains. Ba Vi is different because the mountain is still inside Hanoi's boundaries. You can leave after breakfast, spend several hours on the mountain and be back before the evening rush, provided you start early and keep the plan modest.

The ride has three distinct parts. First comes an urban exit through western Hanoi, which is the most tiring section for many visitors. Second is a stretch of semi-rural road past Son Tay, with farmland, dairy country and small towns. Third is the park road itself, which rises from the lowland forest through several marked elevation levels to the upper zone. Each part needs a slightly different riding mindset, and treating them separately helps you judge whether to continue or turn back.

## Two motorbike-legal ways out of western Hanoi

As of October 2026, the Road Traffic Order and Safety Law, effective from 1 January 2025, keeps ordinary motorcycles and mopeds off expressways. That matters on this trip because the fastest line on many car-focused maps uses roads a scooter may not enter. Before you start, read our note on [how to check if a navigation app suggests an expressway](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/how-to-check-if-a-navigation-app-suggests-an-expressway/), and set your app to avoid highways.

The simplest and most commonly recommended route is National Road 32. From the Cau Giay side of the city it runs northwest through Nhon and Phung toward Son Tay. It is an ordinary national road with shops, junctions and buses, so it is slower than it looks on a map, but it has no expressway sections to stray onto. At Son Tay, travel guides describe continuing past the bus station area and following signs toward Ba Vi and the national park.

The second option uses the side roads of Thang Long Avenue, which runs west toward Hoa Lac. This can be quicker, but only if you stay in the correct lanes, as explained in the next section. Riders unsure about lane rules should simply use Road 32 both ways.

## Thang Long Avenue: main carriageway versus service roads

Thang Long Avenue is the road older guides still call the Lang to Hoa Lac highway. Its central carriageway is built to expressway standard and carries fast car and truck traffic. Motorcycles are not allowed in those central lanes. Vietnamese press reports describe traffic police stopping riders who drift into the car lanes, and the penalty for a motorcycle entering an expressway under Decree 168/2024/ND-CP is significant, as our page on [traffic fines that catch foreign riders out](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/traffic-fines-vietnam-foreign-riders/) describes.

Motorbikes use the parallel service roads on each side of the avenue. These behave like normal suburban roads, with junctions, two-way motorbike traffic in places and occasional roadworks. The practical problem is continuity. Service roads can end, merge or detour around interchanges, and some travel guides warn that riders have to leave the avenue and use local roads for part of the way. If the lane you are in suddenly feels like a motorway, with no side traffic and high speeds, you are probably in the wrong place. Do not reverse or stop on a ramp. Continue to the next lawful exit and reroute.

Construction around major junctions on western Hanoi's ring roads has also caused temporary diversions in 2025 and 2026. Follow posted detour signs rather than the line on your screen.

## From Son Tay to the park gate

Beyond Son Tay the traffic thins and the landscape opens out into fields, hills and dairy farms. Travel guides describe taking the provincial road from Son Tay toward Da Chong and watching for a sign to turn left into Ba Vi National Park, or reaching the Tan Linh junction and riding a few more kilometres to the entrance. Signs to the national park are generally clear, but the exact turn you meet depends on which way you came.

A note on names: Hanoi reorganised its communes and abolished district-level administration on 1 July 2025, so some official addresses now differ from older guidebooks that place the park in Tan Linh commune, Ba Vi district. Road signs and local people often still use the familiar names. Use the national park itself as your navigation target rather than a commune name.

This lowland section is also your last easy chance to buy petrol. Several guides stress that there is no fuel station inside the park, and the climb uses noticeably more fuel than flat riding. Fill up before the gate. Our article on [fuel in Hanoi and filling up a rental scooter](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/fuel-in-hanoi-petrol-stations-rental-scooter/) explains how stations work if you have not refuelled in Vietnam before.

## What happens at the park entrance

The main gate has a ticket booth where visitors buy an entry ticket, and vehicles pay a separate parking or vehicle charge. Fees change from time to time and differ between adults, students and children, so check the current notice at the booth rather than relying on figures from an old blog post. Keep your ticket with you; staff may check it further up.

Opening hours are reported inconsistently by different sources, with some describing an early morning opening and a late afternoon close and others listing longer hours. The safe approach is to arrive in the morning and plan to be off the mountain well before dusk, whatever the posted times say. Riding the summit road in fading light and fog is the situation you most want to avoid.

Motorbikes are allowed on the park road up to the upper parking area. Large coaches are restricted at lower elevation levels, which is one reason the upper road feels quieter than the approach. Respect any temporary closures, which can follow heavy rain or special events, and follow instructions from park staff and forest rangers.

## The 12 km summit road: gradients, hairpins and cloud

The road from the gate to the upper zone is roughly 12 kilometres long, paved and generally well maintained, but it is narrow and winds through dense forest with many tight bends. Cars, minibuses, cyclists and walkers all share it. On a busy weekend you will meet vehicles coming downhill around blind corners, so keep firmly to your side and never cut a bend.

Cloud is the defining feature of Ba Vi. The upper slopes are frequently in mist even when Hanoi is clear, and visibility can drop to a few dozen metres within minutes. The surface becomes damp, fallen leaves collect on the inside of corners, and painted lines get slippery. Switch your lights on, slow down before the cloud rather than inside it and leave extra braking distance.

Temperatures near the top can be several degrees lower than in the city. Bring a light jacket even in summer and a proper warm layer in winter. Rain gear is worth packing in any season, because you can ride from sunshine into drizzle on the same climb. Our guide to [riding in Hanoi rain](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/riding-in-hanoi-rain/) covers what to wear.

## The elevation markers: 400, 600, 800 and 1,100 metres

Vietnamese visitors describe places on the mountain by their elevation level, and you will see these numbers on signs. Around the 400-metre and 600-metre levels there are camping areas, resorts and picnic spots, making them sensible first stops to check how the bike and passenger are coping. Some travellers stay overnight at these levels instead of riding up and back in one day.

Around the 800-metre level is the ruined French-era church, a roofless stone shell partly reclaimed by the forest. It is one of the most photographed places in the park, especially when mist drifts through the empty window frames. The area also contains other remains from the colonial hill station, a reminder that Ba Vi was once a cool-season retreat.

The road ends around the 1,100-metre level, where there is a parking area for motorbikes and cars. Park only in the designated area, lock the steering and take valuables with you. From here, the remaining ascent is on foot.

## Walking to the Upper Temple and the summit

From the upper parking area, paths and stone steps lead to two main sites. The Upper Temple on Tan Vien Peak, reported at roughly 1,226 to 1,227 metres, honours the mountain deity Tan Vien Son Thanh and draws pilgrims throughout the year. The highest point, Vua Peak at about 1,296 metres, holds the temple dedicated to President Ho Chi Minh. Reaching it involves a long staircase through forest, described in hiking guides as more than a thousand steps.

Allow realistic time for this. The steps are steep, uneven and slippery when wet, and many visitors underestimate how tiring they are after a morning on the bike. Wear proper shoes, not sandals, and carry water. Dress respectfully at both temples, with shoulders and knees covered, and keep voices low. On clear days the view stretches over the plains toward the Red River and the Da River, but on many days you will see only cloud, which is part of Ba Vi's character.

## Choosing a bike that copes with the climb and the descent

The climb itself is within the ability of a normal 110cc to 125cc scooter or manual bike with one rider. Two adults on a small automatic will make it, but slowly, with the engine working hard on the steepest bends. A guide written for foreign visitors suggests knowing how to ride in first gear on the way up, which points toward a manual or semi-automatic for anyone carrying a passenger. Our comparison of the [Honda Wave and Yamaha Sirius](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/honda-wave-vs-yamaha-sirius-rental/) explains how these geared bikes behave.

The descent deserves more thought than the climb. On a manual bike, select a low gear and let engine braking control speed, adding the brakes gently. On an automatic scooter you rely almost entirely on the brakes, so use both levers progressively, stop to let them cool if they start to feel soft, and never coast downhill at speed. A 50cc scooter or a low-range electric scooter is not a good match for this road.

## A realistic day plan from Hanoi

Leave Hanoi early, ideally soon after the morning rush has eased, so that you reach the park gate by late morning. Allow around one and a half to two hours each way for the lowland ride, depending on your starting point and traffic, and do not trust car-based time estimates. Spend the middle of the day on the mountain: a stop at the lower levels, the church, then the upper parking area and the walk to the temples.

Begin the descent in the early afternoon. That leaves a margin for fog, a slow walk down the steps or a coffee stop, and still gets you back to the city before dark. Remember that a daily rental runs for 24 hours from pickup, and late returns are charged at VND 30,000 per hour for one to five hours, so build the return time into your plan rather than racing to beat it.

Weekends and public holidays bring heavy visitor numbers, particularly in late autumn when wild sunflowers bloom along the lower slopes and at the start of the year when many pilgrims visit the temples. A weekday visit is calmer on the summit road.

## FAQ: riding to Ba Vi National Park

### Can I ride a scooter on Thang Long Avenue to Ba Vi?

Only on the parallel service roads, not the central expressway-standard carriageway. Service roads are not always continuous, so if you are unsure about lane rules, use National Road 32 through Son Tay instead.

### How long is the road inside the park?

The paved park road from the gate to the upper parking area is roughly 12 kilometres. It is narrow and winding, and it climbs to around the 1,100-metre level, from where you continue on foot.

### Can I ride all the way to the summit?

No. Motorbikes stop at the parking area near the 1,100-metre level. The Upper Temple and the temple on Vua Peak are reached by steps and footpaths.

### Is there petrol inside the national park?

Guides consistently report that there is no fuel station inside the park. Fill the tank in the lowland area before you reach the gate.

### Is an automatic scooter good enough for the climb?

A 110cc to 125cc automatic will manage with one rider, and with two riders more slowly. The main concern is braking on the descent, so ride down gently and give the brakes time to cool.

### When is the best time of year to visit?

Spring and autumn usually offer the most comfortable riding. Late autumn brings wild sunflowers, while the summit is cold and often misty in winter. Check the weather forecast for the mountain, not just for Hanoi.
