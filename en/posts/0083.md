Sandals and flip-flops are the most common footwear on Hanoi's scooters and the worst match for a semi-automatic motorbike. A bike with a toe-operated gear lever and a foot-operated rear brake asks your feet to do precise work, and a thin strap, an open toe or a slipping sole makes that work harder and a fall more costly. Honda Vietnam's safe-riding training document is direct: footwear should cover the toes and heel. This guide explains the specific risks of riding a Honda Wave or Yamaha Sirius in open footwear, from changing gear to braking to a low-speed fall, what the law says (it is silent on footwear), what happens if the shoe comes off and what to wear instead, including cheap, practical options. It is general advice, not a medical or legal opinion.

## What your feet do on a semi-automatic bike

On a scooter, your feet rest on a flat floor and do very little. On a semi-automatic bike, the left foot operates the gear lever and the right foot operates the rear brake pedal. Honda's Wave RSX FI manual says to press the shift lever gently with the toe until it moves, to reduce the throttle before shifting, and to avoid resting a foot on the lever. Honda's training document says the middle of the sole should rest on the footrest, the right foot should stay ready on the brake pedal and the left foot should not press down on the gear lever.

All of that assumes your foot is held firmly in a shoe. In a loose sandal, the foot can slide on the footrest, the toe may miss the lever or catch under it and the heel may drop off the pedal at the moment you need it.

## Risk 1: the gear lever

Changing gear with the toe needs a firm, controlled tap. In a flip-flop, your toes grip the strap, not the lever, and the sandal can flex or slip. A common result is a missed or half-completed shift, a lever that bends your toe back or a shift that feels vague. The lever can also bruise or cut a bare toe if you press too hard or if it snaps back. A closed shoe with a stiff toe cap protects against that. In stop-and-go traffic, where you change gear many times, the risk repeats.

## Risk 2: the rear brake pedal

The rear brake pedal is on the right, and Honda says to keep the foot ready on it. A sandal that slips off the pedal under hard braking leaves you without the rear brake at the moment you need it, or leaves your foot dangling near the wheel. Honda's braking sequence calls for both brakes to be used together, so a missed rear brake changes the balance of the stop. A sole with grip and a strap that holds the foot in place matter here.

## Risk 3: the exhaust, chain and moving parts

The exhaust pipe on a small bike runs close to the right foot and can reach high temperatures. Honda's manual warns that luggage and anything flammable should be kept away from the exhaust. A bare ankle or foot that touches it can burn. The chain and rear sprocket are close to the left foot, and a loose strap, a dangling flip-flop or a trailing lace can catch. A sandal that falls off at speed can also be thrown into the rear wheel or the chain.

## Risk 4: falls and drops

Most rental falls happen at low speed, in a turn, on a wet patch or when the bike tips at a stop. In a fall, the foot is often the first thing between the bike and the ground. A bike that weighs around 100 kilograms trapped on a bare foot can crush toes, and a slide along the road scrapes skin off an uncovered foot in seconds. A closed shoe does not make you invulnerable, but Honda's advice to cover the toes and heel exists because those are the parts most often hurt.

When you put a foot down at a stop, a slippery sole on a wet or oily surface can skid, and a sandal that comes off leaves you with a bare foot on hot or dirty asphalt. In the rainy season, a wet sandal is slippery on a wet footrest.

## Risk 5: walking and parking

Sandals are comfortable for walking, and that is why many visitors wear them. But riding includes mounting, dismounting and pushing the bike in and out of crowded parking rows. Honda's training document describes the steps for getting on and off safely, and for moving a bike with the engine off. A bare toe under a rolling wheel or a kicked-out stand is easily injured. ## What the law says about footwear

We read Article 7 of Decree 168/2024/ND-CP, which sets the fines for motorbike riders, in full on the Government portal, and found no offence for riding in sandals or open shoes as of October 2026. Decree 238/2026/ND-CP, which amends the decree from 15 August 2026, does not change Article 7. So you will not be fined for flip-flops. The risk is physical, not legal. ## What to wear instead

Honda Vietnam's training document lists protective clothing for gear bikes: shoes that cover the toes and heel, gloves, a long-sleeved jacket, long trousers such as jeans or leather and bright or reflective clothing. For feet, the simple rule is closed shoes with a firm sole.

Good options for Hanoi:

- **Light trainers** with a flat, grippy sole and a closed toe. They are cheap, comfortable and easy to find.
- **Lace-up shoes** that stay on, tied tightly.
- **Light hiking shoes** if you plan hills and rough roads.
- **Slip-on closed shoes with a heel strap,** as a minimum if you dislike laces.

Avoid:

- **Flip-flops, slides and thong sandals.** They have no hold.
- **Open sandals with a thin strap.** They protect nothing.
- **High heels and platform soles.** They catch the footrest.
- **Loose, long laces** that can reach the chain, which you should tuck in.

Our guide to [motorbike footwear for Hanoi: comfort versus protection](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/motorbike-footwear-for-hanoi-comfort-versus-protection/) covers the trade-offs in heat, rain and walking.

## The rest of Honda's protective list

Feet are only part of the picture. The same Honda document that asks for covered toes and heels also lists gloves, which improve grip and reduce injury in a fall, a long-sleeved jacket, long trousers such as jeans or leather, and bright or reflective clothing. In Hanoi's heat most visitors will not wear all of it, but a thin long-sleeved top and light long trousers cost little in comfort and protect skin that would otherwise be scraped on asphalt. If you can only improve one thing, make it the shoes, because the feet and ankles sit nearest the ground and the bike's moving parts.

## If you only have sandals

If you arrive with only sandals, buy a cheap pair of closed shoes before you rent. A pair of trainers costs less than most things you will buy on a trip, and it removes a real risk. If you must ride a short distance in sandals, choose a secure style with a heel strap, ride slowly, change gear gently and keep your foot clear of the exhaust. Do not ride long distances, in rain or on hills in them.

If you ride an automatic scooter instead, the risks are lower, because there is no gear lever, but the same advice applies in a fall. ## FAQ: sandals on a semi-automatic bike

### Is it illegal to ride in sandals in Vietnam?

We found no footwear offence in Article 7 of Decree 168/2024/ND-CP as of October 2026. The risk is injury, not a fine.

### Why does Honda say footwear should cover toes and heel?

Because those are the parts most likely to be hurt in a fall or by the gear lever and the pedal. Honda's training document lists it among basic protective gear.

### Can I change gear in flip-flops?

You can, but it is harder and riskier. The strap can slip and the lever can hurt a bare toe.

### What shoes are best?

Closed, light shoes with a grippy sole that stay on your feet, such as trainers.

### Does the rental shop provide shoes?

### Will sandals affect my insurance?

They might if your policy has conditions on protective gear. Check your policy.
