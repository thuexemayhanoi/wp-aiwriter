If your rental bike gets a flat tyre, refuses to start or you lose the key somewhere in Hanoi, the order of action is simple: get yourself and the bike safely out of the traffic, phone the rental shop before any repair begins, and only then let a roadside mechanic touch the bike, with the price agreed first. Hanoi is one of the easiest cities in the world to break down in, because small repair stands sit on almost every street, but a quick fix arranged in a hurry can still turn into a misunderstanding about cost or responsibility. This guide walks through punctures, engines that cut out, lost and broken keys, what to say about money, and when a problem stops being mechanical and becomes an emergency, as of October 2026.

## The first minute: get yourself and the bike out of the flow

Most problems announce themselves gently. A puncture shows up as a wobbly, heavy feeling in the steering or a rear end that slides from side to side; a fuel or electrical fault usually means the engine coughs and dies. Whatever it is, signal, slow down and move to the right-hand edge of the road, then onto the pavement or into an alley mouth if you can do so without riding through pedestrians. Do not stop in the middle of a junction or on a bridge if you can coast a few more metres to a safer place.

Under Article 18 of the Law on Road Traffic Order and Safety (Law 36/2024/QH15, effective from 1 January 2025), a driver whose vehicle has to stop because of a technical fault and is left partly on the carriageway, or where stopping is not allowed, must warn other road users with hazard lights or a warning sign behind the vehicle. Many small scooters have no hazard switch at all, so the practical answer on a motorbike is to push it completely off the carriageway. Switch off the engine, take the key, put the bike on its stand and step away from the traffic before you do anything else.

## Call Nguyen Ha before anyone picks up a spanner

Once you are safe, phone the shop: 0334 699 969 (or +84 334 699 969 from a foreign SIM), staffed from 09:00 to 21:00 daily. Explain where you are, what happened and what the bike is doing. A clear photo of the tyre, the dashboard or the spot where you are parked helps more than a long description, and a map pin sent by message is better still. The shop knows the bike, its tyres and its history, and can often tell you whether the problem is something a corner mechanic can fix or something to leave alone.

This call matters for a second reason. Who pays for a puncture, a new key or a repair depends on your rental contract and on what caused the problem, and this guide cannot answer that for you. Ask the shop, check the contract you signed, and keep any receipt. The [rental guide](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/rental-guide/) and the [FAQ](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/faq/) explain the general terms; your own contract is what applies. If you call outside opening hours, leave a message, make the bike safe and wait rather than authorising major work on your own.

## Flat tyres: finding a roadside repair stand

The roadside repair business in Hanoi is everywhere. Look for a hand-painted sign reading "sua xe" (motorbike repair) or "va xe" (tyre patching), a small compressor and a stool by the kerb, or a row of inner tubes hanging from a tree. Many stands are a single mechanic working from the pavement in front of a house; others are proper workshops with a lift. In the Old Quarter and in residential streets of Long Bien and Gia Lam it is unusual to push a bike more than a few hundred metres before finding one.

Push the bike rather than riding it on a flat tyre. Even a short distance on a flat can cut the tyre's sidewall or, on a tube-type tyre, chew the inner tube to pieces, turning a small patch into a full replacement. If you really cannot push it, ride at walking pace in first gear or with the throttle barely open, at the very edge of the road. A local translation app or a photo of the tyre shown to the mechanic is usually enough to explain what you need.

If the tyre is only soft rather than flat, it may simply need air. Every repair stand can pump a tyre, and a soft tyre should be pumped before you ride on, not after. The [ten-minute rental scooter inspection](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/rental-scooter-inspection-checklist/) shows how to check tyre pressure by feel at handover, which prevents a good share of roadside stops.

## Tubeless plug or new inner tube: what the mechanic will actually do

Most modern automatic scooters, such as the Honda Vision, run on tubeless tyres. For a nail or screw in the tread, the usual roadside fix is a rubber plug pushed into the hole from outside, which takes a few minutes and lets you ride on straight away. Some older bikes and some manual models use tyres with inner tubes; there the mechanic takes the wheel off or levers the tyre aside, finds the hole and either patches the tube or fits a new one. Ask the shop on the phone which kind your bike has.

A plug or a patch is a sound repair for a small hole in the tread. It is not the right answer for a cut in the sidewall, a tyre torn by riding flat, or a damaged rim. In those cases the tyre or tube needs replacing, and that is exactly the kind of decision to make with the shop on the line, because the type and size of tyre matter and the cost is higher. Mention any repair when you return the bike so the shop can look at it properly, and keep the nail if the mechanic hands it to you.

## The engine coughs, stalls or will not start

Before you assume a breakdown, rule out the obvious. Check the fuel gauge: an empty tank is the most common reason a rental scooter stops, and the [fuel guide for Hanoi](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/fuel-in-hanoi-petrol-stations-rental-scooter/) explains how much petrol you are handed and where to fill up. Make sure the side stand is fully up, because many scooters cut the engine or refuse to start with the stand down. On most automatic scooters, hold one brake lever while pressing the starter button; on many models the electric starter will not turn without it.

If the starter clicks weakly or not at all, the battery may be flat, often after the lights or the ignition were left on. Manual bikes such as the Honda Wave usually have a kick-starter, and many automatics also have one or can be started that way; the shop can tell you over the phone. If the engine turns over but will not catch after a few tries, stop. Repeated cranking drains the battery, and if the bike has been through deep water the engine may be wet inside; our guide to [riding in Hanoi rain](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/riding-in-hanoi-rain/) covers stalls in floods.

Warning lights, strange smells, smoke, a grinding noise from the brakes or a snapped throttle or brake cable are all reasons to stop riding immediately. Do not try to limp home on a bike whose brakes or steering feel wrong. Describe the symptom to the shop, and let them decide whether a nearby mechanic should look at it or whether the bike should not be ridden at all.

## Lost key, broken key or a key that will not turn

A lost key is not a mechanical breakdown, but it can strand you just as completely. Before you panic, retrace your steps: cafes, guarded parking lots and hotel receptions often keep a key that was left in a bike or dropped nearby, and the parking attendant who gave you a ticket is a good first person to ask. If the key is gone, call the shop and tell them where the bike is parked and whether it is in a guarded lot.

Do not ask a street locksmith to cut a new key, force the ignition or bypass the lock, even if one offers. Some newer scooters, including recent versions of popular Honda models, use a smart key fob with an immobiliser, and a forced lock can damage the ignition and the bike's security. Any replacement key and the question of who pays for it belong to the shop and your contract, so leave the bike secured where it is and wait for instructions.

A key that will not turn usually means the steering lock is jammed against the handlebars. Wiggle the handlebars gently left and right while turning the key, without forcing it. If the key snaps or bends in the lock, leave the pieces as they are and phone the shop. Nguyen Ha does not provide an extra lock, so locking the steering and parking in guarded places is part of looking after the bike; the [parking guide](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/parking-a-motorbike-in-hanoi/) explains how the guarded lots work.

## Agree the price before work starts

Roadside mechanics in Hanoi are generally honest and fast, but they are used to customers who know the going rate. Prices for the same job vary by street, by the part used and by what the mechanic finds once the wheel is off, so this guide deliberately gives no figures. What protects you is a short, polite routine: ask what the problem is, ask what they plan to do and ask the total price before any work starts. Use a calculator or a phone screen so the number is written down and both of you can see it.

If the mechanic finds a second problem halfway through, ask for the new total before agreeing to it. Anything beyond a simple patch, plug or pump-up, such as a new tyre, a new tube, a battery or a part, is worth a quick call to the shop first; they may prefer to arrange it themselves. Pay in cash, keep small notes ready, and ask for a written note of what was done and what it cost, or at least take a photo of the old part and the bike. That receipt is useful when you return the bike and talk to the shop about it.

## When the bike lets you down outside central Hanoi

Problems on a day trip are handled the same way, but the distances change. Villages and towns around Hanoi have repair stands too, while quiet country roads, dyke roads and hill passes can have long gaps between them. Before leaving the city, check whether your rental terms allow the trip at all, as explained in [taking a rental motorbike out of Hanoi](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/take-rental-motorbike-out-of-hanoi/), and save the shop's number somewhere other than your phone in case the battery dies.

Never try to solve a breakdown by pushing a bike along an expressway: motorbikes are barred from expressways under Law 36/2024/QH15, and the narrow emergency lanes are for cars and lorries. If a navigation app has led you onto a road where scooters are not allowed, leave at the next exit on foot if you must and call the shop; our guide on [checking whether a navigation app suggests an expressway](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/how-to-check-if-a-navigation-app-suggests-an-expressway/) helps you avoid it in the first place.

After dark, a breakdown on an unlit road is more dangerous than the fault itself. Push the bike well off the road, stay visible with your phone torch, and if a local mechanic or a passer-by offers help, accept the help but still phone the shop before agreeing to anything beyond air or a patch.

## When it is an emergency: 113, 114 and 115

A breakdown becomes an emergency the moment someone is hurt, a vehicle is on fire or you feel unsafe. Vietnam's emergency numbers are 113 for the police, 114 for fire and rescue and 115 for medical emergencies, with 112 for search and rescue. Calls to emergency numbers are free. Operators may not speak English, so give the street name, a nearby landmark and the number of people injured as simply as you can, or ask a Vietnamese bystander to make the call.

If you have been in a collision, stay at the scene, keep injured people still and away from traffic, and do not move vehicles involved in a serious crash unless they are causing further danger. Photograph the scene and the vehicles, then phone the shop. The police will ask for your licence, the registration certificate and the compulsory insurance certificate that travel with the bike; the [licence rules for foreigners](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/motorbike-licence-rules-for-foreigners-in-vietnam/) explain why a valid licence matters here too.

## Habits that make roadside stops rarer

Most breakdowns on a city rental come from a handful of causes: low tyres, an empty tank, a battery drained by lights left on, a stand left down, or water in the engine after a flooded street. The habits that prevent them are equally short. Press the tyres before each day's ride. Glance at the fuel gauge whenever you park. Switch the ignition fully off and take the key every time you get off. Ride around deep standing water instead of through it.

Keep the shop's number saved as a contact, carry your phone charged, and keep a little cash for a patch or a pump. Photograph where you parked, including the parking ticket if there is one. None of this takes more than a minute a day, and together these habits remove most of the reasons you would ever need to look for a repair stand.

## FAQ: breakdowns, punctures and keys on a Hanoi rental

### What should I do first if my rental scooter breaks down in Hanoi?

Move yourself and the bike completely off the road, switch off the engine and take the key. Then phone the shop on 0334 699 969 before any repair, explaining where you are and what happened, and send a photo or map pin if you can.

### Can I get a flat tyre repaired at any roadside stand?

Yes, small repair stands are found on most Hanoi streets and can usually plug a tubeless tyre or patch an inner tube. Push the bike there rather than riding on the flat, and ask the shop by phone before agreeing to a new tyre or tube.

### Who pays for a puncture or a lost key on a rental bike?

That depends on your contract and the cause, so this guide cannot say. Call Nguyen Ha between 09:00 and 21:00, check the contract you signed and keep any repair receipt to show when you return the bike.

### How much does a puncture repair cost in Hanoi?

Prices differ by street, by tyre type and by what the mechanic finds, so we do not quote a figure. Ask for the total price before work starts, have it written on a phone or calculator, and ask again if extra work is proposed.

### Should I let a locksmith make a new key for the bike?

No. Forcing or bypassing the ignition can damage the lock, and some newer scooters use a smart key with an immobiliser. Leave the bike locked in a safe place, ideally a guarded lot, and let the shop decide how to replace the key.

### Which emergency numbers work in Vietnam?

Dial 113 for the police, 114 for fire and rescue and 115 for an ambulance; 112 is the search and rescue number. Calls are free, but operators may not speak English, so a landmark and a nearby Vietnamese speaker help.
