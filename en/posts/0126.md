An automatic scooter can turn in a much smaller space than a car, but a Hanoi alley can still be narrower than the circle the scooter needs for a U-turn. Honda Vietnam's published specifications list length, width and wheelbase for its scooters but, on the pages I could read, no minimum turning radius, so the practical answer comes from geometry and a short test on the actual bike. A rough rule: the tighter the alley, the more you rely on a slow speed, full steering lock and your feet, and the less you rely on the throttle. Researchers describe about 90 percent of Hanoi's alleys as under 4 metres wide, which is why this guide explains what turning radius is, which dimensions limit a scooter, how to test your bike and when to stop and walk it round.

## What turning radius means

The minimum turning radius is the radius of the smallest circle a vehicle can trace when the steering is turned to its limit. It is normally measured to the outer wheel, and the full width of the vehicle, including the handlebar ends and any luggage, needs more room than the wheel path alone.

Three things limit how sharply a scooter turns:

- **Steering lock.** The angle at which the front fork or the handlebar meets its stop.
- **Wheelbase.** The distance between the axles. A longer wheelbase needs a wider circle.
- **Rider and load.** A heavy bag on the front or a passenger changes balance, and a scooter at walking pace is easier to tip than one at speed.

A scooter that looks small can still have a wide turn if the steering lock is limited. This is why published radius figures, when they exist, are more helpful than looks.

## What the manufacturer publishes

Honda Vietnam's product pages give the dimensions that set the limits. For the Lead ABS the page lists a length of 1,844 mm, a width of 714 mm and a wheelbase of 1,273 mm. For the Air Blade the page lists 1,887 mm, 686 mm and 1,286 mm. The Honda Lead has a 12-inch front wheel and a 10-inch rear wheel, and the Air Blade uses 14-inch wheels. The pages I read do not state a minimum turning radius.

Some third-party sites do list radius figures for individual scooters. A Wikipedia entry for the Honda Vision, for example, gives 1.94 m, but it does not say how that was measured or for which version. Treat any such figure as an order of magnitude and not as a promise about the bike you are handed. The reliable method is to measure the bike yourself, and the next sections show how.

## How narrow Hanoi alleys really are

Alleys in northern Vietnam are called ngo, and the narrower branches off them ngach. An academic overview published by the International Institute for Asian Studies reports that about 88 percent of Hanoi's residents live in alleyway neighbourhoods and that 90 percent of Hanoi's alleys are less than 4 metres wide. The same article mentions one Hanoi alley, on Giai Phong Street, that residents widened from 1.2 metres to 2 metres by contributing parts of their own land. Reporting on the Old Quarter describes passages, off streets such as Hang Buom and Ngo Gach, that are only wide enough for a single person.

Put those numbers beside the scooter's own width. A Lead is about 0.71 m wide and an Air Blade about 0.69 m, before mirrors or bags. A 2-metre alley therefore leaves room for the scooter and not much more, and a 1.2-metre alley leaves almost none, particularly once a rider's elbows and knees are counted.

Many alleys are also shared. Pedestrians, children, parked bikes, delivery carts and shop displays use the same strip. The turning radius that matters in practice is the one you can use without moving anything or anyone.

## A simple turning-circle test

You can measure the real turning circle in a quiet car park or empty street in about ten minutes, ideally with a helper and a tape measure or pieces of tape on the ground.

1. **Pick a flat, dry, empty area** with no traffic. Avoid gravel and smooth tile.
2. **Ride slowly in a straight line, then turn the handlebars fully to one side** and keep riding at walking pace in a circle.
3. **Have the helper mark where the front wheel touches the ground** in a few places on the circle, or mark where the outer edge of the handlebar passes.
4. **Measure the diameter** across the circle. The radius is half of that.
5. **Add room for your body and the handlebars.** The space you actually need is wider than the wheel path.
6. **Repeat on the other side,** because many scooters turn more sharply one way.

A simple example, not a specification: if your test circle measures about 4 metres across, then in a 4-metre alley a one-motion U-turn would leave no margin at all. Those are the situations where the right decision is a three-point turn or a walk-round, not a bigger throttle.

## Technique for slow, tight turns

Tight turns at walking pace are about balance more than power. The main points:

**Look where you want to go.** Look through the turn to the exit, not at the wall or the pedestrian. The bike tends to follow the eyes.

**Keep the speed very low and steady.** Honda's owner's manual tells riders to open the throttle slowly to accelerate and to close it fully when braking. In a tight turn that means a small, steady throttle opening and no sudden changes.

**Use both brakes.** Honda's manual says to use the front and rear brake levers together. The Lead has a combined braking system, which Honda says distributes force between front and rear depending on which lever is used. Feather the brake gently, not abruptly, and do not grab the front lever with the handlebars at full lock.

**Keep your feet ready.** At walking pace you can put a foot down to steady the scooter. Keep your knees relaxed and lean the body slightly into the turn, not the handlebars.

**Do not turn the bars to full lock at a standstill unless you have to,** and never from a stop with a load hanging from the front. Full lock with the front wheel loaded is when a scooter drops.

**Switch off what you do not need.** Honda's manual describes an Idling Stop system on the Lead, which stops the engine at a standstill and restarts it when the throttle is twisted. In a tight alley with repeated stops and starts, ask the shop how the system behaves on your bike and whether the switch can be set to the Idling position.

## When to turn, when to reverse, when to walk

A quick decision guide for the moment you reach a tight junction or dead end:

| Situation | Better choice |
|---|---|
| Alley wider than about two scooter lengths, clear of people | U-turn at walking pace, feet ready |
| Alley a little wider than the scooter, dead end ahead | Stop, put the stand down or hold the bike and walk it round |
| Narrower than the scooter plus elbows | Do not enter; park outside and walk |
| Crowded with pedestrians or delivery carts | Wait, or walk the scooter |
| Corner with no sightline | Stop before the corner, check, then go |
| Slope plus wet surface | Walk the scooter |

Walking a scooter is not a defeat. Hold the left grip and the rear handle, lean the bike slightly toward you and push at a walking pace, with the engine off. Honda's manual describes using the rear grab handle when lifting a scooter onto its centre stand, and the same handle helps when you steer the bike by hand.

If you must reverse, do it with the engine off and your feet. Automatic scooters have no reverse gear.

## Why alleys are more than a space problem

A tight turn rarely goes wrong because the circle is too small. It goes wrong because something else is in the way:

- **Blind corners.** Another rider or a child can appear without warning. Slow down well before the turn.
- **Slippery surfaces.** Wet tiles, loose gravel and the smooth top of a drain cover reduce grip. Our guide to [riding in Hanoi rain](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/riding-in-hanoi-rain/) lists the streets and surfaces that stay slick.
- **Parked bikes and shop stock.** Handlebar ends clip them first. Leave a hand's width.
- **Overhead items.** Washing lines, awnings and cables can catch a taller rider or a helmet.
- **Doors and steps.** A step that looks small can unseat a 10-inch rear wheel.

The wider approach to narrow streets is covered in our guide to [riding in the Old Quarter](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/riding-in-hanoi-old-quarter/), and the article on [crossing Hanoi intersections](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/crossing-hanoi-intersections/) explains how traffic flows into and out of narrow side streets.

## Choosing a scooter for tight places

The two dimensions that matter most in an alley are width and wheelbase. By Honda's figures the Air Blade is about 28 mm narrower than the Lead but has a wheelbase about 13 mm longer, so the difference on paper is small. A smaller or lighter scooter is generally easier to handle in a tight space, and the shop can tell you what is available. Our comparison of [Honda Vision and Air Blade](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/honda-vision-vs-air-blade-which-scooter-to-rent-in-hanoi/) discusses how each one handles in city traffic.

Remember that parking space is a limit as well. Our guide to [parking a motorbike in Hanoi](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/parking-a-motorbike-in-hanoi/) describes guarded lots and the street rules, and the shop does not provide an extra lock, so lock the steering and park somewhere guarded.

## FAQ: turning radius and alleys

### What is the turning radius of a Honda Lead or Air Blade?

Honda Vietnam's product pages do not give a figure. Measure it with the test above on the bike you receive.

### Can a scooter do a U-turn in a 3-metre alley?

It depends on the bike, the rider and the surface. Test your turning circle first. If it is close to the alley width, do a three-point turn or walk the scooter.

### Should I use the rear brake in slow turns?

Honda says to use both brake levers together. On a bike with a combined braking system the rear lever also acts on the front, so squeeze both gently.

### Is it better to put a foot down?

At walking pace, yes. A foot on the ground adds a safety margin.

### Can I reverse a scooter?

Not with the engine. Switch off and push it backwards on foot.

### What if the alley is too narrow?

Park outside in a guarded place and walk in.
