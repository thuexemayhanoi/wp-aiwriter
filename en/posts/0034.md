Hanoi's big roads look confusing on a map because the same name can cover two very different things. Ring Road 2 and Ring Road 3 each have an elevated deck reserved for cars and a ground-level road where motorbikes are allowed. Thang Long Avenue has a fast central carriageway closed to motorbikes and parallel service roads that are open to them. The formal expressways leaving the city, such as Phap Van–Cau Gie, Hanoi–Hai Phong and Noi Bai–Lao Cai, are closed to motorbikes along their whole length. This guide explains which part of each road a rental scooter may use, which roads to take instead, and how to recognise the signs at the ramps. It reflects rules and reporting reviewed as of October 2026; temporary closures change often, so the signs on the day always take priority.

## The short answer: one road name, two different rules

The most useful idea to carry with you is that Hanoi's major corridors are usually built in layers. An upper or central layer is designed for fast car traffic with few junctions. A lower or outer layer carries local traffic, including motorbikes, and connects to ordinary streets with traffic lights and turns.

When a navigation voice says "continue on Ring Road 3", it may mean either layer. For a motorbike rider the distinction is everything. The elevated deck and the expressway-style central lanes are off limits; the ground road below or beside them is the legal route.

A rough rule of thumb for Hanoi in 2026:

- **Elevated ring-road decks** (Ring Road 2 and Ring Road 3): no motorbikes.
- **Ground-level ring roads** under or beside those decks: motorbikes allowed, following local lane markings.
- **Thang Long Avenue central lanes**: no motorbikes; use the service roads.
- **Formally designated expressways** (cao toc in Vietnamese): no motorbikes at all.
- **Most Red River bridges**: motorbikes allowed in a marked lane or on a dedicated deck, but arrangements differ bridge by bridge.

This article focuses on the road network itself. If your problem is a phone that keeps routing you onto a car-only road, read our separate guide on [how to check if a navigation app suggests an expressway](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/how-to-check-if-a-navigation-app-suggests-an-expressway/).

## What the law says about motorbikes and expressways

Vietnam replaced its 2008 road traffic law with two new laws that took effect from 1 January 2025: the Road Traffic Order and Safety Law (Law 36/2024/QH15) and the Road Law (Law 35/2024/QH15). The long-standing principle did not change. Expressways are reserved for certain motor vehicles that can maintain expressway speeds, and two-wheeled motorcycles and mopeds are not among them. The Traffic Police's own explainer on expressway rules summarises the same exclusion of motorbikes, mopeds, bicycles and pedestrians, with narrow exceptions for vehicles maintaining the road.

Penalties are set by Decree 168/2024/ND-CP, also effective from 1 January 2025, and amended during 2026. Riding a motorbike onto an expressway sits in one of the higher fine brackets for two-wheelers, and legal-information sites report that it can also lead to licence point deductions. Rather than quote a figure that may have shifted with the amendments, treat it simply as a serious offence that police actively enforce at Hanoi's ramps.

The elevated ring-road decks and Thang Long Avenue's central carriageway are restricted by Hanoi's traffic organisation decisions and posted signs rather than by being named expressways. The practical result for a rider is the same: if a sign bans motorbikes, you may not enter, and riding there is treated as a lane or sign violation. Our overview of [traffic fines that catch foreign riders out](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/traffic-fines-vietnam-foreign-riders/) explains how stops and penalties usually work.

## Ring Road 3: elevated deck closed, ground road open

Ring Road 3 is the outer urban ring on the western and southern side of the city. Its best-known stretch runs as an elevated road from the Mai Dich area in the north-west, south along Pham Hung and Khuat Duy Tien, then east along Nguyen Xien toward Phap Van, where it meets the expressway heading south. Beyond that the ring continues across the Red River on Thanh Tri bridge.

Motorbikes are not allowed on the elevated deck. Vietnamese media have reported repeated police enforcement against riders who took a ramp onto it, including fines, licence suspension and temporary seizure of the bike. The ground-level roads beneath it, including Pham Van Dong, Pham Hung, Khuat Duy Tien and Nguyen Xien, are ordinary urban roads where motorbikes are permitted.

On the ground road you will meet traffic lights, U-turn points and heavy rush-hour congestion, especially around the junctions with Nguyen Trai, Tran Duy Hung and the Mai Dich interchange. Stay in the right-hand lanes where motorbikes are directed, and watch for on-ramps branching off to the left or up the middle. Those ramps lead to the car-only deck. Missing your turn on the ground road is a minor inconvenience; following a car up a ramp is not.

## Ring Road 2 and the elevated section near Nga Tu So

Ring Road 2 is the inner ring, running through busy inner neighbourhoods and crossing the Red River at Vinh Tuy and Nhat Tan in its wider loop. The elevated section between Nga Tu Vong and Nga Tu So, along Truong Chinh, opened in November 2020. Hanoi's traffic plan at the time stated that the elevated deck would carry cars only, with motorbikes, non-motorised vehicles and pedestrians barred.

The ground-level Ring Road 2 below remains open to motorbikes under the usual signals and lane markings. Media reports since opening have described riders trying to escape Truong Chinh's rush-hour jams by riding up the ramps despite clear prohibition signs, and police stepping up patrols in response.

If you are riding between the Nga Tu So area and the south-east of the city, plan for the ground road and accept that it will be slower. The elevated section was built to separate fast car traffic from local traffic, and that separation is exactly why it is closed to you.

## Thang Long Avenue: central carriageway versus service roads

Thang Long Avenue runs west from the Tran Duy Hung area toward Hoa Lac and beyond. It is often described as an expressway in casual English, and its central carriageway is managed like one: cars only, with motorbikes, bicycles and pedestrians excluded. Reports have described the central lanes as having a maximum car speed of 80 km/h, while motorbikes use the parallel service roads (duong gom) on each side.

The service roads are not always simple. In 2024 sections were temporarily closed because of flooding after a typhoon, and in 2025 construction near the junction with the planned Ring Road 3.5 narrowed parts of the service road. Media reported riders squeezing into the car lanes to avoid the barriers, and police issuing fines. The lesson is to expect delays on the service road and to keep using it anyway.

If you are heading west toward Hoa Lac, the Vietnam National Village for Ethnic Culture and Tourism, or further toward Ba Vi, allow extra time for the service roads and the junctions where they rejoin local streets.

## Expressways leaving Hanoi that ban motorbikes

Several formal expressways radiate from Hanoi. None of them may be used on a rental scooter:

- **Phap Van–Cau Gie** (south, toward Ninh Binh and beyond). Motorbikes have been formally barred since February 2012 and directed onto the old National Highway 1 via Ngoc Hoi instead.
- **Hanoi–Hai Phong** (east, toward Hai Phong and the coast). The ordinary route for motorbikes is National Highway 5 and connecting provincial roads.
- **Noi Bai–Lao Cai** (north-west, toward Lao Cai and Sapa). Riders use national highways and provincial roads instead; on long northern trips that usually means a much longer journey time than the expressway would suggest.
- **Hoa Lac–Hoa Binh** (west). Motorbike riders use National Highway 6, which also serves Mai Chau.
- Newer expressways opened around the capital follow the same rule.

Do not confuse "expressway" with "toll road" or "national highway". Some national highways have tolls and still allow motorbikes, and some ring-road sections without tolls ban them. ## Crossing the Red River on a scooter

Because Nguyen Ha Motorbike Rental is in Long Bien, on the east bank, many customers cross the Red River on their first day. Bridge rules differ, so it helps to know the main options before you set off.

**Long Bien bridge** is the historic iron bridge. It carries trains, motorbikes, bicycles and pedestrians, not cars, which makes it a natural first crossing for scooter riders. **Chuong Duong bridge** next to it carries general traffic including motorbikes, although it has had periodic restrictions for heavy vehicles and repairs.

**Vinh Tuy bridge** is part of Ring Road 2. After its second phase opened in 2023, motorbikes and non-motorised vehicles were assigned a mixed lane next to the right-hand railing, separated from the car lanes by a hard divider and with a lower speed limit.

**Thang Long bridge** in the north-west has two levels. The upper deck carries car traffic; the lower level carries the railway and lanes for motorbikes and bicycles. Notices during repair works in 2020 confirmed that motorbikes continued to use the lower level while the upper deck was closed.

**Nhat Tan bridge** connects toward Vo Nguyen Giap road and Noi Bai airport. Reporting on its lane arrangement describes motorbikes using the outermost lane on the bridge section, then being separated onto service roads further toward the airport. **Thanh Tri bridge**, which carries Ring Road 3, has a layout that is less clear-cut for visitors; check the signs at the approach rather than assuming either way.

## Reading the signs at a ramp

Vietnam's road signs follow the national technical standard QCVN 41:2024/BGTVT, which applies from 2025. Three sign types matter most for this topic.

**Prohibition signs** are round, with a red border and white background. P.104, which shows a rider on a motorbike, means no motorbikes. A related sign, P.111a, shows a moped without a rider and is used to ban mopeds. Legal commentators do not all agree on exactly how the two divide 50cc mopeds and larger motorbikes, so if you see either one at a ramp, treat it as closed to your rental scooter.

**Combined signs** such as P.105 ban both cars and motorbikes and are often used at temporary closures.

**Expressway direction signs** use a green background with white lettering. The start-of-expressway sign shows the expressway name or number and its speed limits. If the signs ahead suddenly turn green and show a motorway-style symbol, you are approaching an expressway. Blue direction signs are used on other roads.

Hanoi also uses lane-control signs above multi-lane roads, showing which vehicle types may use each lane. On roads such as Vinh Tuy bridge and parts of the ring roads, a motorbike symbol over the right-hand lane is your instruction, not a suggestion.

## Planning a legal route out of the city

The simplest approach is to plan routes around ground-level roads and national highways rather than trying to work out which fast roads you might be allowed on. A few practical habits help:

1. Before setting off, identify the specific bridge you will use to cross the river and the ground road you will follow.
2. Learn the names of two or three roads on your route, so you recognise the correct one on a sign.
3. Expect the motorbike route to be slower and longer than the car route. That is normal.
4. Avoid joining traffic at an interchange you do not understand. Stop somewhere safe and check first.
5. Ride in daylight when leaving the city for the first time. Our guide to [riding at night in Hanoi](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/riding-at-night-in-hanoi/) explains why ramps and service roads are harder to read after dark.

## If you find yourself on a banned road

Mistakes happen, especially at large interchanges. If you realise you are on a ramp or deck that bans motorbikes, do not reverse, do not make a U-turn across traffic and do not stop suddenly in a live lane. Continue carefully, keep to the far right, and leave at the first lawful exit.

If police stop you, stay calm, switch off the engine and show your documents. Do not argue about the sign; explain that you made a navigation mistake and follow their instructions. Any penalty is set by law and paid through official channels, not on the roadside.

## FAQ: motorbikes on Hanoi's fast roads

### Can I ride a motorbike on Ring Road 3 in Hanoi?

Yes, on the ground-level road. The elevated deck of Ring Road 3 is closed to motorbikes, and police enforce this at the ramps. Follow Pham Van Dong, Pham Hung, Khuat Duy Tien and Nguyen Xien at street level.

### Is Thang Long Avenue an expressway?

Its central carriageway operates like one and is closed to motorbikes. Riders must use the service roads on either side, which can be slower because of junctions and construction.

### Does a bigger motorbike change the rules?

No. The expressway ban applies to motorbikes in general, not to small engines only. A large-capacity bike is still not permitted on Hanoi's expressways or the car-only elevated decks.

### Which bridge should I use to cross from Long Bien to the Old Quarter?

Long Bien bridge or Chuong Duong bridge are the usual choices for scooter riders. Long Bien bridge carries no cars, which makes it calmer, although it can be narrow and busy at peak times.

### Can I ride to Noi Bai airport on a motorbike?

Yes, using roads open to motorbikes. Reporting on the Nhat Tan–Noi Bai corridor describes motorbikes sharing the outer lane on part of the route and being directed onto service roads on another part. Follow the lane signs closely.

### Are the rules different at night?

The motorbike bans on elevated decks and expressways apply at all hours. Separately, some decks and bridges close overnight to all traffic for maintenance, so check for temporary notices before late rides.
