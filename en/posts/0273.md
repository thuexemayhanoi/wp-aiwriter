Battery percentage tells you how much energy is left in the battery, while estimated range is a prediction of how far that energy might take you if you keep riding the way the bike assumes you will. Percentage is a measurement, range is a forecast, and the forecast can swing as your speed, load, road and temperature change. On a rental electric scooter in Hanoi the sensible habit is to trust the percentage, treat the range figure as a rough guide, and test the real consumption yourself on a short ride before planning a longer one. This article explains why the two numbers disagree, what a manufacturer's rated range really means, how to work out your own kilometres per percentage point with a simple method and which warnings matter at the low end. The detailed figures come from VinFast's published material for particular models, so they are examples, not promises for the bike you rent.

## Two numbers, two different questions

The percentage answers: "How full is the battery?" The range answers: "If conditions stay like this, how far can I go?" These sound similar, but they are not the same kind of number.

- **Percentage (state of charge)** is an estimate of the energy remaining relative to a full battery. It does not depend on how you ride; two riders on the same bike see the same percentage. The battery management system works it out from voltage, current and other data, so it is also an estimate, but it measures the battery.
- **Estimated range** takes the energy left and multiplies it by an assumed efficiency, such as the average of your recent riding or a fixed standard figure. Change the assumption and the range changes, even if the battery has not.

The VinFast Evo 200 manual's description of its instrument cluster lists remaining battery capacity and charging status, with the capacity bar flashing below 20 per cent and the battery symbol flashing below 10 per cent. The manual's description does not list a range figure in kilometres, though the phone app or another model may show one. So on some bikes you will see only a percentage or bars, and on others both. Ask the shop what your bike shows.

## Why the range figure is a guess

The energy a scooter uses per kilometre is not fixed. The US Department of Energy's Alternative Fuels Data Center, writing about electric vehicles in general, notes that efficiency and driving range vary substantially with driving conditions, that rapid acceleration reduces range compared with gradual acceleration, and that hauling heavy loads or climbing significant inclines can reduce it as well. A US Department of Energy analysis adds that speed, acceleration, deceleration, idle time and road grade all affect energy consumption and range, and that much of the temperature effect on cars comes from cabin heating and cooling, which a scooter does not have. These are findings about cars, so use them as a pattern, not as numbers for a two-wheeler.

For a rented scooter in Hanoi, the inputs that move the estimate are:

- **Speed.** Faster riding uses more energy per kilometre. A rated range measured at a steady, gentle speed flatters a bike ridden faster.
- **Acceleration.** Repeated hard starts away from lights draw more than smooth ones.
- **Load.** A passenger and luggage mean more mass to move.
- **Slopes.** Flyovers and bridges such as Long Bien cost more energy going up, even if some is not recovered coming down.
- **Stop-and-go traffic.** Frequent restarting takes energy, while time spent idle in a jam uses little.
- **Battery condition and temperature.** VinFast's manual says that battery performance is reduced when the temperature is above 40 degrees Celsius or the charge is below 30 per cent, and that battery capacity gradually declines over time through normal wear.
- **Starting point.** A bike that left the shop at 80 per cent starts the day with a shorter range than one at 100 per cent, whatever the dashboard claims.

## What "rated range" really means

When a brochure gives a range, it comes from a test with defined conditions. VinFast's English brochure for the Evo 200 and Evo 200 Lite lists 203 and 205 km per charge, and the manufacturer's FAQ for older models explains that its quoted distances (120 km for the Klara S, 70 km for the Impes and 75 km for the Ludo) are the maximum at a speed of 30 km/h. VinFast's Indonesian page for the Evo, a different model from any in a Hanoi rental fleet, says that its displayed distance is an estimate with a fully charged battery and under test conditions at a constant 30 km/h.

Three points follow:

1. **The rating comes from a steady 30 km/h test,** not a ride through Hanoi's junctions with a passenger.
2. **A different model's footnote does not describe your bike.** The Indonesian Evo is a different product, and the older models' figures are older still. They show how the industry measures, not what a particular rental will do.
3. **Real riding can land above or below the rating.** Gentle city riding may come close, while fast riding, hills and loads usually fall short. The wise assumption is a cushion, with a short test to calibrate it.

## Measure your own kilometres per percentage point

This takes five minutes and turns two vague numbers into one useful one. The figures below are an invented example to show the arithmetic.

1. **Note the starting percentage and the trip distance** (the trip meter, or the odometer reading) at the start of an ordinary ride, for example 90 per cent.
2. **Ride about 10 km** in the sort of conditions you expect for the day.
3. **Note the percentage and distance again,** for example 78 per cent after 10.0 km.
4. **Divide.** The battery fell 12 points over 10 km, so roughly 0.83 km per point.
5. **Multiply by the usable points.** If you want to keep 20 per cent in reserve and you have 78 per cent, you have 58 points, which is about 48 km of comfortable riding at that rate.

Repeat later in the day because consumption changes with traffic and slope. If your route will include a long climb, or a passenger rides with you, assume more energy per kilometre. The method also protects you against a range figure that seems optimistic, because you are using your own data. If the bike shows only bars, count how many bars disappear over a known distance instead.

## When the numbers seem to disagree

| What you see | Common explanation | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Range falls faster than the percentage | The estimate assumes recent heavy use | Ride smoothly; use percentage as the main guide |
| Percentage drops quickly on a climb, then steadies | Higher power draw uphill | Expect a lower reading on a hill |
| Range rises after you slow down | The estimate follows your recent efficiency | Do not rely on it to gauge a fast section |
| Range reads very high at the start | It may reflect a rated figure or a gentle past ride | Calibrate with a short test |
| Percentage and range both drop at pickup compared with the shop's figure | Battery rested, or bike used since | Photograph the screen; ask the shop |
| Percentage jumps after a few minutes' rest | Battery voltage recovers once load is removed | Wait a minute and re-read |
| Bars and percentage do not seem to match | Bars are coarse steps | Use the percentage if the bike shows both |

These are typical patterns, not rules for every bike; only the maker can explain a specific display.

## The low end: warnings that matter

The VinFast manual says that when the battery falls below 30 per cent, the bike's performance is reduced to protect the battery, the capacity bar flashes below 20 per cent and the battery symbol flashes below 10 per cent. If your bike behaves similarly, the sensible reading is as follows:

- **About 30 per cent:** stop treating the day as open-ended. Plan where you will charge.
- **About 20 per cent:** go and charge, or head back to the shop.
- **Below 10 per cent:** stop at the first safe place and call, instead of trying to get "a bit further".

The manual also advises that keeping the charge between 20 and 80 per cent suits the battery's life and says not to store the bike for long at under 20 per cent, which is a good reason to ask the shop what level it expects at return. ## Planning a day with both numbers

1. **Start with the percentage at pickup,** photographed, and the range figure if the bike shows one.
2. **Pick the longest realistic route,** and add detours and return legs.
3. **Convert to percentage points** using your calibration ride, as above.
4. **Keep a reserve** of at least 20 per cent unless the shop gives different guidance.
5. **Check halfway.** If the percentage is dropping faster than planned, shorten the route.
6. **Charge before you are in trouble,** not after the warning starts flashing.

## FAQ: battery percentage and estimated range

### Which should I trust, percentage or range?

Trust the percentage as the main measure of what is left. Use the range as a rough guide that changes with your riding.

### Why does the range change when the battery has not?

Because it is calculated from assumptions about how you ride, and those change with speed, load and road.

### Is the manufacturer's rated range achievable in Hanoi?

It depends on the model and on how you ride. Ratings usually come from steady low-speed tests, so check by measuring.

### How much reserve should I keep?

A common approach is 20 per cent, and VinFast's manual warns at 20 and 10 per cent. Ask the shop for its advice for your bike.

### Does a heavy rider or a passenger reduce the range?

Generally yes, since more mass takes more energy. No exact figure is available for any particular bike.

### Why did the percentage change after the bike sat for a while?

Battery voltage and the estimate can settle after a rest. A big unexplained drop is a reason to tell the shop.
