Do not ride a scooter if a medicine makes you drowsy, dizzy, slow or unable to concentrate, or if the label or pharmacist warns against driving or operating machinery. Many everyday medicines can do this, including some allergy tablets, travel-sickness pills, strong painkillers, sleeping tablets and anti-anxiety drugs. Law 36/2024/QH15 requires operators of vehicles to meet the health conditions suited to their vehicle (Article 59) and bans operating a vehicle with banned narcotic substances or stimulants in the body (Article 9). Health guidance in the United Kingdom adds a practical rule: if you feel sleepy or cannot concentrate, you must not drive, whether the medicine was prescribed or bought over the counter.

This guide explains why medicines affect riding, which types to check, how to read a label and ask a pharmacist in Vietnam, what the published guidance says about timing, what to do if you have already taken something, what the law says and what to tell the shop. It is general information based on the law and published health guidance as of October 2026, not medical advice. Ask a doctor or pharmacist about your own medicines.

## Why medicines matter more on a scooter

A scooter asks a lot of your attention. You balance the bike, watch for vehicles from every direction, judge gaps and react in a fraction of a second. A medicine that slows your reactions or narrows your attention by even a little can turn a near-miss into a crash. Reviews of medicines and driving performance find impaired performance with a range of sedating medicines, and note that drivers using sedating antihistamines may not perceive that they are impaired, since the usual signs of drowsiness, such as yawning, may be absent even when performance is affected.

Medicines also add up. One health leaflet notes that taking several sedating medicines, or adding alcohol, increases the effect. Alcohol and some medicines together can raise drowsiness, which is one more reason to follow our guide to [avoiding alcohol and impairment before riding](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/avoiding-alcohol-and-impairment-before-riding/).

## Types of medicine to check

The lists below come from United Kingdom hospital and National Health Service guidance and a review of published studies. They are examples, not a complete list, and brand names in Vietnam may differ from those abroad, so check the active ingredient on the box.

| Type | Examples named in published guidance | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| **Drowsy (sedating) antihistamines** for allergies, colds and itching | chlorphenamine, diphenhydramine, hydroxyzine, promethazine, cinnarizine | Can cause drowsiness, even the next day |
| **Less drowsy antihistamines** | loratadine, fexofenadine, acrivastine, cetirizine | Less likely to cause sleepiness, but cetirizine has been linked with slight impairment in some studies, so check how you feel |
| **Strong painkillers** | codeine, tramadol, morphine, oxycodone and others | Drowsiness, slowed reactions |
| **Anti-anxiety medicines and muscle relaxants** | diazepam, lorazepam and similar | Sedation, slowed reactions |
| **Sleeping tablets** | temazepam, zopiclone and similar | Can leave you drowsy the next day |
| **Nerve-pain medicines and some antidepressants** | gabapentin, pregabalin, amitriptyline and others | Drowsiness, dizziness |
| **Anti-sickness and travel-sickness medicines** | cyclizine, prochlorperazine, some hyoscine products | Drowsiness, blurred vision |
| **Anti-inflammatory painkillers** | ibuprofen, naproxen and similar | Dizziness or drowsiness in some people, according to the review |
| **Cold remedies with several ingredients** | products combining an antihistamine, a decongestant or a painkiller | Check each ingredient |

Other side effects matter too: blurred vision, dizziness, slowed movement, loss of focus and low blood sugar or blood pressure can all make riding unsafe, according to a pharmacist's guide on medicines and driving.

## How to check a medicine in Vietnam

1. **Read the label.** The leaflet or box may warn against driving or operating machinery. Labels in Vietnam are often in Vietnamese. If you cannot read it, photograph the box and use a translation tool, or ask the pharmacist.

2. **Note the active ingredient.** The generic name, which is written in Latin letters on most boxes, is the same worldwide. Use it for the table above and when you speak to a pharmacist.

3. **Ask the pharmacist.** Say that you will be riding a scooter and ask whether the medicine can make you drowsy, dizzy or slow, and whether there is a less sedating alternative. A pharmacist can often suggest one.

4. **Ask a doctor about prescribed medicines,** especially if the dose has changed.

5. **Tell the pharmacist everything you take,** including supplements, other medicines and alcohol. A pharmacist's guide advises telling them about all medicines, including over-the-counter products and supplements.

6. **Do not stop prescribed medicine on your own.** The same guide says not to stop taking medicines, but to speak to your doctor or pharmacist about side effects.

## What the guidance says about timing

Guidance written for patients in the United Kingdom, for specific groups of medicines, includes several rules of thumb that are worth knowing, although they were written for UK drivers and for those medicines, so treat them as a guide and not a rule for every medicine.

- **Do not drive if you feel sleepy or cannot concentrate.** A hospital pharmacy leaflet says this plainly.

- **Be careful when you start a medicine or change the dose.** A hospital patient leaflet advises avoiding driving for the first five days after starting or changing the dose of certain strong medicines. Another hospital leaflet notes that drowsiness may wear off after a short time, but that you should wait until it has passed.

- **Leave time after a strong painkiller.** A hospital pharmacy leaflet suggests not driving for at least three hours after a strong painkiller if you feel sleepy, and then assessing whether you are fit.

- **Night-time sedatives can last into the next day.** The same leaflet warns that people who take sedating medicines at night may still be drowsy the following day.

- **Do not combine with alcohol.** The leaflets and the review note that alcohol adds to drowsiness.

- **Do not ride in bad conditions or at night** until you know how a medicine affects you, as one leaflet suggests for those who feel confident to drive.

The best test is how you feel. If you feel drowsy, dizzy, foggy, shaky or slow, you are not fit to ride, whatever the leaflet says.

## What the law says

Article 59 of Law 36/2024/QH15 requires operators of vehicles on the road to meet health conditions suitable to the type of vehicle, and Article 9 bans operating a vehicle with law-banned narcotic substances or stimulants in the body. A prescribed or over-the-counter medicine is not a banned substance in itself, but riding while impaired by it can still lead to serious consequences if you cause an accident, and officers can test operators for alcohol and drugs, as our guide to [what to do if a traffic officer stops your motorbike](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/what-to-do-if-a-traffic-officer-stops-your-motorbike/) explains. Read the current text of the law and decree before relying on a rule.

## If you have taken something and need to get somewhere

- **Do not ride.** Take a taxi or a ride-hail car, a bus or a lift.

- **Wait until the effect has passed,** and for strong medicines, longer, as the guidance above suggests.

- **Ask the shop to hold the bike or arrange a later time.** The shop's published terms say a late return costs VND 30,000 per hour for the first one to five hours and one extra rental day from six hours, and that advance notice does not automatically waive it. Our guide to [how to avoid missing your agreed return time](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/how-to-avoid-missing-your-agreed-return-time/) explains the options, and your safety is worth more than a fee.

- **Do not ride as a passenger on a bike driven by someone else who is impaired.**

- **If you start to feel drowsy while riding,** stop at the first safe place. Our guide to [what to do if you feel fatigued on a long ride](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/what-to-do-if-you-feel-fatigued-on-a-long-ride/) explains where and how.

## Travel sickness and mountain roads

Travel-sickness tablets are a common reason for drowsiness among visitors. If you take one for a car or bus journey, do not ride on the same day unless a pharmacist confirms that it is safe. Passengers may take them, but the rider should not. ## A quick checklist

1. **Read the label, and note the active ingredient.**

2. **Ask a pharmacist before you ride: can this make me drowsy, dizzy or slow?**

3. **Be extra careful after starting a medicine or changing a dose.**

4. **No alcohol with drowsy medicines.**

5. **If you feel sleepy, dizzy, foggy or slow, do not ride.**

6. **Choose a taxi, a ride-hail car or a bus, and tell the shop.**

7. **Stop at the first safe place if you feel drowsy on the road.**

## FAQ: medicines and riding

### Can I ride after taking an allergy tablet?

It depends on the tablet. Some antihistamines, such as chlorphenamine and diphenhydramine, are known to cause drowsiness, while others, such as loratadine and fexofenadine, are less likely to. Ask a pharmacist, and do not ride if you feel sleepy.

### Does Vietnamese law ban riding on medicine?

Article 9 of Law 36/2024/QH15 bans operating a vehicle with law-banned narcotic substances or stimulants in the body, and Article 59 requires operators to meet suitable health conditions. A medicine that impairs you is a safety and responsibility issue even where it is not a banned substance.

### How long should I wait after a strong painkiller?

A UK hospital leaflet suggests not driving for at least three hours after a strong painkiller if you feel sleepy, and avoiding driving for the first days after starting or changing the dose. Ask your doctor about yours.

### Can a pharmacist in Vietnam help?

Yes. Show the box, say that you will be riding a scooter and ask about drowsiness and alternatives.

### What if I take medicine every day?

Ask your doctor whether it affects riding, and do not stop it without advice.

### Can I take travel-sickness pills and ride?

Check first. Many cause drowsiness, so riders should not rely on them without a pharmacist's advice.
