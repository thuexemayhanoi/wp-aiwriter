Comfort on an electric scooter over uneven Hanoi streets depends on four things you can influence: correct tyre pressure, healthy suspension, a relaxed riding position and speed that is slow enough to let the suspension work before you reach the bump. The scooter's small wheels and compact frame mean sharp edges such as patched asphalt, manhole covers and speed bumps are felt more than on a larger motorbike, but a well-set-up bike ridden smoothly handles them without drama. This guide explains why a rental scooter feels harsh in some places, what to check at pickup, how to ride rough patches with less jarring and when a thud or wobble is a fault to report. The specifications quoted come from VinFast's Evo 200 owner's manual, which covers one electric motorbike model, so treat the numbers as an example and ask the shop for the figures of the bike you actually get.

## Why uneven roads feel harsher on a small electric scooter

Few visitors think about the road surface until they hit it. Typical trouble spots on city streets include patched trenches where utilities were dug up, raised or sunken manhole covers, speed bumps and ramps, drain grates, kerb cuts, railway crossings and loose gravel at the edge of a lane. This list is a general description of urban roads, not a survey of any particular street. Surfaces change from one month to the next, and rain makes everything harder to read.

Three features of a small electric scooter make these defects more noticeable:

- **Small wheels.** The Evo 200 manual lists 90/90-12 tyres, which sit on 12-inch wheels. A small wheel drops into a hole more deeply than a larger one and has less sidewall to cushion an edge.
- **Heavy, rear-biased weight.** The manual gives a kerb weight of 100 kg including the battery, with 43 kg on the front wheel and 57 kg on the rear. The extra mass at the back goes straight into the bump when the rear wheel drops.
- **A quiet drivetrain.** With no engine note, you hear the road, and a thump seems louder than it would on a petrol bike. Some of what feels rough is simply that nothing masks the noise.

None of this makes a rental scooter unsuitable. It means that setup and technique count for more than they do on a bigger bike.

## What the scooter gives you to work with

The manual lists suspension at both ends, a seat height of 750 mm and a ground clearance of 150 mm. It also says that a working suspension contributes to a smooth ride and that you can judge how springy it is by feel while riding. A seat of that height lets many adults put their feet near the ground, which helps when you need to steady the bike over a rough patch. Ground clearance of 150 mm is a measurement, not a promise: take kerbs and steep ramps at an angle and slowly.

The same manual gives the permitted load, including rider, passenger and everything carried, as 130 kg, and allows two people. Load changes comfort. A heavier load compresses the suspension further, so the same bump feels harder, and the manual says tyre pressure should be set for speed and for the weight of the rider, passenger and luggage. If you plan to ride with a passenger or carry luggage, tell the shop at pickup. Our guide to [carrying a passenger and luggage on a scooter](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/scooter-for-two-riders-and-luggage-hanoi/) covers practical limits, although it is written about petrol scooters.

## Tyre pressure: the cheapest comfort setting

Tyre pressure is the first thing to check, and it matters for comfort as much as for safety. The Evo 200 manual states pressures of 200 kPa at the front and 225 kPa at the rear for its 90/90-12 tyres. It says the pressure must be checked and adjusted before riding, with the tyres cold, meaning at the same temperature as the surroundings, and set according to speed and load.

Practical points for a renter:

- **Do not let air out to make the ride softer.** An under-inflated tyre squashes into rim-edge hits, can be damaged by sharp edges and handles badly. A bike that feels harsh is better served by slower speed than by less air.
- **Do not assume the figures above are for your bike.** Different models and tyres have different pressures, often printed on a label on the bike. Ask the shop to show you.
- **Ask for the pressure to be checked before you leave.** A tyre that has been sitting low for a few days makes every bump worse. The shop can inflate it and tell you the value.
- **Look at tyre condition.** The manual advises checking for cuts, bulges and sharp objects, and notes that tyres have wear indicators. This model uses tubeless tyres.

## Check the suspension before you go

Staff should be present for this check, and the bike should be upright on a flat surface. The manual's front-fork check is:

1. Look at the fork tubes for damage and for oil leaks.
2. Hold the bike upright and firmly, apply the front brake and press the front end down several times.
3. Watch whether the fork compresses and rebounds smoothly.

If you see an oil film on a fork tube, hear knocking or feel the fork stick or slam back, the manual tells owners to take the bike to an authorised dealer. For a renter, the equivalent is to ask for another bike. The rear suspension cannot be judged by eye, so note anything odd and ask a mechanic.

A short test ride on a plain stretch shows more. Roll over a small manhole cover or a low bump at walking pace. The bike should settle in one motion, without a repeated bounce, a clunk from the front or a feeling that something bottomed out.

## Riding technique that reduces the jolt

Technique costs nothing. The points below are general good practice for small-wheeled bikes, together with what the manual says about throttle and brakes.

- **Look ahead and choose a line.** Scan a few seconds in front for patches, covers and edges. Early notice lets you pick a smoother line without swerving, which matters in dense traffic.
- **Slow down before the obstacle, not on it.** Braking hard on the bump compresses the front fork and can unsettle the bike. Brake early, then roll over it with the brakes released.
- **Cross edges as square as you can.** A diagonal edge, such as a drain slot or a rail at an angle, can pull the front wheel sideways. Aim to meet it close to a right angle as traffic allows, and slow down more if you cannot.
- **Keep a relaxed grip.** A tight grip passes every impact to your wrists and shoulders. Soft elbows and loose fingers let the bars move a little.
- **Be smooth with the throttle.** The manual advises adjusting the throttle to the terrain and increasing gradually when pulling away. A jerky throttle on a rough patch worsens the pitching.
- **Use both brakes together.** The manual says to apply both brakes at once and not too abruptly. On this model the right lever works the front wheel and the left lever the rear, so check which way round your bike is at handover.
- **Stay seated.** A scooter has a floor deck, not footpegs, so keep your weight low and your feet flat, as the manual advises.

## Posture and seat comfort

Comfort on rough roads depends on where your body is as well as on the bike. The manual's safety advice includes keeping both hands on the bars and both feet on the floor deck. With the seat at 750 mm, check that you can put at least the balls of both feet on the ground at a stop. Sit upright with a slight bend in the knees and elbows so they act as extra suspension, and avoid slumping, which sends shocks straight up the spine.

If you carry a backpack, wear it high and snug, or put it in the under-seat storage. The manual lists 22 litres of storage on the Evo 200, within the maximum load. A heavy bag pulling back on the shoulders adds fatigue over an hour of rough streets.

## Quiet bike, noisy surface: use the horn

The Evo 200 manual points out that an electric motorbike runs quietly, so riders need to watch carefully and use the horn when necessary. On bumpy streets this matters. Pedestrians and other riders do not hear you coming, and you may be too busy watching the surface to see them. A quick tap is normal and understood in Hanoi traffic.

## Wet, uneven and dark

A rough road is harder when it is wet or dark. Puddles hide depth, painted lines and metal covers become slippery and glare from other lights flattens the surface. The manual warns that riding in storms and floods is dangerous for the rider and can damage the electrical system. Slow right down, avoid water of unknown depth and see our guides to [riding in Hanoi rain](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/riding-in-hanoi-rain/) and [riding at night in Hanoi](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/riding-at-night-in-hanoi/).

## Normal harshness or a fault?

| What you notice | Likely meaning | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Sharp thump over a manhole cover at normal speed | Normal for small wheels | Slow down; choose a better line |
| Same bump feels worse than before | Tyre pressure low, or heavier load | Ask the shop to check pressure |
| Fork feels stuck or slams back | Possible suspension fault | Stop and call the shop |
| Oil on a fork tube | Possible seal leak | Report it; do not ride far |
| Repeated bouncing after a bump | Possibly weak damping | Ask for another bike |
| Handlebars shake or the bike pulls to one side | Tyre, wheel or steering issue | Stop safely and call |
| Metallic knock from the rear | Loose part or suspension | Stop and call |

The causes in the middle column are common explanations, not diagnoses. Only a mechanic can say.

## Planning a smoother day

You cannot avoid every rough surface, but you can choose routes that reduce them. Quieter lakeside and riverside roads tend to be easier than busy arteries, as in our guides to [West Lake by scooter](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/tay-ho-west-lake-by-scooter/) and the [Long Bien and Red River first ride](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/long-bien-red-river-first-ride/). Ride in daylight when you can read the surface, and allow extra time. Going slower is more comfortable and is likely to be kinder to the battery too. If a stretch looks bad, getting off and walking the scooter across it is a sensible option.

## FAQ: rider comfort on uneven streets

### Is an electric scooter more uncomfortable than a petrol one?

Not necessarily. The wheel size and weight distribution are similar to many small scooters. The quiet drivetrain makes bumps seem louder.

### Should I lower the tyre pressure for a softer ride?

No. Under-inflated tyres are less safe and can be damaged by sharp edges. Ask the shop for the right pressure.

### How do I know if the suspension is worn?

A fork that sticks, slams, leaks oil or bounces repeatedly is a warning sign. A mechanic must judge it, so ask for another bike.

### Can I ride standing up over rough ground?

Not on a rental scooter. Stay seated with relaxed arms and slow down.

### Does rough road use more battery?

Slower, smoother riding generally uses less energy, but we have no measurement for any particular bike.

### Where do I find the correct tyre pressure?

Ask the shop. The figure can differ between models and is often on a label on the bike.
