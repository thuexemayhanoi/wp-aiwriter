Ask a Hanoi rental shop for "a motorbike" and the first question back is always the same: automatic or manual? The answer shapes everything else, from how quickly you feel at home in the traffic to how much storage you have, how far a tank goes and what your licence must cover. Vietnam adds a twist that confuses many visitors: the bike locals call a "manual" (xe so, a geared bike) is usually a semi-automatic Honda Wave or Yamaha Sirius with no clutch lever at all, while a true manual with a clutch is a different, sportier machine. This guide explains the three transmissions, how each behaves in Hanoi, and which one suits a first-timer, a couple, a commuter or a rider heading for the hills. The models and prices are those Nguyen Ha publishes as of October 2026; the exact bike is confirmed when you book.

## Three transmissions, one city

An **automatic scooter** has a continuously variable transmission. You twist the throttle and go; there are no gears to change, a brake lever on each side of the handlebar, a flat floor between your feet and a storage compartment under the seat. The Honda Vision, Air Blade, Lead and Click and the Yamaha Mio are automatics.

A **semi-automatic bike** has gears that you change with your left foot, but no clutch lever: a centrifugal clutch engages as the engine speeds up. The Honda Wave, Honda Blade and Yamaha Sirius are the classic examples, and they are what Hanoi means by a "manual" or "number" bike. The gear pattern is a rotary shift: press the pedal down with your toe to go up through the four gears, and on most Hondas press the heel plate to go down, so that you never hunt for neutral.

A **fully manual bike** has a clutch lever on the left handlebar and a conventional foot shift, like a motorcycle anywhere else in the world. In Vietnam that usually means a sportier 150 cc machine such as the Honda Winner X or Yamaha Exciter. They are not part of Nguyen Ha's standard fleet, which is made up of automatics, semi-automatics, 50cc bikes and electric models; if you need a clutch bike for a long tour, ask, but plan around the two groups the shop rents every day.

## Automatic scooters: who they suit

The automatic is the default choice for a visitor, and for good reason. Hanoi traffic is a slow, dense stream that stops and starts every few hundred metres; with no gears to think about you can give all your attention to the scooter in front, the bus pulling out and the gap you are aiming for. The flat floor takes a bag of shopping, the under-seat box takes a helmet, and the bodywork keeps the worst of the rain off your legs. Most people who have ridden any scooter before can ride a Vision within minutes of leaving the shop.

The automatics also cover the widest range of sizes. The Vision is small, light and low, the friendliest bike for short riders and nervous ones; the Air Blade and Lead are larger, with more storage and a steadier feel at speed, and carry a passenger more comfortably. The trade-offs are modest: an automatic gives you less engine braking on a long downhill, is slightly less frugal than a semi-automatic of the same size, and a large one is heavier to push onto its stand. Licence-wise, the 110 cc and 125 cc models fall under class A1 or its foreign equivalent; only the 160 cc Air Blade needs the higher class. ## Semi-automatic bikes: who they suit

The Wave, Blade and Sirius are the workhorses of Vietnam. They are light, simple, very frugal and repairable by any roadside mechanic in the country, which is why delivery riders and farmers ride them and why a rider heading out to Ninh Binh or Mai Chau on a budget should consider one. The riding position is more upright and natural than on a scooter, the narrow 17-inch wheels handle potholes and gravel better than small scooter wheels, and engine braking through the gears makes long descents easier on the brakes.

What you give up is convenience. There is no storage beyond a small hook and whatever rack the shop has fitted, no flat floor for a bag, and no fairing to keep rain off your legs. You also have something to learn: when to change up, how to change down before a junction, and the habit of touching the gear pedal lightly rather than stamping on it. Riders who drive a manual car or have ridden any geared bike pick it up in half an hour; complete beginners should start on an automatic. Semi-automatics are 110 cc, so class A1 or its foreign equivalent covers them.

## Fully manual bikes with a clutch

A clutch bike is the right tool for a long tour with luggage over mountain passes and the wrong tool for a week of city errands. In Hanoi traffic you spend your time at walking pace, feathering the clutch and changing between first and second, which is tiring and offers no advantage over a semi-automatic that does the same job without the lever. For the trips most visitors make from Hanoi, a day to Ninh Binh, a weekend to Mai Chau, a semi-automatic Wave is the better choice: cheaper to rent, cheaper to run, and fixable anywhere. If a clutch bike is still what you want, make sure your licence covers its engine size and that you have ridden one recently; a 150 cc manual is quick, and Hanoi is not the place to relearn clutch control.

## What changes the answer

**Where you will ride.** Inside the city, an automatic wins on ease and storage. On the national roads and in the countryside, a semi-automatic's lighter weight, better wheels and engine braking start to matter, and its lack of storage matters less when you carry a backpack.

**How long you are renting.** For a month of commuting the cheapest group to run is the semi-automatic, both in rent and in fuel; for three days of sightseeing the difference is small and the automatic's convenience wins.

**Who is on the back.** Two adults are more comfortable on a larger automatic such as the Air Blade or Lead, whose seat and suspension are made for it. A semi-automatic will carry two, but the passenger sits higher and holds on harder.

**Your experience.** If you have never ridden a geared bike, rent an automatic; Hanoi traffic is enough to learn in one go. If you ride geared bikes at home, the semi-automatic will feel natural within a few streets.

**The weather.** In the rainy season from May to October a scooter's leg shield keeps your trousers drier; in the dry season it hardly matters.

## What each group costs

Nguyen Ha prices the two groups almost identically per day and differently over longer periods. Automatic scooters and semi-automatic bikes are both VND 150,000–200,000 per day. Over a week the semi-automatics are VND 700,000–800,000 and the automatics VND 600,000–1,000,000; over a month the semi-automatics are VND 1,000,000–1,200,000 and the automatics VND 1,200,000–2,000,000, the wider range reflecting the gap between a small Vision and a large Lead or Air Blade. Fuel favours the semi-automatic: a 110 cc Wave is one of the most frugal petrol vehicles made, and even a thirsty day on the national road costs little. ## A quick decision guide

- **First time on two wheels in Asia, city only:** automatic, and the Vision if you are not tall.
- **Confident scooter rider, some days out of town:** a larger automatic such as the Air Blade or Lead.
- **You ride geared bikes at home and want the cheapest month:** a semi-automatic Wave or Sirius.
- **A long tour with luggage and mountain roads:** a semi-automatic with a rack, or a clutch bike if your licence and experience cover it.
- **Two adults every day:** a large automatic.

Whichever you choose, the handover check is the same: brakes, horn, lights, tyres, mirrors, photographs of every mark, fuel level noted. The [rental guide](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/rental-guide/) walks through it.

## FAQ

### Is a semi-automatic hard to ride?

Not if you have ridden any geared vehicle. There is no clutch lever; you change gear with your foot and the bike does the rest. Complete beginners should start on an automatic and learn the traffic first.

### Which is cheaper to rent in Hanoi?

Per day the two groups cost the same at Nguyen Ha, VND 150,000–200,000. Over a month the semi-automatics are cheaper, from VND 1,000,000, and they use less fuel.

### Do I need a different licence for a manual?

No. Licence classes follow engine size, not transmission: both the 110 cc semi-automatics and the 110–125 cc automatics fall under class A1 or its foreign equivalent. A 150 cc clutch bike or a 160 cc Air Blade needs the higher class A.

### Can an automatic scooter do a day trip to Ninh Binh?

Yes. Any 110–125 cc automatic covers the hundred kilometres each way on National Road 1A; a larger one is more comfortable two-up. See our [Ninh Binh ride guide](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/hanoi-to-ninh-binh-by-motorbike/).

### Does Nguyen Ha rent clutch bikes?

The standard fleet is automatics, semi-automatics, 50cc bikes and electric models. Ask when you book if you need a clutch bike for a particular trip.
