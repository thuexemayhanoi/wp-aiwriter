For most visitors, renting a Honda SH in Hanoi is a want rather than a need. The SH is a comfortable, well-built premium scooter with large wheels and a smooth engine, and it does make long or two-up rides more relaxed. But in the narrow, crowded streets of central Hanoi it is bigger, heavier and more attractive to thieves than an ordinary rental scooter, and the larger SH 160i and SH350i need a Class A licence rather than A1. If your licence only covers bikes up to 125 cc, or most of your riding will be short city hops, a mid-size automatic such as a Honda Air Blade or Lead will do almost everything an SH does with fewer complications. This guide explains how to weigh the decision honestly before you pay a premium for the badge.

## Why the Honda SH carries a premium reputation in Vietnam

In Vietnam the SH is more than a scooter model. For years it has been a status symbol: a big-wheeled, Italian-styled machine that signals success in the same way a German sedan might elsewhere. Honda Vietnam assembles and sells it locally, and it sits near the top of Honda's scooter range in both price and finish. That reputation shapes everything around the bike, from what owners charge to rent it out to how much attention it draws when parked on the pavement.

The engineering behind the image is genuinely good. The SH uses Honda's liquid-cooled eSP+ four-valve engine, and the higher trims add two-channel ABS. The large-diameter wheels roll over potholes and expansion joints more calmly than the smaller wheels of budget scooters, and the riding position is upright and roomy. None of that, however, changes the fundamental question for a short-term renter: does your trip actually use what the SH does best?

## SH 125i, SH 160i, SH350i and SH Mode: know which one you are offered

"Rent an SH" can mean four quite different machines, and the difference matters both legally and practically.

- **SH Mode 125** is the smaller, lighter, more fashion-oriented model. It shares the name but not the size of the full SH.
- **SH 125i** is the full-size SH with an engine of about 125 cc. It falls inside the A1 licence class.
- **SH 160i** replaced the old SH 150i in Vietnam at the end of 2022. Honda Vietnam essentially renamed it to match the engine's real displacement of roughly 157 cc. Older listings and many renters still call it "SH 150", which is why searches for "rent SH 150 Hanoi" persist.
- **SH350i** is a maxi-scooter with an engine of about 330 cc, a seat height of roughly 805 mm and a kerb weight in the low 170-kilogram range. It is a very different proposition from a city scooter.

Before you agree to anything, ask the owner to confirm the exact model and engine size on the registration papers. A listing that says "SH 150" might be a 125, a 160 or an older bike, and the licence you need depends on which.

## Licence class: A1 versus A under Law 36/2024

As of October 2026, motorcycle licence classes in Vietnam follow the Road Traffic Order and Safety Law 36/2024/QH15, effective from 1 January 2025. Under Article 57, **Class A1** covers two-wheel motorcycles up to 125 cc and electric motorcycles up to 11 kW. **Class A** covers two-wheel motorcycles above 125 cc or above 11 kW, plus everything in A1. Legal commentary on the new law gives the SH 160i and the Air Blade 160 as typical examples of bikes that now need Class A.

In practice this means the SH Mode 125 and SH 125i sit in the same licence band as a Honda Vision or Lead, while the SH 160i and SH350i do not. Vietnamese riders who hold an older A1 licence issued before 2025 have transitional rules when they renew; those rules concern Vietnamese licences and are not a loophole for visitors.

The consequences of getting this wrong are not trivial. Under Decree 168/2024/ND-CP, in force since 1 January 2025, the fine for riding a motorcycle above 125 cc without a licence that covers it sits in a higher band, VND 6–8 million, than the fine for a bike up to 125 cc. Police may also hold the vehicle. Fine schedules have since been amended, so check the current figures before relying on any number, but the principle that a larger bike carries a larger penalty has not changed. Our guide to [traffic fines that catch foreign riders out](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/traffic-fines-vietnam-foreign-riders/) covers the wider picture.

## What an SH changes for a foreign rider's paperwork

Most foreign visitors ride in Vietnam on an International Driving Permit issued under the 1968 Vienna Convention, alongside their home licence. On the permit, the motorcycle category matters. A permit that shows only the light-motorcycle subcategory, A1, does not cover a bike above 125 cc, and a permit with only car categories covers no motorcycle at all. If you want an SH 160i or SH350i, your permit needs the full motorcycle category A, supported by the matching class on your home licence.

This is where many SH plans quietly collapse. Plenty of travellers hold a car licence and assume a scooter is covered; others learnt on a 125 cc bike at home and have a restricted category without realising it. Our explainer on [licence rules for foreigners riding in Vietnam](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/motorbike-licence-rules-for-foreigners-in-vietnam/) walks through which permits are recognised. If your paperwork only stretches to A1, the decision is effectively made for you: choose a scooter of 125 cc or less.

Travel insurance deserves the same check. Many policies exclude accidents on motorcycles the rider was not licensed to use, and some set their own engine-size limits. A premium bike does nothing for you if the policy refuses a hospital claim.

## Size, weight and seat height in Hanoi traffic

A full-size SH is noticeably longer and heavier than a Vision or Lead, and it has a taller seat. On an open road that size feels planted and stable. In the Old Quarter it feels different. Lanes between parked bikes are narrow, pedestrians step out from shopfronts, and you will be stopping, starting and duck-walking the bike backwards far more often than you expect.

Think about these moments specifically:

- **Paddling backwards** out of a tight parking row. A heavier scooter is harder to manoeuvre with your feet, especially on a slight slope.
- **Low-speed balance** at traffic lights and in jams. A taller seat means shorter riders may only reach the ground on tiptoe, which is unsettling when a wave of traffic surges.
- **Kerbs and ramps** into cafes, hotels and guarded lots. Bigger wheels help here, but extra weight punishes a misjudged ramp.
- **Lane filtering**. Wider bodywork and mirrors make the small gaps local riders use less forgiving.

If you are not used to bikes of this size, the city centre is the worst place to learn. Our notes on [riding in Hanoi's Old Quarter](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/riding-in-hanoi-old-quarter/) explain why even small scooters can be awkward there.

## Comfort on long, rough or two-up rides

The SH earns its price on longer rides. The larger wheels and longer wheelbase soak up broken surfaces better than budget scooters, the seat is broad, and the engine of the 160 and 350 has enough reserve to carry two adults without feeling strained on inclines. If you plan regular trips out of the city with a passenger, that comfort is real and not just marketing.

There are limits, though. Remember that motorbikes of every size are barred from Vietnam's expressways under Law 36/2024, so a powerful SH does not unlock faster routes; you will share the same national roads as everyone else. Mountain routes north of Hanoi are often better suited to a manual or semi-automatic bike that gives more engine braking on long descents. And the SH's under-seat storage, while usable, will not swallow a full-face helmet plus luggage for two. For practical advice on loading a scooter, see our guide to [two riders and luggage on a Lead or Vision](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/scooter-for-two-riders-and-luggage-hanoi/).

Before any trip beyond the city, confirm that your rental agreement allows it. Not every owner permits long-distance travel on any bike, let alone an expensive one; our article on [taking a rental out of Hanoi](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/take-rental-motorbike-out-of-hanoi/) lists the questions to ask.

## Parking, theft risk and the attention an SH attracts

The SH's status in Vietnam has a downside: it has long been one of the most targeted scooters for theft, precisely because it holds its value and is easy to sell on. Owners of SH bikes therefore tend to be careful about where they leave them, and a renter is expected to be equally careful.

In practice, that means using staffed, ticketed parking whenever you stop for more than a few minutes, locking the steering every time, and never leaving the bike on an unattended pavement overnight. Some hotels in the Old Quarter have very limited indoor parking and may prefer smaller bikes. Before you book, ask your accommodation whether they can take a full-size SH, and where it will sit at night.

Our guide to [parking a motorbike in Hanoi](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/parking-a-motorbike-in-hanoi/) explains how guarded lots and tickets work, and why pavement parking can lead to towing. With an SH, those rules matter more because the cost of losing the bike is so much higher.

## Deposit, damage exposure and why costs scale with the bike

Every rental contract in Hanoi makes the renter responsible for some or all of the damage and loss that happens on their watch. On a basic scooter, a scraped side panel or broken mirror is an annoyance. On an SH, the same tip-over in a parking row can mean expensive painted bodywork, premium mirrors and lights, and in the worst case a stolen bike whose replacement value is several times that of an everyday scooter.

Expect an owner renting out an SH to ask for a larger deposit and to inspect the bike closely at handover and return. Photograph every panel before you ride off, note existing scratches in writing, and agree how damage will be priced. Our article on [how a rental deposit works in Hanoi](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/motorbike-rental-deposit-hanoi/) describes a sensible process, and the [10-minute rental scooter inspection](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/rental-scooter-inspection-checklist/) gives a checklist you can use on any bike.

Ask, too, what happens if the bike breaks down far from Hanoi. Parts for common models are available almost everywhere; specialised parts for a premium scooter may take longer to source outside the city.

## When a premium scooter is genuinely worth it

An SH can be a reasonable choice if most of the following are true for you:

1. Your home licence and International Driving Permit clearly include the full motorcycle category A (for the 160 or 350) or at least A1 (for the 125).
2. You are an experienced rider who is comfortable with a heavier bike at walking pace.
3. You plan frequent rides with a passenger or regular day trips where comfort genuinely matters.
4. Your accommodation offers secure parking that will accept a large scooter.
5. You have read and accepted the contract terms on damage, theft and travel outside the city.

If two or more of these are shaky, the premium is unlikely to repay itself. You would be paying more, taking more risk with the deposit and possibly riding outside your licence, for a level of comfort you may only use on a handful of journeys.

## A quick decision checklist before you rent any SH

Run through these questions before you commit, whichever shop you use:

- What exact model and engine size is on the registration papers?
- Does my licence and permit category cover that engine size?
- Does my travel insurance cover this bike and my licence class?
- Where will the bike be parked at night, and is that place staffed?
- How much is the deposit, and how will damage or theft be valued?
- Am I allowed to take it outside Hanoi, and who handles a breakdown?
- Would an Air Blade or Lead serve 90 per cent of my plans just as well?

If the answers are clear and comfortable, enjoy the bike. If not, choose the simpler scooter and spend the difference on the trip itself.

## FAQ: Renting a Honda SH in Hanoi

### Do I need a Class A licence to ride a Honda SH in Vietnam?

It depends on the model. The SH 125i and SH Mode 125 fall within Class A1, which covers motorcycles up to 125 cc. The SH 160i and SH350i are above 125 cc and need Class A under Law 36/2024/QH15. Foreign riders need the equivalent motorcycle category on a recognised permit.

### Is "SH 150" the same as the SH 160i?

Broadly, yes. In Vietnam the SH 150i was replaced and renamed SH 160i at the end of 2022 to reflect its real displacement of about 157 cc. Either way it is above 125 cc, so the A1 class does not cover it.

### Does Nguyen Ha rent the Honda SH?

No. The shop's listed automatic scooters include the Honda Vision, Air Blade, Lead and Click, along with Yamaha models. See the motorbikes page for current options and confirm availability when booking.

### Is a Honda SH better for riding two people in Hanoi?

It is more comfortable on longer rides with a passenger, but in the city its size and weight make parking and manoeuvring harder. A Lead or Air Blade handles two riders well for most visitor itineraries.

### Can I ride a Honda SH on Vietnam's expressways?

No. Since 1 January 2025, Law 36/2024/QH15 bars motorcycles and mopeds of all engine sizes from expressways. A larger engine does not change that.

### Why do owners worry so much about where an SH is parked?

The SH is a high-value, easily resold scooter and has long been a frequent theft target in Vietnam. Using staffed parking and locking the steering protects both the owner and your deposit.
