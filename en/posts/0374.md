Plan fuel stops from the return leg backwards. Decide where you want to be at the end of the day, mark a station on the way out of the countryside and the way back into town, then add one extra stop in the middle of any leg that feels long. A small Honda tank holds only about 3.7 to 4.8 litres, and rural stations are further apart than city ones, so the plan has to exist before you leave, not be improvised when the gauge drops. Treat each fuel stop as a fixed waypoint that also serves as a rest break, a toilet stop and a weather check. This guide gives a route-first method for riders heading out of Hanoi on a rented bike, explains the petrol grades on sale in 2026, covers payment and backup options, and ends with a one-page planning sheet. It is general planning advice, and you should confirm the details for your own bike with the shop.

## Why the plan matters more in the countryside

In central Hanoi a petrol station is rarely more than a short ride away, so running low is an inconvenience. On rural roads the same mistake can mean pushing a bike along a busy national road or waiting for a stranger with a bottle of petrol. Petrolimex says its own network is about 5,500 stations nationwide, and describes more than 17,000 stations across all operators in Vietnam. That is a large network, but it clusters in towns and along main routes, so a quiet side road or a hill road can have long gaps.

Small tanks make the gaps matter. Honda Vietnam's published figures for the Wave Alpha 110 list a 3.7-litre tank and 1.72 litres per 100 km. Its figures for the Vision list 4.8 litres and 1.82 litres per 100 km. These are Honda's numbers for those two models, not for every rental bike, and the shop confirms which bike you are actually getting. Divide the tank by the rate and you get a theoretical range of a couple of hundred kilometres, but real riding with hills, wind, a passenger and luggage is heavier on fuel than a test figure. Our guide to [when to refuel a manual motorbike during a day trip](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/when-to-refuel-a-manual-motorbike-during-a-day-trip/) covers the half-tank rule and range table, and this guide is about the route itself.

## Step one: work backwards from the return

Most riders plan outward and hope. A better order is:

1. **Choose your latest safe return time.** Daylight and the shop's opening hours, 09:00 to 21:00, both bound the day. Our guide to [riding at night in Hanoi](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/riding-at-night-in-hanoi/) explains why you do not want a fuel search after dark.
2. **Place a station near the end of the return leg.** Ideally this is the last town before the city edge, so the bike comes back with a little petrol and you are not hunting for one in the dark.
3. **Place a station at the turnaround point or the destination.** Fill there before you start back, because your next opportunity could be a long way off.
4. **Fill the gaps.** If any leg between two stations looks long on the map, add a mid-leg top-up.
5. **Fill before you leave the city.** Handover quantities vary, so start the day with a visit to a station near the shop or your hotel.

The rule behind the steps is simple. Never leave a station without knowing where the next one is.

## Step two: find stations before you ride

Use a map app on a phone at home or in your hotel, not by the roadside with a weak signal. Search for petrol stations along each leg and note three things for each.

- **Name and brand.** Branded stations from the big distributors are generally easier to recognise and to pay at than a roadside seller.
- **Which side of the road.** On a divided road, a station on the wrong side can be awkward to reach.
- **Where it sits relative to turns.** Mark the last station before a hill road, a ferry or a ring-road exit.

Download offline maps for the area before you leave. Mobile signal can drop in hills, and an offline map still shows where you are. Our guide to [checking whether a navigation app suggests an expressway](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/how-to-check-if-a-navigation-app-suggests-an-expressway/) explains why you should read a suggested route before you follow it, since an unwanted road also changes where stations are.

## Step three: match fuel stops to rest stops

Fuel stops are free rest stops, so use them. New Zealand's transport agency advises riders on long trips to take a rest break about every two hours or every 100 kilometres, and an Australian rider handbook suggests stopping every hour and a half or 150 kilometres. These are guidance from outside Vietnam, but the logic holds for any small, exposed motorbike: tired riders make mistakes, and a motorbike is more tiring than a car.

In practice this means:

- **Stop for fuel when you stop to rest,** not as a separate event. Drink, stretch, check the bike and look at the sky.
- **Do not skip a rest because the tank is still half full.** If you are due a break, take it at the next station.
- **Do not skip a top-up because you are not tired.** A half-full tank on a rural road is the right time to fill, not a reason to push on.

Our guide to [planning breaks on a long semi-automatic bike ride](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/how-to-plan-breaks-on-a-long-semi-automatic-bike-ride/) gives a sample break rhythm.

## What petrol you will find in 2026

Fuel grades changed this year, and it helps to understand them before you reach a pump. Under Circular 50/2025/TT-BCT, issued by the Ministry of Industry and Trade on 7 November 2025, unleaded petrol has been sold as E10, which blends 10 per cent bio-ethanol, since 1 June 2026. The ministry has said E5 RON92 stays available until the end of 2030, so you may see both E10 and E5 RON92 on a pump board. Reports before the rollout said E10 sales were concentrated in cities, so a remote station may be slower to change over.

Honda Vietnam has said that most of its current motorbikes can use E10 normally, and the Vietnam Association of Motorcycle Manufacturers assessed current Honda and Yamaha models as needing no modification. Honda's Wave RSX FI manual says that fuel with up to 10 per cent ethanol can be used and that more than that can damage paint, rubber fuel lines and the tank. The same manual lists RON 95 or higher as its recommended grade.

For a renter, the practical points are:

- **Ask the shop what the bike takes** and which grade to buy. Do not assume.
- **Use branded stations** where possible. Reports on the rollout advise refuelling at reputable stations so that the ethanol blend meets standards.
- **Read the pump or ask the attendant** if the label is unclear. Vietnamese pump labels name the grade, and attendants are used to questions.
- **Never put diesel or an unknown mixture in the tank.** Diesel pumps are usually marked differently, but check before you hand over money.

## Paying at rural stations

Petrolimex says its stations accept Visa and Mastercard credit cards, domestic Napas cards and VNPAY-QR payments, and that it has installed contactless terminals. Not every station in Vietnam is a Petrolimex station, and card terminals can be offline. Carry cash in small notes for every fuel stop, and treat a card or QR payment as a bonus. Our guide to [paying for fuel, parking and tolls as a visitor in Hanoi](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/paying-fuel-parking-tolls-hanoi-visitor/) explains the cash and QR options in more detail.

## A sample planning sheet

Copy this onto a note on your phone before you ride. The distances are blank on purpose, because you read them from your own map on the day.

| Leg | Station name | Distance from the previous stop | Fill or top-up | Rest? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Start in Hanoi | Near hotel or shop | n/a | Fill to full | No |
| Leg 1 | First station in the countryside | Read from map | Top-up if past half | Yes |
| Mid-leg | Only if the gap is long | Read from map | Top-up | Yes |
| Destination | Station near the turnaround | Read from map | Fill before return | Yes |
| Return leg | Last town before Hanoi | Read from map | Top-up | Brief |
| Back in Hanoi | Station near the shop | Read from map | Light top-up | Return |

A good habit is to write a second backup station beside each entry. If the first is closed, out of fuel or has a broken pump, you already know where to go next.

## Choose routes where stations are easy

Some destinations suit fuel planning better than others. Short hill and park trips are easy because the main road passes towns with stations before the climb. Longer rides, such as the road to Mai Chau or Ha Long, need more planning. Our route guides to [Hanoi to Ba Vi](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/hanoi-to-ba-vi-by-motorbike/), [Hanoi to Tam Dao](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/hanoi-to-tam-dao-by-motorbike/) and [Hanoi to Mai Chau](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/hanoi-to-mai-chau-by-motorbike/) describe the roads, and [small-engine manual bikes for rural roads near Hanoi](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/small-engine-manual-bikes-for-rural-roads-near-hanoi/) explains how bike choice affects the plan.

If your plan depends on a station you have not confirmed, change the plan, not your luck. A shorter destination, a different direction, or turning round earlier all cost less than running dry.

## The shop's fuel rules and your plan

The shop hands petrol bikes over with roughly 0.5 to 2 litres and expects them back with roughly 0.5 to 1 litre. An empty tank at return costs VND 20,000. Those figures matter for planning in two ways. First, the handover amount is small, so your first stop should come soon after you leave, ideally before you are out of the city. Second, you do not need to return the bike full, so there is no point in filling it to the brim on the last leg. Ask the shop for the exact fuel arrangement for your bike before you ride, and see our guide to [returning your rental motorbike in Hanoi](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/returning-your-rental-motorbike-hanoi/) for the return routine. ## If you cannot find a station

1. **Stop in a safe place** and look at your gauge and your map.
2. **Slow down.** Steady, lower speeds use less petrol than hard acceleration.
3. **Ask locally.** In a village, a shop or cafe owner will usually know the nearest pump.
4. **Use a roadside seller only as a last resort,** and only if you can see the fuel is clean. Poor-quality fuel can harm the engine.
5. **Call the shop** on 0334 699 969 (+84 334 699 969), open 09:00 to 21:00 daily, if you are stuck, and share your location.

## What about electric bikes?

An electric scooter does not need fuel stops, but it needs charging stops and the same backward-from-the-return logic. Range depends on the model, the load and the road, so confirm it with the shop. Our guides to [renting an electric scooter](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/renting-an-electric-scooter-in-hanoi/) and [charging an electric scooter in Hanoi](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/charging-an-electric-scooter-in-hanoi/) explain what to expect. ## FAQ: fuel stops for rural rides

### How far apart are petrol stations outside Hanoi?

There is no single answer. Stations cluster in towns and on main roads, with longer gaps on quiet or hilly roads. Check your own route on a map before you leave.

### How often should I fill up on a day trip?

Aim to top up at about half a tank and always before a long or hilly stretch. Our guide to the half-tank rule gives the details.

### Can I use E10 petrol in a rental motorbike?

Honda Vietnam has said most of its current motorbikes can use E10. Ask the shop which grade your bike takes.

### Should I carry spare petrol?

Do not carry petrol in an unsuitable container. A better plan is a fuller tank and a backup station.

### Do rural stations take cards?

Some do, but do not rely on it. Carry cash in small notes.

### What if the shop's fuel rules differ from my plan?

Follow the shop's rules, and ask before you ride if anything is unclear.
