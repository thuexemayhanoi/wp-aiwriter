Electric two-wheelers in Hanoi are not one category but several, and the rules that apply to you depend on which one you are sitting on. A pedal-assisted electric bicycle is treated much like a bicycle; a silent scooter limited to about 50 km/h is a moped in law, with a 40 km/h speed limit and no licence; a faster VinFast is a motorcycle that needs the same A1 licence as a petrol Honda. Helmets are compulsory on all of them, expressways are closed to all of them, and the city's new low-emission zone around Hoan Kiem changes less for visitors than the headlines suggest. This guide explains each class, the speed limits and the places you may and may not ride, based on the law in force since 1 January 2025 and checked as of October 2026.

## Four electric classes, sorted by numbers on the spec sheet

Vietnam's Law on Road Traffic Order and Safety (Law 36/2024/QH15) and its implementing rules sort electric two-wheelers by design speed and motor power rather than by what the seller calls them. In practice there are four groups worth knowing.

- **Electric bicycle (motor-assisted bicycle).** A bicycle with pedals and a small motor, a design speed of no more than 25 km/h, and the ability to be pedalled with the motor off. In law it falls with bicycles among the non-motorised, so-called rudimentary vehicles.
- **Electric moped.** A scooter-shaped machine with a design speed of no more than 50 km/h and a motor of no more than 4 kW. It sits in the same class as a petrol 50cc bike.
- **Light electric motorcycle.** Anything above the moped limits with a motor of up to 11 kW. Most mainstream electric scooters on Hanoi streets, including the faster VinFast models, sit here.
- **Larger electric motorcycle.** A motor above 11 kW. These are rare in the rental market but exist.

The labels in shops are looser. "Xe dien" on a sign may mean any of the four, and a scooter sold as an "electric bike" may legally be a motorcycle. The spec sheet, the registration paperwork and the plate tell you the truth, not the marketing name.

## How to tell which class you are actually riding

The quickest test is the pedals. If the machine has working pedals and tops out around 25 km/h, it is almost certainly an electric bicycle. If it has a flat floor, a seat box and no pedals, it is either a moped or a motorcycle, and you need to look further.

Ask for the maximum speed and motor power, which are printed in the vehicle papers and usually in the manufacturer's specification. A model advertised as limited to 49 km/h with a motor at or below 4 kW is a moped; one that will do 60 or 70 km/h is a motorcycle whatever its size. A rental shop that keeps its paperwork in order will show you the registration certificate on request, and the bike should carry the plate that matches it.

## Licence and minimum age for each class

The licence rule follows the class. An electric bicycle needs no licence. An electric moped needs no licence either, but the rider must be at least 16, the same age rule that applies to petrol mopeds under 50cc.

A light electric motorcycle needs a class A1 licence. Under Law 36/2024/QH15, a new A1 licence covers two-wheel motorcycles up to 125 cc or with an electric motor of up to 11 kW, and the minimum age is 18. Anything above 11 kW needs the full class A licence. Old-style A1 licences issued before 2025 keep their original scope, which was drawn slightly differently, so a Vietnamese resident with an older card should check the wording on it.

For a foreign visitor the practical question is whether your home licence, plus an International Driving Permit issued under the 1968 Convention, covers motorcycles. If it does, you may ride the same electric motorcycles a Vietnamese A1 holder may. If it does not, an electric moped or an electric bicycle is the legal choice. Our guide to [licence rules for foreigners](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/motorbike-licence-rules-for-foreigners-in-vietnam/) explains the permit question in detail, and it applies to electric and petrol bikes alike.

## Helmets: no electric exemption

Many visitors assume that a slow electric bicycle is exempt from the helmet rule, as a pedal bicycle is in many countries. In Vietnam it is not. Riders and passengers of motorcycles, mopeds and motor-assisted bicycles must all wear a helmet that meets the national standard, with the strap fastened. Under Decree 168/2024/ND-CP, effective from 1 January 2025, the fine for riding an electric bicycle without a helmet, or with the strap undone, is VND 400,000–600,000, and the same penalty applies to an unhelmeted passenger.

Children are the most common exception people ask about. The rules allow narrow exceptions, for instance for very young children and medical emergencies, but a rented bike carrying an adult tourist is not one of them. Nguyen Ha supplies one basic helmet per rider free of charge; if you plan longer rides, bring or buy a better one. Our [helmet rules guide](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/helmet-rules-in-vietnam/) covers standards, fit and the fines in more depth.

## Speed limits: 25, 40 and the motorcycle tables

Speed limits for electric two-wheelers are set by Circular 38/2024/TT-BGTVT, effective from 1 January 2025, together with the design limits that define each class.

An electric bicycle is built to stop assisting at about 25 km/h. There is no separate posted limit to watch for, because the machine cannot legally go faster, and a modified bicycle that can is no longer an electric bicycle in law.

An electric moped has a maximum operating speed of 40 km/h on any road where it is allowed, inside or outside built-up areas, even if the scooter can physically reach 49 km/h. This catches people out: the speedometer happily shows 45, and a camera or patrol may treat that as speeding.

A light electric motorcycle follows the ordinary motorcycle limits. Inside built-up areas the general maximum is 60 km/h on dual carriageways and on one-way roads with two or more motor lanes, and 50 km/h on two-way roads without a central divider and one-way roads with a single lane. Outside built-up areas the motorcycle maximum is higher, but in Hanoi you are almost always inside a built-up area, and posted signs always override the general table. Realistically, city traffic keeps everyone well below these limits most of the day.

## Lanes and road position in the city

Where a road has separate lanes, motor vehicles and non-motorised vehicles are expected to use the lanes intended for them. An electric bicycle belongs with bicycles; on a wide boulevard with a marked lane or service road for slow vehicles, that is where it should be, and it should keep to the right. Electric mopeds and motorcycles ride in the motorbike stream like any petrol scooter, keeping right and out of lanes signed for cars only.

Several Hanoi roads separate traffic by type with painted lines, physical barriers or signs at the start of each section. Read the pictograms rather than following the bike in front: a sign showing a car only means scooters are excluded from that lane, even if a few riders ignore it. Bus lanes on some corridors are also off limits outside the signed times.

Pavements are not a riding surface for any of these vehicles. Riding along a footpath to skip a jam is a common local habit and a common reason for a stop. When you need to reach a pavement parking spot, push the bike the last few metres. Our guide to [parking a motorbike in Hanoi](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/parking-a-motorbike-in-hanoi/) covers where to leave it.

## Roads where no electric bike may go

The biggest restriction is the expressway network. Law 36/2024/QH15 bars motorcycles and mopeds of every type from expressways, and electric models are no exception. Bicycles are barred too. This matters more than it sounds, because navigation apps sometimes route a short hop over an expressway link or a toll road; read our guide on [checking whether an app suggests an expressway](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/how-to-check-if-a-navigation-app-suggests-an-expressway/) before you set out.

Inside the city, the elevated sections of the ring roads carry signs that exclude two-wheelers, and the ramps onto them are where tourists most often make a wrong turn. Stay on the ground-level road beneath when the signs show a crossed-out motorbike. Some bridges also separate traffic by type, so look for the lane markings as you approach the Red River crossings.

Finally, the pedestrian streets around Hoan Kiem Lake and in the Old Quarter close to vehicles on weekend evenings, and an electric bike does not exempt you from that closure. Plan to park at the edge and walk in; our [Old Quarter riding guide](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/riding-in-hanoi-old-quarter/) shows where the closures start.

## Ring Road 1 and the low-emission zone: what changed in 2026

In July 2025 the Prime Minister's Directive 20/CT-TTg set a target that, from 1 July 2026, petrol and diesel motorcycles and mopeds should no longer circulate inside Hanoi's Ring Road 1, the innermost ring that encloses Hoan Kiem, the Old Quarter and much of Ba Dinh. The headline was widely read as an outright petrol-scooter ban in central Hanoi from mid-2026. That is not what happened.

Hanoi chose a phased low-emission zone instead. Under the city's resolution and the scheme approved in 2026, the first pilot runs from 1 July to 31 December 2026 in a small core area of Hoan Kiem ward, and later phases are planned to extend the zone. Reports on the final pilot rules differ in detail between drafts: some describe restrictions mainly on petrol ride-hailing motorbikes plus weekend evening restrictions in the innermost streets, others stress that private petrol motorbikes were not banned outright and that the city relied on encouraging owners to switch. Reporting from June 2026 says the broader restriction on non-compliant petrol motorbikes across Ring Road 1 was pushed back to 1 January 2028.

For a visitor in October 2026 the practical reading is this: a petrol rental scooter is still legal on almost all of central Hanoi's streets, and an electric bike is not a pass through closures that apply to every vehicle. Check local signs in the Hoan Kiem core, and expect the rules to tighten from 2027 and 2028. Riding electric now is a good habit to build, not yet a legal requirement for tourists.

## Plates, papers and what to carry on an electric ride

Electric mopeds and electric motorcycles are registered vehicles and carry number plates, like their petrol equivalents. The registration certificate, or a copy the rental shop provides, should travel with the bike. Carry your passport or a copy, your licence and International Driving Permit if you are on a motorcycle-class model, and the rental contract.

Electric bicycles are lighter on paperwork, but the helmet, lighting and road-position rules still apply. At night, all classes need working lights; an electric bicycle without a front light and rear reflector is a hazard on Hanoi's darker streets. Our guide to [riding at night in Hanoi](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/riding-at-night-in-hanoi/) covers the lighting rules.

Remember that the alcohol limit for every rider of a motorised vehicle is zero, and that police checks do not distinguish between electric and petrol scooters. Fines, licence point deductions and impoundment apply in the same way; the [traffic fines guide](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/traffic-fines-vietnam-foreign-riders/) lists the offences that most often catch foreign riders.

## Choosing the right class for your plans

The class you rent should follow your licence first and your itinerary second. Without a motorcycle licence, an electric moped is the sensible choice: quiet, easy and legal, as long as you keep to 40 km/h and stay off expressways. An electric bicycle suits short neighbourhood hops, a lakeside loop or riders who want something slow and light.

With a valid motorcycle licence and permit, a VinFast-class electric motorcycle gives you more speed for longer cross-city journeys, such as Long Bien to Tay Ho. For day trips beyond the city, though, range and charging become the limiting factors rather than the law, and a petrol scooter is usually the more practical choice. ## FAQ: electric bike rules in Hanoi

### Is an electric bicycle legally the same as an electric scooter?

No. An electric bicycle has pedals and a design speed of no more than 25 km/h and is treated with bicycles. A pedal-less electric scooter is either a moped (up to 50 km/h and 4 kW) or a motorcycle (above those limits), and different rules apply to each.

### What is the speed limit for an electric moped in Vietnam?

40 km/h on every road where mopeds may ride, under Circular 38/2024/TT-BGTVT, effective from 1 January 2025. This is lower than the 49 km/h many electric mopeds can actually reach.

### Do I need a licence for a VinFast electric scooter?

It depends on the model. Moped-class models limited to 50 km/h with a motor up to 4 kW need no licence for riders aged 16 or over. Faster models with motors up to 11 kW need a class A1 licence, or a foreign licence plus an International Driving Permit covering motorcycles.

### Is a helmet required on an electric bicycle?

Yes, for the rider and any passenger, with the strap fastened. Riding without one can bring a fine under Decree 168/2024/ND-CP.

### Can I ride an electric bike on Vietnamese expressways?

No. Motorcycles and mopeds of every kind, electric included, are barred from expressways, and so are bicycles. Use national highways and local roads instead.

### Are petrol scooters banned inside Ring Road 1 from 2026?

Not outright. The 2025 directive set a 1 July 2026 target, but Hanoi began with a limited low-emission pilot in the Hoan Kiem core, and reporting in mid-2026 placed wider restrictions on non-compliant petrol motorbikes from 1 January 2028. Watch the local signs.
