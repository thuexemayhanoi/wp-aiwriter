Check the charging cable the way you would check a helmet strap: slowly, with your hands, before you need it. Look along the whole lead for cuts, kinks, crushed sections and bare wire, press the points where the cable enters the plug and the charger for splits or movement, look at the connector for bent pins, dirt or scorch marks, and confirm that the charger belongs to this bike. Then watch it while it works: a charger that is slightly warm is normal, but one that is too hot to hold, smells of hot plastic or crackles is not. This guide walks through a three-minute pickup inspection, what to watch during the first charge, what never to do to a cable and what to tell the shop. It is general information about e-bike and e-scooter chargers, not an electrician's test.

## What counts as the charging cable

On most electric bikes and scooters the "cable" is really a short chain of parts. A mains lead runs from the wall socket to a charger block, the block converts the power, and a second lead ends in the connector that fits the battery or the bike's charging port. Some chargers are one piece with a plug on a short cord.

Every join in that chain can fail, and the joins are where most damage happens. That is why the inspection below follows the chain from wall to bike instead of just looking at the middle of the cable.

## Why the cable deserves attention

Fire services say the same thing about charging: most fires involving e-bikes and e-scooters happen while the battery is charging. London Fire Brigade explains that charging stresses the battery, and that if the charger is incompatible or the battery is damaged, it can overheat and fail. Damaged leads add a second path for trouble, because a cable with exposed wire can cause electric shock or a short circuit even when the battery is healthy.

You are not expected to be an electrician. You are expected to notice what is obviously wrong, stop, and tell the shop.

## A three-minute inspection at pickup

Do this with the charger unplugged, ideally at the counter with a staff member beside you.

1. **Follow the lead with your fingers.** Run it through your hands from end to end. You are feeling for cuts, flat spots where it has been crushed, kinks, swelling and places where the outer jacket is split.

2. **Look for colour.** The Electrical Safety First advice on plugs and cables says cables should be free from cuts, nicks and crushing, should have no joints or repairs with insulating tape, and that coloured wires should not be showing. These points apply to any mains lead, whatever the country.

3. **Check the strain relief.** That is the thick rubbery section where the cable meets the plug or charger. Gently flex it. A good one holds firm. A loose, cracked or pulled-out one means the wires inside are being stressed.

4. **Inspect the connector.** The metal contacts should be clean, straight and the same colour as each other. Bent pins, green or white crusting, dark burn marks or melted plastic mean trouble. Scorch marks and melted plastic around a connector are classic overheating signs.

5. **Look at the charger block.** The casing should be intact, with no cracks, dents, warping, discolouration or signs of liquid. Vents should be clear.

6. **Match the charger to the bike.** The label on the charger should show an output that matches the label on the battery or bike. Fire guidance consistently says to use the charger supplied with the battery or one the manufacturer recommends. If the shop hands you a charger that looks different from the one in the photos on the bike's page, or a loose one with no label, ask why.

7. **Photograph everything.** Take a clear photo of the whole cable, both ends, the charger label and the connector. These pictures protect you at return.

## What a good cable looks like

| Part | Healthy | Warning |
|---|---|---|
| Outer jacket | Smooth, even, flexible | Cuts, splits, flat spots, tape repairs |
| Strain relief | Firm, attached | Cracked, loose, pulled away |
| Wall plug | Pins straight, casing intact | Bent or wobbly pins, scorch marks |
| Charger block | Clean, cool when idle, intact | Cracks, warping, discolouration |
| Connector | Clean contacts, snug fit | Bent pins, corrosion, melted plastic |
| Label | Readable, matches the bike | Missing, peeled, does not match |

## Watching the first charge

If the shop lets you charge before you leave, or you charge on your first evening, stay nearby and watch for the first ten or fifteen minutes.

It is normal for batteries and chargers to become warm. GOV.UK guidance says it is normal for batteries to generate some heat when charging or in use, but if one feels extremely hot to the touch it may be defective and at risk of fire, so stop charging it immediately.

Other signs to stop on, taken from the NFCC Charge Safe campaign and GOV.UK guidance:

- a strong or unusual smell
- hissing, cracking or crackling noises
- visible smoke
- a swollen or misshapen battery
- a charger that is leaking

Electrical Safety First adds that burn marks, arcing sounds, buzzing or crackling, or a plug that feels too hot to touch are reasons to unplug and contact the retailer or manufacturer.

If you notice any of these, unplug the charger if it is safe to do so, move away from the bike, and call the shop. If there is smoke or flame, leave the area and call emergency services. Do not try to fight a battery fire yourself.

## Things that should never be done to a charging cable

- **Do not repair it with tape.** A taped cable is a hidden fault. The Electrical Safety First guidance says a cable should have no joints or repairs with insulating tape.

- **Do not pull the cable to unplug.** A US federal safety fact sheet on damaged power cords notes that damage often occurs at the plug and that pulling on the cord to remove the plug can pull it away from the wire covering and expose the wires inside. Hold the plug, not the cable.

- **Do not trap it.** Do not run it under a door, a bed leg or a heavy bag. Pinching can cause internal damage that does not show.

- **Do not cover the charger.** GOV.UK and the NFCC both warn against covering chargers or battery packs while charging, because this can cause overheating.

- **Do not use a mismatched charger.** NFPA's guidance says to never use charging equipment that did not come with your device, and notes that e-bike and e-scooter battery fires have been associated with faulty charging equipment and overloaded circuits.

- **Do not daisy-chain extension leads.** If you need an extension, GOV.UK advises using it uncoiled and suitably rated, and the NFCC says it is safer to plug directly into the wall socket.

- **Do not leave it plugged in after the battery is full.** NFPA advises not to continue to charge the battery once it is full, and fire services advise unplugging the charger when charging is finished.

## Where the cable sits while charging

Where you put the cable matters as much as its condition. Charge on a hard, flat surface where heat can disperse, as London Fire Brigade advises for batteries, and keep the lead out of walkways so nobody trips on it or yanks it from the socket. Keep it away from towels, bedding and other things that burn, and never lay the charger block under a bag or a jacket. If the bike has to be charged outdoors or in a covered parking area, ask the shop how it recommends protecting the connector from rain, because a wet connector is a separate hazard from a worn one.

## What to say to the shop

A short, specific message works best. If you spot a problem at pickup, say which part, show the photo and ask for a replacement before you leave. If a problem appears during the rental, stop using the charger and phone the shop on 0334 699 969 (+84 334 699 969), open 09:00 to 21:00 daily.

The cable is only half the circuit. Older sockets, loose sockets and overloaded multi-way strips are common problems anywhere. The guidance cited here comes from the UK and US, so details such as plug standards do not carry over, but the principle does: do not plug a charger into a socket that is loose, scorched, hot or crackling. ## At return

Return the cable and charger with the bike, coiled loosely and not wrapped tightly around the block. Tight wrapping puts strain on the joins. Photograph them again if you wish. ## FAQ: electric bike charging cable

### Is it normal for the charger to get warm?

Yes, some warmth is normal. If it is too hot to hold, or if you smell hot plastic, unplug it and call the shop.

### Can I use a phone charger or another charger that fits?

No. Use the charger supplied with the bike or one the manufacturer recommends. A plug that fits is not proof that the charger is right for the battery.

### Can I repair a split cable with tape?

No. Stop using it and ask the shop to replace it.

### Who pays if the cable is damaged?

It depends on the cause and the contract. Ask before you sign, and keep your pickup photos.

### Should I unplug the charger as soon as it is done?

Yes. Fire services advise unplugging once charging is finished.
