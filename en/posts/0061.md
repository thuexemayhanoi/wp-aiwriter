In stop-and-go traffic the secret to a semi-automatic bike is to do less, not more. Stay in first or second gear, change gear only when the flow genuinely changes, control your speed with the throttle and the brakes rather than the gear pedal, and keep your feet where they belong. A Honda Wave or Yamaha Sirius has no clutch lever to work, so the hard part is not coordination but restraint: resisting the urge to change gear every few seconds. This guide explains how to do that in Hanoi's dense, continuous traffic, how to creep at walking pace without lurching, how to manage the gap in front of you and how to avoid the fatigue and mistakes that come from hours of repeated stopping and starting. It is general riding advice, not a substitute for supervised training.

## Why stop-and-go is the real test

On an open road a semi-automatic bike asks very little of its rider. In a city centre the same bike asks for a stop, a start and a small speed change every few seconds. The skills are the same ones you learn on a quiet road, but they are repeated hundreds of times, and a small habit that looks harmless in a practice session, such as resting a foot on the gear lever, becomes a daily source of wear and error.

Hanoi adds its own texture. Traffic does not stop in tidy queues. It compresses and releases in waves, with riders joining from side streets, pedestrians crossing between bikes and buses pulling out. Your job is to move with the wave, not to race the gap in front. ## Pick a gear and stay in it

Honda's Wave RSX FI manual tells riders to avoid unnecessary gear changes, and says that resting a foot on the shift lever can damage the shifting and clutch mechanisms. In stop-and-go traffic that advice has a practical meaning. Choose first gear for the crawling parts and second gear for the faster parts, and change only when the speed of the traffic around you has clearly settled at a new level for more than a few seconds.

A useful rule is this: if you would be in the new gear for less than the length of a few vehicles, stay where you are. A bike in second gear at a crawl will run smoothly if you keep the throttle light. A bike in first gear at a moderate pace will sound busy but is under no real strain. Neither does harm in the short run. What does harm, to your concentration and to the bike, is hunting between gears.

Third gear and fourth gear belong to the open road. If you find yourself in third gear in a crawl, the engine will feel sluggish and the bike will jerk when you ask for power. Drop to second or first before you need the power, not after.

## Use the throttle and brakes as your speed controls

In a stop-and-go flow, your right hand and right foot do most of the work:

- **Throttle:** small, smooth movements. Roll the throttle on and off, never snap it.
- **Front brake:** a light, progressive squeeze to shed speed, with the lever pulled by the fingers and not the whole hand.
- **Rear brake:** a gentle press with the right foot, used together with the front brake for stops.

Honda's safe-riding training document sets out the sequence: release the throttle completely, use both brakes together with the front stronger than the rear, and keep the steering straight. It warns against using only the rear brake, which locks the wheel, and against using only the front brake, which can lift the rear wheel if applied abruptly. For many small speed changes, a light touch on both brakes with the throttle closed is smoother than a gear change.

## Creeping at walking pace

Moving at a walking pace without lurching is the skill that separates a smooth rider from a jerky one. A semi-automatic bike's automatic clutch engages as engine speed rises, so a steady, slightly raised idle moves the bike forward gently and a very small throttle opening keeps it going. The common technique is to hold the throttle steady and use the rear brake lightly to control the creep, rather than opening and closing the throttle.

Practise this in a quiet place first. Roll forward at a walking pace with the throttle steady, lightly press the rear brake to slow, release it to speed up and keep your eyes up. The brake is a fine speed control at very low speeds because it gives a gradual response, whereas the throttle on a small engine can jump. If you find the bike stalls or surges, ask the shop to check the idle and the automatic clutch, since a tired clutch or a too-low idle makes crawling harder.

Keep your feet on the footrests while creeping. Putting them down too early is a common cause of tipping over when a wheel finds a pothole.

## Managing the gap

In stop-and-go traffic the gap to the vehicle in front is your safety margin, and it is the thing most riders let shrink. Honda's training document includes a section on keeping a safe distance from the vehicle ahead. Use a simple rule: leave enough space to stop without braking harshly if the vehicle in front stops suddenly, and enough that you can still see its rear wheel touching the ground.

A larger gap also gives you time to use the gentle speed control described above. If the gap shrinks, ease off the throttle first and brake lightly. If the gap opens, resist the urge to dash forward to close it. Those dashes are what make traffic compress in waves and what put riders into the back of the vehicle ahead.

Watch the front wheels of the vehicles ahead and the movement of riders in the next lane. A wheel turning, a rider's head looking sideways or a brake light a few vehicles ahead warns you before the vehicle in front reacts.

## Filtering and lane position

Filtering between slow or stationary cars is widespread in Hanoi and tolerated in practice, but it is not a lane right and it demands extra care. If you choose to do it, do it slowly, keep your hands on the brakes, watch for opening doors and for pedestrians stepping out between vehicles, and stop before the front of the queue. Our guide to [lane rules for motorbikes in Vietnam](https://blog.rentbikehanoi.com/en/lane-rules-motorbikes-vietnam/) explains the legal framework. Article 33 of the Law on Road Traffic Order and Safety (Law No. 36/2024/QH15), in force since 1 January 2025, bans riding abreast, and Decree 168/2024/ND-CP fines a rider who rides abreast of three or more vehicles at VND 600,000–800,000 as of October 2026.

Keep to the lane assigned for motorbikes, avoid weaving and signal before any move sideways. If you cannot filter without crossing into a lane reserved for another vehicle type, stay in line.

## Stops and starts: the cycle

The cycle in dense traffic is slow, stop, start, slow. Do each part with intent:

1. **Slow.** Close the throttle early and begin braking before the vehicle ahead stops.
2. **Stop.** Use both brakes together. Honda's document says that once the bike has stopped, the right foot keeps the brake pressed and the left foot goes to the ground.
3. **Wait in gear.** In a short stop, stay in first gear with the brake held, so the bike is ready. For a long stop, such as a long red light, you may shift to neutral, but follow the demonstration for your bike. Honda's manual says that when the Wave is stopped you can shift directly from fourth gear to neutral on the rotary gearbox, but not while moving.
4. **Look and signal.** Before moving, check mirrors and over your shoulder and signal.
5. **Start.** Release the brake and roll on the throttle smoothly. A snatched start causes lurches that annoy riders behind and unsettle a passenger.

If the bike lurches, close the throttle and brake gently. Do not grab the front brake.

## Heat, fatigue and attention

Long periods of slow traffic tire the left foot, the right hand and the mind. Heat adds to it. Take regular breaks in a shaded, safe place. If your left foot hurts, check that you are not resting it on the gear lever, that your boot is not catching the pedal and that your seat position is comfortable.

Watch for signs that you are tired: late braking, drifting in the lane, forgetting to signal. If you see them, stop for a drink and a rest. A small bike in a hot, crowded street rewards patience much more than speed.

## Common mistakes

- **Hunting between gears.** Changing gear every few seconds adds nothing and wears the mechanism.
- **Riding the gear lever.** A foot resting on it risks accidental changes and damage.
- **Dashing into small gaps.** This creates the waves that cause rear-end collisions.
- **Looking at the wheel in front.** Look through the gap, at the road well ahead.
- **Using only one brake.** Use both together, as Honda teaches.
- **Starting without checking over the shoulder.** Bikes appear from behind faster than expected.

## A quiet-road drill

Find a wide, quiet, legal place and set out two markers, such as bottles, thirty metres apart. Ride between them in first gear at a walking pace, with the throttle steady and the rear brake lightly applied, then stop with both brakes at the second marker. Pull away, ride back and repeat. After ten runs, try second gear at a slightly higher speed. The aim is smooth, quiet stops and starts, with the left foot hovering clear of the lever. ## FAQ: stop-and-go riding on a semi-automatic

### Which gear should I use in slow traffic?

First gear for crawling and second gear for moving at a steady, slow pace. Avoid changing between them every few seconds.

### Should I rest my left foot on the gear lever?

No. Honda's manual warns that resting a foot on the lever can damage the shifting and clutch mechanisms, and its training document says the left foot should not press on the lever.

### How do I stop the bike lurching when I pull away?

Roll the throttle on gently with the brake released smoothly. A snatched throttle makes the automatic clutch engage abruptly.

### Is it better to stay in first or shift to neutral at a red light?

For a short stop, many riders stay in first with the brake held. For a long stop you may shift to neutral if you know how your bike behaves. Either is fine if you stay in control.

### How do I avoid running into the vehicle in front?

Leave a gap you can stop in, ease off the throttle early and use both brakes together.

### Is filtering between cars legal?

It is widespread but not a lane right. Riding abreast of three or more vehicles is fined VND 600,000–800,000 as of October 2026.
