To estimate how far a rented electric scooter will go on a day trip, take the maker's claimed range, plan on roughly two-thirds of it for real city riding, then keep a further reserve of about a fifth so you never ride the battery to the bottom. Compare that figure with your route length, including detours, and test the result with a short calibration ride, which shows how many percentage points the battery loses per kilometre. The method takes five minutes and is more reliable than the dashboard's remaining-distance number. The planning factors in this guide are rules of thumb, not manufacturer figures, and the published numbers come from other VinFast models, not the bike you will be handed, so treat the worked examples as a template and ask the shop for your bike's real figures.

## The three numbers you need

Every estimate starts with three facts about the specific scooter.

1. **Battery capacity,** usually in kilowatt-hours (kWh). It is the size of the "tank".
2. **Claimed range and the test it comes from.** VinFast's published figures, for example, are measured at a constant 30 km/h under test conditions, not in traffic.
3. **Current charge,** shown as a percentage or bars on the display.

At Nguyen Ha, the battery level and the charging arrangement are confirmed at handover for every electric model, so ask the staff to read all three to you and photograph the display. The bike you receive is confirmed when you book, so do not assume it matches any figure you saw online.

## Turn the claim into energy per kilometre

Dividing capacity by claimed range gives the energy the scooter uses per kilometre under test conditions. A few published examples show the scale:

| Model (published figure) | Battery | Claimed range | Energy per km in the test |
|---|---|---|---|
| VinFast Evo, one battery | 1.5 kWh | about 85 km | about 18 Wh/km |
| VinFast Feliz Neo | 2.0 kWh | about 114 km | about 18 Wh/km |
| VinFast Evo Grand | 2.4 kWh | about 134 km | about 18 Wh/km |
| VinFast Feliz S | 3.5 kWh | about 198 km | about 18 Wh/km |

These are my own calculations from VinFast's published capacities and ranges, on different models from the rental bike. The striking thing is that they cluster near 18 watt-hours per kilometre, which shows the test is a steady, gentle ride. Real riding uses more. The comparison is useful because it lets you check any bike: if a shop quotes a battery size and a range, divide them, and a figure far from 18 should prompt a question about how the range was measured.

## Discount for real riding

A test at a constant 30 km/h leaves out nearly everything that happens in Hanoi: stops at lights, harder acceleration, a passenger, wind, hills such as bridge ramps and a battery that is not new. Consumer Reports, testing electric cars, summed it up well: range is not an absolute number, and weather, hills, speed, traffic, cargo, passengers and climate settings all have an impact. The US government's fuel economy site adds that aggressive driving can reduce fuel economy by 10 to 40 percent in stop-and-go traffic, though that figure is for petrol cars and a scooter will differ.

A sensible planning approach is therefore:

- **Planning range = claimed range x 0.65** for mixed city riding with one rider.
- **Use a lower factor, about 0.55,** if you will carry a passenger, ride faster, climb a lot or the bike is old.
- **Usable range = planning range x 0.8,** which keeps a 20 percent reserve in the battery.

The factors are my own rules of thumb and err on the cautious side, because the cost of being wrong is a long push.

| Claimed range | Planning range (x 0.65) | Usable, with 20% reserve |
|---|---|---|
| 85 km | about 55 km | about 44 km |
| 114 km | about 74 km | about 59 km |
| 134 km | about 87 km | about 70 km |
| 198 km | about 129 km | about 103 km |

Treat the last column as the total distance for the day, including the ride back. If you start the day below a full battery, scale it down in proportion: at 80 percent, take 80 percent of the usable figure.

## Add the route, and then the detours

Measure your route in a map app, then add a margin for reality. A rule that works for sightseeing is to add 15 to 20 percent for wrong turns, one-way streets, a stop for photographs and the ride from and to your hotel. For example, a 30 km loop becomes about 35 km. Against the 44 km usable figure above, that fits with a little room. On a 114 km bike, with a 59 km usable range, the same loop is comfortable, and a longer one could work.

If the route is out and back, count both directions and do not assume the return will cost less than the outward leg. The best day-trip plan for a rental electric scooter is a loop or an out-and-back that stays well within the usable range, not a point-to-point ride that depends on finding a charger at the far end. ## Run a calibration ride

The most useful check takes ten minutes. Before you set off on the real route, ride a flat stretch of about 5 to 10 km at the speed you expect to use, with the load you expect to carry. Note the battery percentage and the trip meter at the start and the end.

If the battery drops by 4 percentage points over 5 km, it uses about 0.8 points per kilometre, which implies roughly 125 km from 100 percent, or about 100 km from the 80 percent you might have after a long wait. In practice the figure usually worsens in the last quarter of the battery, so use the reading to compare against your plan, not to stretch it.

If the drop is bigger than expected, the likely reasons are a head wind, a rider and passenger together, soft tyres or a power mode that favours acceleration. VinFast's scooters have Eco and Sport modes. A low-power mode is the first thing to try when the numbers worry you.

## Rules of thumb for the day

Four simple rules keep the day safe.

- **The half rule.** Turn back when the battery reaches half of what you started with, unless you are doing a loop.
- **The third rule.** For a loop, check at one-third of the route. If the battery has lost more than a third of your usable figure, shorten the route.
- **The 20 percent rule.** Do not plan to arrive anywhere with less than 20 percent left.
- **The stop rule.** Never ride on once the display shows a warning. Stop in a safe place and call the shop.

## Why the dashboard number moves

Many displays show the remaining distance as well as a percentage. That figure is an estimate based on your recent riding, so it falls fast after a burst of acceleration and recovers when you ride gently. It is also hard to compare between models. The percentage is a more stable guide, and the one to use with the method above.

A few other points about the gauge:

- **The needle can drop quickly at the bottom.** Batteries are often less predictable in the last 20 percent.
- **A rest can raise the reading slightly,** which does not mean new energy has appeared.
- **Battery age matters.** An older battery holds less than a new one, so the published range may be too optimistic.

## When a different vehicle is the better choice

If your preferred route needs more than about two-thirds of the usable range, or if there is no practical place to charge in the middle of the day, consider a different plan. You can shorten the route, split it over two days, or choose a petrol scooter for the long day and the electric one for the short days. ## FAQ: estimating range

### How far will a rental electric scooter go in Hanoi traffic?

It depends on the model, the rider and the route. A planning figure of about two-thirds of the claimed range, minus a reserve, is a cautious starting point. Ask the shop for the real figure for your bike.

### Is the remaining-distance number on the display reliable?

It is a guide, not a guarantee, because it changes with how you ride. Use the percentage for planning.

### Does a passenger change the range?

Yes. More weight needs more energy. Use the lower planning factor and check the model allows a passenger.

### Can I top up the battery during the day?

Possibly, if there is a socket and someone to supervise, but a full charge takes hours, and not all scooters can use public chargers. Plan to ride within one charge.

### What should I do if the battery is low?

Stop in a safe place and call the shop. Do not use a charger that did not come with the bike.
